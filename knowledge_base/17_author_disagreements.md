# 17. Author Disagreements

This file catalogs every place the five source books disagree, diverge in emphasis, or contradict each other — and every place a book contradicts *itself*. For each cross-book disagreement: what each author recommends, why, the supporting evidence offered, and the trade-offs — presented side by side, without adjudication. For each internal inconsistency: the exact location and nature of the conflict, preserved as found in the extraction (never silently resolved).

Cross-references: `10_position_sizing.md` and `11_risk_management.md` for the underlying Kelly/vol-target formulas; `16_research_hypotheses.md` for the cross-book convergences (the flip side of this file — where the books agree).

## 1. Kelly Criterion / Optimal f framing: Vince vs. Chan vs. Kaufman

All three books treat the same underlying mathematics (fraction-of-equity betting to maximize long-run geometric growth) but differ sharply in framing, confidence, and prescribed practical use.

### Vince (*The Mathematics of Money Management*, 1992) — the mathematical-foundation source
- Presents optimal f as the book's central subject: the fraction of capital that maximizes the long-run geometric growth rate (TWR), derivable empirically (brute-force search over historical trade P&L) or parametrically (fit a distribution, solve for the growth-maximizing fraction).
- Frames optimal f as producing **the fastest possible compounded growth, but also the most severe drawdowns** — presented as an inescapable, mathematically necessary trade-off, not a flaw to engineer away. Vince's own metaphor: "Optimal f is like plutonium. It gives you a tremendous amount of power, yet it is dreadfully dangerous" (Vince Ch.1).
- States explicitly that trading ABOVE optimal f is strictly worse in every respect than trading at or below it: the TWR/geometric-mean curve is single-peaked, so more risk beyond the peak is not "more reward for more risk" — it actively reduces long-run growth while increasing drawdown (Vince Ch.1-2). This is presented as pure mathematics, not a caution about behavior.
- Vince's own explicit practical accommodation is **fractional f** (trading some fraction of full optimal f) purely as a psychological/drawdown-tolerance compromise, not because full optimal f is mathematically wrong — "the crudest way" to set this is the investor's own subjective drawdown tolerance (Vince Ch.8).
- Does not address risk-of-ruin via classical closed-form gambling-theory equations in the main text — develops only its own "risk of ruin = 1 for unlimited-liability + infinite horizon" argument (an acknowledged gap, per `16_research_hypotheses.md` §6).

### Chan (*Quantitative Trading*, 2009) — Kelly as automatic discipline mechanism, with an explicit fat-tail correction
- Presents the full Kelly formula (F* = C⁻¹M, the matrix generalization for a multi-asset portfolio) and explicitly frames it as doing double duty: a mathematical growth-optimum AND a **psychological discipline mechanism** — it mechanically instructs reducing size after losses and increasing after gains, replacing an emotionally-driven sizing decision with a formula-driven one (Chan Ch.6).
- Unlike Vince, Chan gives a specific, numeric practical modification: **never exceed the SMALLER of half-Kelly and a fat-tail-implied leverage cap** derived from historical worst-case one-period loss vs. the trader's own maximum tolerable drawdown. This is Chan's own explicit correction for the Gaussian assumption he states is "known to be wrong" for real markets (Chan Ch.6).
- Chan's own long-term-growth formula, g = r + S²/2 at Kelly-optimal leverage, is used to argue Sharpe ratio (not raw return) is what should be maximized — directly informing his repeated correction of "buy-and-hold is optimal" and "bigger absolute returns matter more than Sharpe" misconceptions.
- Chan discloses two of his own past failures from deviating from Kelly-disciplined sizing (see `18_common_failure_modes.md` §Overleveraging) — used as first-person evidence for why formula-based discipline, not personal judgment, is necessary.

### Kaufman (*Trading Systems and Methods*, 6th ed., 2019) — the applied-caution/practical-guardrail layer
- Kaufman's own Ch.23 treatment is **explicitly framed by the source extraction as more risk-averse in its stated PRACTICAL recommendation than a naive reading of Vince's book might suggest**, even though Kaufman directly cites and builds on Vince's own *Portfolio Management Formulas* and *The Handbook of Portfolio Mathematics* (Kaufman Ch.23, Research_Ideas #104, #108).
- Kaufman modifies Vince's risk-of-ruin approach "for convenience" into a spreadsheet-friendly form (Table 23.15), but repeatedly frames optimal f as a **theoretical ceiling to stay well below**, not a sizing formula to trade at — citing Elder's "never trade full optimal f" and Markowitz's "use leverage-optimization only to reduce, not increase leverage" as the operative practical guidance (Kaufman Ch.23).
- No contradiction in the underlying mathematics was found between Kaufman and Vince by the source extraction — the difference is one of **emphasis and prescribed practical distance from the theoretical optimum**: Vince presents the mathematics of the optimum itself (with fractional-f as an explicit psychological compromise the reader must choose to make); Kaufman assumes the reader will apply a substantial safety margin as a matter of course and spends more of his own chapter on the guardrails than on the optimum's derivation.

### The three-way synthesis (not adjudicated, presented as the extraction found it)
- Vince: here is the mathematically optimal fraction, and here is the (severe) cost of deviating from it in either direction; the practical compromise (fractional f) is explicitly a psychological concession, not a mathematical one.
- Chan: use the Kelly formula as an automatic sizing discipline, but cap it at half-Kelly-or-tighter using a fat-tail-derived worst-case-loss adjustment, because the Gaussian assumption underlying the formula is known to be wrong.
- Kaufman: treat optimal f purely as an upper-bound sanity check, not a sizing target — the practical operating fraction should sit well below it as a matter of standard practice, per Elder and Markowitz's cautions.
- All three treat the underlying formula as valid; they diverge on how much of a safety margin to build in by default, and on whether that margin is framed as a fat-tail-driven mathematical correction (Chan) or a general practical/psychological guardrail (Kaufman, Vince's fractional-f).

## 2. Volatility-target levels: Kaufman's traditional-market benchmark vs. this project's crypto practice

- **Kaufman's stated practical volatility-target range**: 6% minimum, 8% typical, with 16%+ annualized volatility explicitly flagged as "dangerous" (Kaufman Ch.23).
- This range is drawn entirely from traditional futures/equities/FX markets and is dramatically lower than volatility targets commonly used in crypto position-sizing (this project's own TrendVolTarget strategy, documented in MEMORY.md, used a 40% vol-target).
- The source extraction explicitly flags this as **an open reconciliation item, not a resolved question**: either crypto's structurally higher baseline volatility genuinely justifies a proportionally much higher target (plausible, since crypto's baseline unconditional volatility is itself far above traditional markets), or the higher crypto-side figure should be re-examined against Kaufman's traditional-market benchmark as a sanity check rather than assumed appropriate by asset-class difference alone (Kaufman Research_Ideas #101, cross-referenced in `15_crypto_specific.md`).
- Kaufman's chapter does not itself address crypto at all — this is not a disagreement between authors, but a **flagged gap between a traditional-market author's stated practical range and current crypto practice**, worth stating clearly for any researcher calibrating a crypto vol-target level against this book's numbers.
- Chan's own leverage discussion (half-Kelly + fat-tail cap) offers a partially independent cross-check mechanism — since it derives a cap from worst-observed-loss rather than a fixed volatility percentage, it could in principle be applied to crypto data directly rather than requiring a traditional-market benchmark translation, though Chan never states this himself.

## 3. Pardo's internal Walk-Forward-window-ratio inconsistency (Ch.6 vs. Ch.11)

This is an inconsistency **within a single book**, not a cross-book disagreement, but it directly affects anyone implementing walk-forward analysis from this knowledge base.

- **Ch.6 ("The Historical Simulation")** states the walk-forward window should be sized as **1/8 to 1/3** of the optimization window.
- **Ch.11 ("Walk-Forward Analysis")** states the rule of thumb as walk-forward window ≈ **25-35%** of the optimization window size, "to be refined empirically."
- These do not perfectly align: 1/8 (12.5%) is well below the Ch.11 range's lower bound of 25%, while 1/3 (33.3%) sits inside the Ch.11 range. The two stated heuristics are **not reconciled anywhere in the source** — both are reported in the extraction exactly as given, without resolving which should govern (Pardo Ch.6, Ch.11; noted explicitly in Pardo's own Master_Summary.md "Warnings & Limitations").
- Practical implication flagged in the extraction: a researcher implementing Pardo's walk-forward methodology from this book alone would need to independently choose a ratio within (or bridging) these two stated ranges, since the source itself offers no adjudication.

## 4. Optimization philosophy: Pardo's WFA-centric approach vs. Kaufman's average-of-tests emphasis vs. Hilpisch's absent WFA

Three distinct postures toward the same underlying problem — how to avoid trusting an overfit backtest — appear across the books, differing in how formal/automated the safeguard is expected to be.

### Pardo — Walk-Forward Analysis as the near-complete answer
- WFA is presented as "the closest thing to a silver bullet the book offers" — described (with the author's own qualifying language) as "the only 99 percent foolproof method" / "an idiot-proof way of optimizing a trading strategy," while the same chapters explicitly acknowledge it is not literally infallible (a genuinely sound strategy can still be inadvertently overfit through bad procedure elsewhere) (Pardo Ch.11, Ch.13).
- Ch.13's fifth listed cause of overfitting is simply "Absence of a Walk-Forward Test" — Pardo explicitly anticipates the objection that this sounds like an oversimplified answer to a complex problem and rebuts it directly: "It is not oversimplification however. It is a statement that is the result of extensive research and use of this technique" (Pardo Ch.13).
- Walk-Forward Efficiency (WFE — the ratio of out-of-sample to in-sample annualized profit) is Pardo's proposed single primary forward-performance-forecasting metric, with a stated benchmark (WFE ~25% signals a problem; robust strategies typically show WFE >50-60%).
- **Note the author's own disclosed commercial interest**: Pardo's firm sells Walk-Forward Analysis/reporting add-in software (Walk-Forward Analyst, TradeProfiler) referenced throughout the book's Notes section — the extraction flags this explicitly as something readers should weigh alongside his WFA-superiority claims (Pardo, Master_Summary.md, Author Insight #12).

### Kaufman — robustness via parameter-neighborhood averaging, walk-forward as one tool among several
- Kaufman's chapter on testing methodology (Ch.21) treats walk-forward analysis as one part of a broader toolkit, alongside in/out-of-sample discipline (a stated 60/20/20 split, vs. Pardo's WFA-centric framing which doesn't anchor to a single fixed split ratio the same way), the kurtosis>6-8 overfitting alarm, and — Kaufman's single most-repeated conclusion in this chapter — that **the average performance across a parameter neighborhood, not the single best backtest, is the correct forward estimate**.
- Kaufman does discuss walk-forward's own specific failure mode (the "feedback" problem: re-running the full step-forward process more than once means the "out-of-sample" portion has actually been seen during development) as an explicit caution not framed with Pardo's near-silver-bullet confidence (Kaufman Ch.21).
- Where Pardo's WFE is a single ratio-based headline metric, Kaufman's emphasis spreads robustness evidence across multiple independent signals (parameter-neighborhood smoothness, kurtosis, cumulative-trial counting, cross-market consistency) rather than concentrating trust in one calculated efficiency ratio.

### Hilpisch — no walk-forward analysis anywhere in the book
- Every ML/backtesting example in *Python for Algorithmic Trading* uses a **single fixed in-sample/out-of-sample split**; walk-forward re-fitting or k-fold time-series cross-validation is never implemented anywhere, despite the book's own strong stated emphasis on avoiding overfitting (Hilpisch Ch.4-5, `16_research_hypotheses.md` §2).
- The book cites the formal academic literature on backtest-overfitting probability (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu 2015) as background reading but implements no such correction in code — pointing readers to the paper rather than showing a working method (Hilpisch, Research_Ideas #5).
- This stands in the sharpest contrast among the five books: Pardo treats WFA as near-essential and Kaufman treats parameter-neighborhood robustness testing as essential, while Hilpisch's otherwise rigorous, code-first pedagogical approach never operationalizes either safeguard — an explicit, self-acknowledged gap flagged in the extraction, not something Hilpisch defends or explains away.

### Chan's position (a fourth data point, not fully aligned with any of the above three)
- Chan's preferred safeguard is **continuous moving-window re-optimization** (Ch.3 sidebar, Ch.7 Example 7.1) — closer in spirit to Kaufman's neighborhood-robustness philosophy than to Pardo's single WFE-ratio metric, but computationally heavier, and Chan discloses he "rarely traded such models" himself for exactly that reason.
- Chan's other primary safeguard, the ~252×parameter-count sample-size rule plus a 5-parameter ceiling, is a simpler, cheaper heuristic than either Pardo's WFA or Kaufman's parameter-sweep-averaging — reflecting Chan's book's general "simplicity over sophistication" stance (see §6 below).

## 5. Trend-following confidence (Kaufman) vs. mean-reversion-leaning statistical arbitrage (Chan)

- **Kaufman treats trend-following as the dominant, most defensible paradigm** across the entire book — the single most repeated methodological confidence in *Trading Systems and Methods*. Stated rationale: it works because of fat tails (a few large outlier profits pay for many small losses, Ch.8); it "self-limits risk" since a trend system's own structure keeps losses small relative to trend-following winners (Ch.9); and it has survived across markets and decades where the book's own testing methodology can evaluate it. The corollary Kaufman states just as often: all trend methods, however different-looking (MA, point-and-figure, ARIMA, breakout), extract profit from the same underlying moves and correlate under stress (Ch.22, Ch.24) — trend-following's robustness as a paradigm does not imply diversification benefit across different trend implementations.
- **Chan's book centers on statistical arbitrage and mean-reversion/cointegration-based strategies** (pairs trading, cointegration testing, stat-arb via the O-U half-life) as the primary strategy paradigm explored in depth (Ch.7). Chan's own explicit heuristic for choosing between the two regimes (Ch.7): a price move driven by identifiable news/fundamentals suggests momentum ("don't stand in front of a freight train"); a move with no apparent news/fundamental cause suggests a liquidity event, more likely to mean-revert. This is presented as Chan's own opinion/heuristic, not an empirically validated law even within the book's own equity-focused testing.
- **The disagreement is not stated as a direct rebuttal between the two authors** (Chan's book, published 2009, predates or is contemporaneous with several of Kaufman's later editions and does not cite Kaufman) — it is a genuine difference in which paradigm each author's own career and testing experience led them to emphasize as the more reliable source of edge. Kaufman's stop-loss guidance is explicitly conditioned on trend/mean-reversion regime (see below), which is itself evidence the two paradigms are understood by Kaufman to require materially different risk management, not merely different entry logic.
- **Chan's own stated corollary on stop-losses directly mirrors this regime-dependence**: stop losses are beneficial in a momentum/trending regime, but actively harmful in a mean-reverting regime, where they force an unnecessary exit before the position would otherwise recoup its loss (Chan Ch.6). This is a point of *agreement* between the two books' underlying logic even though their emphasis differs — both treat trend-following and mean-reversion as requiring genuinely different risk-management postures, not just different entry signals.
- **Practical implication for a researcher using both books**: Kaufman's chapters (Ch.7-9, 17, on trend/momentum systems) should be read as the more battle-tested, cross-market-validated source for trend-following mechanics; Chan's Ch.7 should be read as the more rigorous source for the *statistical validation procedure* (cointegration testing, stationarity, half-life estimation) a mean-reversion strategy needs to clear before being trusted — the two books are complementary rather than contradictory on this axis, but a researcher should not expect either author to validate the other's preferred paradigm with comparable enthusiasm.

## 6. Complexity tolerance: simplicity-as-doctrine (Chan, Kaufman) vs. sophistication-with-caution (Pardo, Vince) vs. capability-agnostic (Hilpisch)

- **Chan's single most repeated theme across the entire book is "simplicity beats sophistication."** Chan states his own PhD-physics-trained, institutionally-resourced efforts produced years of losses, and only adopting "the simplest but still quantitative strategies" made him profitable — explicitly invoking Einstein's "as simple as possible, but not simpler" and applying it to strategy design generally, not just AI-based methods (Chan, Master_Summary.md, Author Insight #1).
- **Kaufman independently arrives at the same doctrine**: "the fewer inputs, the better" (Ch.6); Occam's razor invoked from Ch.1; every added rule/parameter must "prove the feature helps" (Ch.8) or be removed; complex/AI methods (Ch.20) are surveyed with respect but closed with explicit realistic-expectations warnings, including an explicit non-recommendation of neural networks/deep learning for most traders ("no one is sure there is a solution to the problem as we are specifying it" for markets) (Kaufman, Master_Summary.md, Author Insight #6; Ch.20).
- **Pardo does not share the same blanket simplicity doctrine** — his position is closer to "complexity is dangerous specifically because it hides overfitting," not that complexity is inherently wrong: "complexity is the ultimate hiding place of overfitting" (Ch.5) is a caution about verifiability and degrees-of-freedom cost, not a rejection of sophisticated methods on principle. Pardo explicitly discusses genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, and simulated annealing as *underused* advanced search methods relative to what he calls the "quite primitive" state of mainstream (2007-era) commercial optimization tooling — a stance that reads as more sympathetic to sophistication, provided it is properly validated via WFA, than either Chan's or Kaufman's default skepticism (Pardo Ch.9, Research_Ideas #5).
- **Vince's position is adjacent to Pardo's but framed differently**: Vince explicitly warns against restricting a system's "degrees of freedom" in the name of maximizing historical fit, calling extensive optimization/curve-fitting "the ultimate 'woulda, shoulda, coulda' game" — but his stated alternative is not "keep the system simple" so much as "build the simplest possible system that shows even marginal profitability across many markets, and focus optimization effort on MONEY MANAGEMENT rather than the trading rules themselves," since "the degree of profitability is determined by the money management you apply to the system more than by the system itself" (Vince Ch.1). This redirects complexity-tolerance toward the sizing layer rather than rejecting complexity in the entry/exit rules per se.
- **Hilpisch's book is complexity-agnostic in framing** — it presents linear/logistic regression, scikit-learn classifiers, and Keras/TensorFlow deep neural networks side by side as a progression of technique, without staking out an explicit position on whether added model complexity is dangerous. The book's own closing caveat on the DNN chapter ("this chapter does not claim to have found the holy grail") is a statement about unproven efficacy, not a Chan/Kaufman-style a priori doctrine against complexity (Hilpisch Ch.5, Master_Summary.md).
- **Net picture**: Chan and Kaufman converge on simplicity as a first-order design principle from independent career experience; Pardo and Vince treat complexity as acceptable provided it survives a sufficiently rigorous validation gate (WFA for Pardo, degrees-of-freedom/money-management discipline for Vince); Hilpisch's pedagogical scope simply does not take a position either way.

## 7. Discretion vs. automation

- **Chan and Pardo both explicitly respect discretionary trading's ceiling while building systematic frameworks that assume automation is generally preferable for the independent trader.**
  - Chan states genuine respect for elite discretionary traders (Buffett, Soros, Tudor Jones, Kovner, Pickens named) and acknowledges they achieve results "no systematic strategy (in his assessment, as of writing) has yet matched" — while still stating that manual interference with automated decisions generally makes performance *worse*, not better, for the ordinary quantitative trader his book targets (Chan Ch.1).
  - Pardo similarly frames the entire testing/optimization/WFA pipeline as existing to replace hope with knowledge, and states discretionary trading is psychologically HARDER in some respects than systematic trading — the discretionary trader must be "master of himself" continuously with no external rule structure to lean on (Pardo Ch.2, Psychology.md). Pardo's own explicit open question (never resolved in the book): whether a systematic strategy could ever truly match/exceed the best discretionary traders — argued to be "a matter of degree, not kind" via a chess-computer analogy, but explicitly stated as "not been done yet" to his knowledge.
- **Kaufman's book scopes trader psychology and execution skill as explicitly out of scope** ("Execution skill and market psychology are not considered — only the strategies, the methods for testing those strategies, and the means for controlling the risk," Ch.1) — a notably different posture from Chan's and Pardo's books, both of which devote substantial space to the discretion/discipline question. Kaufman's book is the most purely mechanical-systems-focused of the three on this axis, treating the discretion-vs-automation question as settled in favor of systems and moving on, rather than examining it directly.
- **Hilpisch's book takes the strongest implicit pro-automation stance of all five, without ever arguing for it explicitly.** The entire thrust of the book, especially Ch.10 (moving from backtested strategy to fully automated live deployment with logging/monitoring rather than manual oversight), reflects a belief that automation reduces the risk of ad hoc, emotionally-driven intervention — but the extraction notes this is *never stated as an explicit psychological argument*; it is framed purely in terms of infrastructure/reliability and consistency of execution (Hilpisch, Psychology.md). This is a case of practical convergence with Chan/Pardo's pro-automation conclusion, reached via an entirely different (engineering-first, not psychology-first) route.
- **Vince occupies a distinct position**: his book explicitly disclaims any ability to improve a reader's trading skill or judgment at all — "there is nothing in this text that will make you a better trader, nothing that will improve your timing of entry and exit from a given market, nothing that will improve your trade selection" (Vince, Introduction) — treating psychological/discretionary trading competence as an entirely separate, prerequisite skill the book's money-management mathematics assumes but cannot supply. Vince's music-theory analogy ("understanding money management is like understanding music theory — necessary and valuable, but NOT sufficient to make you a musician") is the clearest single statement among the five books that quantitative mastery and discretionary trading skill are separate, non-substitutable competencies — a framing none of the other four books states quite so starkly.

## 8. Documented internal inconsistencies WITHIN individual books

### Kaufman — six internal inconsistencies flagged in the source's own Master_Summary.md
Preserved verbatim from the extraction, never silently corrected:
1. **Ch.14**: the Gann Hexagon Chart's calendar mapping calls both March 20 and September 23 "solstice" dates — conflicts with standard astronomy (both are equinox dates, not solstices).
2. **Ch.17**: the Dynamic Momentum Index's prose description of its volatility relationship appears to contradict the direction implied by its own DMIt = INT(P/Vt) formula.
3. **Ch.18**: the Kurtosis-Skew Strategy's mean-reversion "Sell" rule, as printed in the source, appears to duplicate the trend-following sell condition rather than mirror the buy condition — flagged directly in `16_research_hypotheses.md` §5 as a reason any implementation should use the corrected/mirrored rule, not the verbatim-as-printed one.
4. **Ch.22**: Table 22.1's Iraq/Kuwait row shows an "Up" shock direction paired with a loss-formatted figure, and the table's 7 rows don't match the 6 narratively-numbered shocks discussed in the surrounding text; Table 22.6 has an apparent duplicate-pairing inconsistency.
5. **Ch.23**: "e = 2.7128" is printed for the natural-log base (the correct value is 2.71828) — a likely typesetting error.
6. **Ch.24**: two chapter-citation errors within the same chapter — risk measures are attributed to "Chapter 21" in one place and "Chapter 23" in another for what appears to be the same material, and a claim that "the efficient frontier was introduced in Chapter 22" (it was actually introduced in Ch.23).

### Pardo — the walk-forward window-ratio inconsistency
Documented in full in §3 above (Ch.6's "1/8 to 1/3" vs. Ch.11's "25-35%").

### Vince — minor internal numerical inconsistencies (preserved, not corrected, per extraction policy)
- The Chapter 5 "single underlying instrument" worked example lists probabilities summing to 1.30 rather than 1.00 — an apparent error in the source's own table.
- The Chapter 8 margin-constraint worked example shows a $2,000 vs. $4,800/$4,600 f$ discrepancy in one intermediate table.
- The Bernoulli-distribution variance formula in Appendix B is printed as `Q=P-1` (conventionally `Q=1-P`).
- Appendix C's phase-length expected-count formula (C.03) has an ambiguous exponent term in the source ("D^2*3*D+1"), most likely intended as `D^2+3*D+1`, but not resolvable from the extraction alone.

### Hilpisch — an unreconciled design inconsistency (not a typo, a substantive gap)
- The Ch.10 ML strategy's backtested bar length (10 minutes) does not match its live-deployed bar length (5 seconds / 2 seconds) — an inconsistency the author never addresses anywhere in the book (Hilpisch, Master_Summary.md, "Open Questions / Ambiguities"). Distinct from a typo-level error, this is a substantive, unflagged mismatch between the validated model and the deployed system.
- Separately: the vectorized SMA strategy is always fully invested (long or short, no neutral state), while the event-based long-only version of "the same" strategy can be neutral — materially different strategies despite sharing the same crossover logic, not reconciled by the author anywhere in the text.
