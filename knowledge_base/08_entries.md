## 1. What an "Entry Rule" Is (Pardo's Design-Level Framing)

Pardo treats entry/exit as one of exactly three components that define any trading strategy — the other two being risk management and position sizing (Pardo Ch.5). This framing is the organizing scaffold for everything else in this file: an entry technique in isolation (a breakout rule, an MA cross, a pattern) is, in Pardo's own terms, incomplete until paired with a stop and a sizing rule — a point Kaufman makes independently and more forcefully (see §9 below).

- **Entry rule (definition)**: "initiates a new long or short position." Can only fire when the strategy is flat (Pardo Ch.5).
- **Exit rule (definition)**: "closes out a current long or short position." Only fires from an open position (Pardo Ch.5).
- **Reversal rule (definition)**: closes an existing position and opens a new, opposite position in a single action (Pardo Ch.5).
- **Symmetrical vs. asymmetrical strategies**: a symmetrical strategy's buy and sell conditions mirror each other (e.g., breakout above a 3-day high to buy / below a 3-day low to sell); an asymmetrical strategy uses unrelated logic per side (e.g., a 5-day-high breakout to buy but a 5/20-day MA cross to sell) (Pardo Ch.5). This same distinction recurs constantly in Kaufman's Strategies.md — most Kaufman systems (N-day breakout, Donchian channels, two-trendline crossovers) are symmetrical/mirror-image by construction, and Kaufman explicitly calls out the exceptions.
- **Reversing vs. nonreversing**: a reversing strategy is always in the market, flipping position on every opposite signal; a nonreversing strategy exits to flat and waits for a fresh entry signal (Pardo Ch.5). Kaufman's basic trendline systems, N-day breakout, and moving-average crossovers are reversing/always-in-market by default (Kaufman Ch.5, Ch.8); Kaufman's Rule Set 2/3 two-trendline variants deliberately introduce a flat "neutral zone" instead (Kaufman Ch.8 — see §3 below).
- **Illustrative entry examples given by Pardo** (buy side; sell side is the mirror unless the system is asymmetrical): 5/20-day MA cross; RSI closes below 20; daily *and* weekly close both up ≥1%; close > yesterday's close + 50% of the daily range; close > each of the previous 3 closes (Pardo Ch.5).

### Entry/Exit Filters (Pardo's Framing)
- **Definition**: "additional information [added] to produce a more accurate and reliable trading strategy entry or exit" — extra confirming conditions layered onto a primary entry signal (Pardo Ch.5).
- Can be single-simple, single-complex, or a compound/multiple-filter combination (Pardo Ch.5).
- **Example filter list (buy side)**: RSI < 30; RSI was < 30 last bar and is now > 30 and rising; today's high > yesterday's high; the last signal was profitable; close > (close 20 days ago + 10 points); yesterday's close fell in the top third of yesterday's range (Pardo Ch.5).
- **Explicit overfitting warning**: filter count and complexity trade off directly against overfitting risk — "as the number and complexity of the trading filters increases, so can the difficulty of coding and testing... the likelihood of overfitting also rises with the number of filters," to the absurd limit of a unique filter per historical bar (a perfect historical fit with zero forward-looking value) (Pardo Ch.5). This is the same warning Kaufman gives independently in Ch.1's Trading System Development Guidelines ("filtering... excessive filtering causes either overfitting or a system that generates no trades at all" — Kaufman Ch.1, per Concepts.md), and it is the throughline connecting this file's §6 (confirmation/filters) to overfitting material that properly belongs in files 10-18.

## 2. Breakout Entries

Breakout entry logic is the single most repeated entry family across the Kaufman knowledge base, spanning Ch.3 (chart-pattern breakouts), Ch.4 (charting-systems channel breakouts), Ch.5 (event-driven N-day/point-and-figure breakouts), and Ch.8 (trend-system band/channel breakouts). Chan's and Hilpisch's code-level strategies do not use pure price-breakout entries (their vectorized examples are cross/threshold-based — see §7-8), so breakout entry technique in this knowledge base is essentially entirely Kaufman's.

### N-Day / N-Bar Breakout (the generic family)
- **Basic rule (always-in-market)**: buy when today's high crosses above the high of the past N days; sell when today's low crosses below the low of the past N days (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Conservative (close-based) variant**: buy when today's *close* is above the high of the past N days; sell when today's close is below the low of the past N days — a later, more confirmed signal than the intraday-high/low trigger (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Risk**: entry-to-exit risk = entry price minus the reversal-trigger price, which grows with N (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Author's own 2000-2017 parameter-sweep test** (10-120 day sweep, 10-day steps, 23 tests/market across futures and stocks): average best N ≈ 93 days; 7 of 13 markets profitable across *all* tested N; 79% average profitable-test rate (vs. 63% for point-and-figure on the same markets); net profit >15× point-and-figure's over the same period (Kaufman Ch.5). Kaufman explicitly uses this cross-parameter consistency (fraction of profitable backtests across a parameter sweep) as a *robustness* signal distinct from peak-optimized performance (Kaufman Ch.5, Concepts.md) — directly relevant to this project's own walk-forward/parameter-neighborhood-averaging discipline (see MEMORY.md).
- **Intraday extension — the N-bar breakout**: the same logic applied to bar counts instead of calendar days (new high/low of the previous N *bars*, not N days); Kaufman flags a discovered risk specific to the intraday version — repeated intraday reversals (new high, then new low, then new high again) can produce unusually large losses; if a trade is held overnight, the N-bar count continues across the session boundary (Kaufman Ch.16).

### Donchian-Family Breakouts
- **Donchian's 4-Week Rule**: go long (cover shorts) when price exceeds the highs of the previous 4 calendar weeks; sell short (liquidate longs) when price falls below the lows of the previous 4 calendar weeks (Kaufman Ch.5). Rated best of the era's popular systems in a 1970 16-year study (Dunn and Hargitt), per Kaufman.
- **Donchian's 40/20 Channel Breakout**: the earliest recorded N-day breakout using *different* periods for entry (40, longer) and exit (20, shorter) — the direct conceptual predecessor of the Turtles' method (Kaufman Ch.5, Ch.8).
- **Donchian's 20-and-40-Day Breakout**: entry = buy when today's high > the highest high of the past 40 days, sell short when today's low < the lowest low of the past 40 days; exit = the mirror 20-day channel (Kaufman Ch.8) — explicitly identified as "the basis for the Turtles' trading method."
- **Donchian's 5- and 20-Day Moving Average System**: combines a 5-day MA (exit trigger, further modified by prior penetration and volatility) with a 20-day MA (primary trend) plus a volatility-penetration criterion — originally the largest prior 1-day penetration of the 20-day MA, modernized in the source's own reference implementation via ATR over the 20-day period (Kaufman Ch.8). Exact inequality thresholds in the modernized rule set are an embedded-graphic gap; the qualitative structure (buy if not-long AND price+volatility conditions met; exit-long if price falls back through the 5-day-MA condition) is preserved.

### The Turtles (S1 and S2) — the canonical breakout-entry system
- **S1**: enter long on a 20-day high breakout, exit long on a 10-day low breakout (mirror for short). **Filter rule**: skip an S1 entry signal if the prior S1 trade (taken or not) was profitable, UNLESS that prior trade lost at least a specified multiple of L (a 20-day ATR-based volatility measure converted to dollars via big-point value) (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **S2**: enter long on a 55-day high breakout (some discretion to shorten slightly is allowed), exit long on a 20-day low breakout (mirror for short). No filter rule (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Compounding on the entry side**: add a unit (or ½ unit) per additional L of favorable movement from entry, up to 5 units (Kaufman Ch.5) — a scale-in entry variant layered on top of the initial breakout entry.
- **Author's own copper backtest (1980-2017/18)**: the slower S2-style system showed steady profits across the full 38 years; the faster S1-style system profited mainly in the early 1980s (the period that motivated the Turtles' founding) — explicitly flagged by Kaufman as a single-market, incomplete-rule-set test, not generalizable (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Ambiguity**: the exact S1 loss-threshold multiple and the 4-tier position caps (single market / correlated group / less-correlated group / net portfolio) are not numerically recoverable from this source (Kaufman Ch.5, Master_Summary.md Open Question #3).

### Regression-Based (Programmed) Channel Breakout
- **Construction**: run a linear regression of closing price (Y) against a sequential bar index (X, not calendar date) over a chosen window; compute the residual for every bar; band = regression line ± the max/min historical residual; project both bands one bar ahead using the regression slope (Kaufman Ch.4, Indicators.md).
- **Entry**: in an uptrend, a close (or low, for an earlier signal) below the projected lower band signals a trend reversal down; in a downtrend, a close (or high) above the projected upper band signals a reversal up. Near-zero slope reads as a sideways channel, with the same breakout rule applying (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Countertrend scale-in variant**: in an established trend, add positions when price approaches (without breaking) the *opposite* channel boundary — e.g., within 15% of the upper band as a percentage of channel width, while in a downtrend — a way to scale into multiple entries without needing to time the exact extreme (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Caution (Kaufman's own)**: all trends pass through a sideways phase when reversing, so a genuinely sideways market should also show a small rate of change, not merely a near-zero slope, before this pattern is trusted (Kaufman Ch.4, Concepts.md — cross-ref `07_market_regimes.md`).

### Point-and-Figure Breakout Entries
- **Basic entry**: buy when an X in the current column is 1 box above the highest X of the last X-column; sell when an O is 1 box below the lowest O of the last O-column — always in the market unless long-only (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Best formations** (Davis, 1965 study, 1,100 stocks, 1954-1964, cited by Kaufman): best buy = ascending triple top; best sell = breakout of a triple bottom (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Three risk-limited entry alternatives** given by Kaufman: (1) wait for a pullback to an acceptable risk level and enter with the standard P&F stop — explicitly do NOT use an arbitrary fixed-boxes-back stop, which "has no logical basis"; (2) enter on the *second* reversal in the trend direction via a trailing order 4 boxes above/below the reversal's extreme (reduces risk, can avoid whipsaws, but may miss trades with no pullback); (3) require a 2-4 box confirming breakout (raised from the standard 1) as volatility increases, to filter false starts (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **Author's own tested refinement — reversal-priority rule**: on an outside day where both a trend-continuation box-fill and a 3-box reversal occur, record the reversal first (rather than the traditional continuation-first convention) — tested by Kaufman to produce earlier, more favorable stop-loss/trend-change timing, except when the reversal value is small (Kaufman Ch.5).
- **The Box-Size Dilemma** (a structural weakness of *every* fixed-increment breakout method, including Renko): a fixed box size that works at one price/volatility regime performs poorly at another (too coarse at low prices, too fine at high prices, or vice versa) — Kaufman explicitly calls this "a problem yet to be solved," surveying Chartcraft's fixed-grid rescaling, a variable box size, and ATR-based dynamic sizing as imperfect partial fixes, without endorsing any as complete (Kaufman Ch.5, Concepts.md).

### Volatility-Band and Trend-Band Breakout Entries (Kaufman Ch.8)
These form a large family: a trendline (MA, exponential smoothing, or regression) plus a band whose width is set by a volatility measure, with entry defined as price crossing the band.
- **Volatility System** (cited to Richard Bookstaber, 1984): sell if close drops by more than k×ATR(n) from the previous close; buy if close rises by more than k×ATR(n) from the previous close; k ≈ 2.0 typically. Kaufman notes this produces few trades but high reliability (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **10-Day Moving Average Rule** (Keltner, 1960): 10-day MA of (H+L+C)/3 with a band = 10-day MA of the high-low range (≈10-day ATR); buy when price crosses above the upper band, sell when it crosses below the lower band; always-reversing (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Bollinger Bands — reversal-variant entry**: buy (close shorts, go long) when price closes above the upper band (20-day MA ± 2 stdev of price changes over the same 20 days); sell short (close longs, go short) when price closes below the lower band. Max risk per trade = current band width (Kaufman Ch.8). Kaufman's own statistical caveat: because prices are not normally distributed, "2 stdev" equates to only ~87% empirical confidence, not the ~95.4% a true normal distribution would imply.
- **Bollinger "Squeeze" variant**: wait for band compression to some percentage of average width (e.g., 50%), then trade the breakout through the bands; trading with the prevailing trend improves results, per cited historical success as a filter (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **MPTDI (Major Price Trend Directional Indicator, R.J. Taylor, 1972)**: a fully volatility-adaptive breakout entry where the calculation period, weighting, entry-penetration threshold, *and* stop-loss all shift together based on a discrete 5-step average-trading-range classification (Types A-E) — entry = penetration of the moving average by the step's assigned point threshold, triggered intraday off values calculated after the prior close (Kaufman Ch.8). Weakness flagged by Kaufman: the discrete step boundaries are "a crude measure" — accurate mid-step, abruptly wrong at the transition points between steps.
- **Adaptive Price Zone (APZ)** (Leibfarth, 2006): unlike the trend-following bands above, this is a *mean-reversion* band — bands touching/expanding to meet the highs and lows as volatility increases is read as a mean-reversion (not breakout) trade opportunity (Kaufman Ch.8). Included here to make the trend-vs-reversion contrast explicit within the same "band" indicator family.

### Volatility-Breakout Day-Trading Entries
See §5 (Day-Trading Entries) for the intraday-specific volatility-breakout family (opening-range breakout, Crabel, Fisher, Williams, TSM Flexible Intraday Breakout) — these are structurally the same "band around a reference price" logic as the daily-bar volatility bands above, applied to the session open or previous close (Kaufman Ch.16).

## 3. Moving-Average Cross Entries

### Basic Moving Average Trendline System (Kaufman Ch.8) — the template for all MA-cross entries
Three entry-rule variants, in increasing lag / decreasing signal-frequency order (Kaufman Ch.8):
1. Intraday price crosses the trendline.
2. Closing price crosses the trendline.
3. The trendline itself changes direction (buy when the trendline turns up, sell short when it turns down).
- **No required conditions beyond the crossing/direction-change event**; the basic form is always in the market (reverses on the opposite signal).
- **Optimization finding (Table 8.2, 10-year Amazon)**: trendline-direction signals (variant 3) produce 26-37% fewer trades and generally better performance than price-penetration signals, *except* at the fastest (5-day) calculation period (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Calculation-period choice is ranked the single most important design decision** — more important than the trend-identification method itself (Kaufman Ch.8, restated as Author Insight #1/#4 in Master_Summary.md).
- **No stop-loss or profit-taking in the basic form** — Kaufman's explicit warning: adding stops/profit-taking to a pure trend-cross system reduces or eliminates its ability to capture the fat tail necessary for long-term profitability (Kaufman Ch.8; cross-ref `09_exits.md`).
- **Worked backtest (80-day trendline-direction system, NASDAQ 100 futures, 1998-June 2018)**: 201 trades, 36.32% win rate, profit factor 1.47, net profit $1,584,665 (longs $1,868,520, shorts −$283,855); average winning trade held 47.3 bars vs. average losing trade 10.09 bars (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Important warning on entry-timing precision**: entering exactly at a signal is a point of high uncertainty — noisy markets often produce an immediate small loss right at entry; delaying execution to the next open improved entry price ~75% of the time in cited multi-year tests but *reduced total profits*, because missed fast breakouts outweighed the price improvements (Kaufman Ch.8).

### Two-Trendline Crossover Systems (generic family, Kaufman Ch.8)
Three rule sets combine a slow trendline (primary trend direction) with a fast trendline (entry/exit timing):
- **Rule Set 1 (always in market)**: buy when the faster MA crosses above the slower MA; sell short when the faster crosses below the slower.
- **Rule Set 2 (creates a flat/neutral zone, price-triggered)**: buy when price crosses above *both* MAs, exit when price crosses below *either*; sell short when price crosses below both, cover when price crosses above either.
- **Rule Set 3 (creates a flat/neutral zone, trend-confirmation-triggered)**: buy when the faster trendline turns up AND the slower trendline is up; sell short when the faster turns down AND the slower is down; exit when the two trendlines conflict.
- **Filter refinement**: a small band around each trendline requiring clear penetration before flipping reduces whipsaw from marginal crossovers (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Worked comparison (euro futures 1990-2018, 100-day/30-day crossover [Rule Set 1] vs. single 120-day MA)**: crossover total P&L $3,441,223, profit factor 2.0; single MA total P&L $2,658,518, profit factor 1.62; the crossover also produced fewer trades (Kaufman Ch.8). Periods were chosen with hindsight in this example, per the source.

### The Golden Cross / Death Cross
- **Definition**: Golden Cross = 50-day MA crosses above the 200-day MA (bullish); Death Cross = 50-day MA crosses below the 200-day MA (bearish) (Kaufman Ch.8). Kaufman notes the origin of these specific periods is unclear, but doubling periods (50/100/200) is a simple way to keep percentage changes roughly constant across an entry family and to get a reasonable result distribution over time.
- **Track record cited**: "very good results for the past 60 years," including avoiding the 2008 decline (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Variant comparison**: combined Golden+Death Cross (long/short, reversing) vs. Golden-Cross-only (long-only) — the combined version's advantage concentrates mainly in the 2000 NASDAQ collapse and following bear market; the combined version's largest drawdown occurred in 2014, not 2008; the long-only version would also earn interest income while flat (Kaufman Ch.8).

### Modified 3-Crossover Model (3-trend confirmation filter)
- **Purpose**: add a third, fast confirming trendline to a 2-trend crossover so a trade is not entered at a moment when price is actually moving opposite to the about-to-be-taken position — a risk of using MA *direction* rather than price for signals (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Filter rule**: do not enter a trade unless the confirming (fast) MA is moving in the same direction as the position about to be entered (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Worked test (3-day timing trend added to the Eurodollar 80-20 crossover)**: small improvement in long profits, a decline in short profits, slightly better overall — more beneficial trading from the long side (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **4-9-18 Crossover Model** (popular late 1970s): a 3-trend crossover using periods slightly faster than the 5/10/20-day periods popular at the time, each roughly double the faster one to keep percentage spacing constant. Kaufman's own out-of-sample test found only marginal profits on a modern futures sample — "none of the results would have convinced you to trade this" — with the explicit caveat it likely *would* have worked in the earlier, lower-noise Donchian-era markets (Kaufman Ch.8).

### "Ahead of the Crowd" 8-18 Day Crossover
- **Purpose**: deliberately use calculation periods slightly faster than the most popular/crowded periods (e.g., 8/18 days vs. the popular 10/20) to gain a small execution edge from anticipating order flow ahead of a known crowd of similar-period traders — cited by Kaufman as a real system used during the 1980s-1990s (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Important warning**: Kaufman notes these specific calculation periods "may not be profitable in today's markets" — the transferable value is the general principle (being slightly ahead of a known crowd), not the specific 8/18 parameters (Kaufman Ch.8).

### Ichimoku Cloud (a hybrid MA-cross + trend-filter entry system)
- **Macrotrend entry**: buy when the cloud (formed by two forward-projected MA-derived lines, Senkou A and B) turns from red to green, or when price moves above a green cloud; sell short when the cloud turns green-to-red or price moves below a red cloud (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Shorter-term momentum/reentry (only in the cloud's current direction)**: buy when the Tenkan-sen (Conversion Line, 9-day) crosses above the Kijun-sen (Base Line, 26-day); sell short on the mirror cross (Kaufman Ch.8). The cloud itself thus functions as both a trend filter and a component of the crossover-entry logic — directly relevant to §6's multi-timeframe/filter discussion.

### Concrete Coded MA-Cross Entry (Hilpisch)
Hilpisch's SMA Crossover strategy is the book's simplest and most directly portable MA-cross implementation (Hilpisch Ch.4, Strategies.md):
- **Entry logic (as coded)**: `IF SMA1 > SMA2 THEN` go/stay long (position = +1); `IF SMA1 < SMA2 THEN` go/stay short (position = −1) in the vectorized/long-short version, or go neutral in the long-only event-based version. SMAs computed via `price.rolling(window).mean()`.
- **Always-invested vs. neutral-capable**: the vectorized version is always fully invested one way or the other with no explicit exit distinct from the opposite entry; the event-based long-only version can instead go flat — Hilpisch does not reconcile these as producing genuinely different strategies despite sharing "the same" crossover logic (Hilpisch Ch.4, Ch.6).
- **Optimization example**: brute-force grid search over `(SMA1 ∈ [30,50], SMA2 ∈ [200,300])` on EUR/USD 2010-2019 found an optimum of SMA1=48, SMA2=238 (gross performance 1.5×) vs. 1.29× for the default (42, 252) — flagged in this project's own Strategies.md extraction as a textbook overfitting risk since no out-of-sample validation of the optimized parameters is shown (Hilpisch Ch.4, Ch.6).
- **Cost/turnover profile**: because MA-cross entries fire relatively infrequently versus momentum-style entries, the SMA strategy was the *most* cost-resilient of Hilpisch's three core strategies in the Ch.6 event-based comparison — net performance on AAPL.O 2010-2019 fell only from +462.05% (no costs) to +419.60% ($10 fixed + 1% proportional costs), actually outperforming the momentum strategy after costs despite underperforming it before costs (Hilpisch Ch.6). Cross-ref §9 below and `09_exits.md`/transaction-cost files for the full cost-sensitivity comparison across Hilpisch's three strategy families.

## 4. Pullback / Retracement Entries (Dunnigan Thrust, Congestion-Phase, Retracement Theory)

This is Kaufman Ch.4's core content, with Ch.3's Action-and-Reaction/retracement-theory material as conceptual scaffolding.

### Dunnigan's Thrust Method (William Dunnigan, 1954)
- **Swing definitions**: a downswing = the current day's high AND low both lower than the corresponding high/low of the prior upswing's highest day (mirror for an upswing); the swing top/bottom is the extreme of that swing (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Five key buy patterns** (sell = mirror image): (1) test of the bottom — price within a set percentage of a prior low; (2) closing-price reversal — a new swing low with a higher close than the prior day; (3) narrow range — less than half the swing's largest range; (4) inside range/inside day; (5) penetration of the top by any amount (a breakout) (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Entry**: a preliminary buy signal requires 2 of the first 4 patterns, confirmed the next day by a "thrust" (a price gain scaled to the market's price level). IF instead price simply penetrates the top (pattern 5), THEN that alone is a valid preliminary signal, and thrust confirmation may occur on any subsequent day. Fallback: standard Dow Theory penetration rules (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Repeat signals** (Dunnigan and followers considered these the strongest part of the method, though they limit profit size): (1) test of the bottom + inside day; (2) closing-price reversal + inside day (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Double thrust**: a second thrust immediately after the first, or after a brief congestion pause, in the same direction (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **No explicit exit/stop rules given** beyond reversing on an opposite entry signal; Kaufman notes Dunnigan separately did significant work on portfolio-level risk-reward management not detailed in this source (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Modernization note (Kaufman's own)**: compare today's move to average true range instead of Dunnigan's original fixed-point thrust threshold (Kaufman Ch.4) — the same percentage-of-volatility-over-fixed-point preference restated throughout the book (Master_Summary.md Author Insight #7).

### Dunnigan's One-Way Formula
- A generalization of the Thrust Method — Dunnigan himself judged the original "too sensitive." The thrust becomes the *preliminary* signal, redefined to require the entire bar's range be an outside day (a stronger condition) (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Confirmation**: an additional up-thrust after formation/test of a double bottom or ascending bottom (may validate on a later test if not confirmed on the first) (Kaufman Ch.4).
- Programmable versions cited: TSM Dunnigan Trend (uptrend = 2 consecutive higher-high/higher-low days, confirmed by breaking the downtrend's high) and TSM Dunnigan One-Way Formula (double bottom + short-term bounce), both credited to Ruggiero's interpretation (Kaufman Ch.4, Companion Website — not independently verifiable from this extraction).

### Dunnigan's Square Root Theory
- Claims price moves in square-root-proportional steps (a market at √X moves to (√X±1)², one "unit"); used by Dunnigan for setting profit objectives/risk under his "trap forecasting" trading style (quick, calculated profit-taking rather than riding the full trend). Conceptually related to (and expanded by) Gann's methods covered in Kaufman Ch.14 (Kaufman Ch.4).

### Nofri's Congestion-Phase System (Eugene Nofri, via Jeanette Nofri Steinberg) — a pure congestion/pullback mean-reversion entry
- **Applicability**: only within a defined congestion/sideways range (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Core entry rule**: IF price is within the congestion range AND has closed in the same direction for 2 consecutive days, THEN take the *opposite* position at the close of day 2; take profit at the close of day 3 (a 3-day reversal system) (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Congestion range definition**: top = a high immediately followed by 2 consecutive lower closes; bottom = a low immediately followed by 2 consecutive higher closes. A new high/low outside the range cancels the congestion area. Two near-unchanged consecutive closes count as one day. The range is not established until both a top AND a bottom exist (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Timing filters**: IF the top/bottom formed right after a major breakout/run, THEN wait 10 additional days before trusting the range; a false breakout lasting 2-3 days triggers a 7-day waiting period (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Stops**: place at the top/bottom of the current congestion range, or use tighter volatility-based stops (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Kaufman's own operationalization of ambiguous original rules**: "large move" = 10-day net change ≥ 2× historical average 10-day net change; "false breakout" = a move outside the range reverting within 2 days; no stop-loss used (1-day hold); a losing 2-day-down long is closed and immediately reopened if the "2-day down" condition persists (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Caveat**: backtested well historically but showed large losses in some markets during the 2008 financial crisis — Kaufman suggests a high-volatility filter may be needed (Kaufman Ch.4).

### Retracement Theory (the conceptual backbone of pullback entries)
- **Action and Reaction**: frames price movement as a series of behavioral overreactions followed by corrective adjustments as the market seeks equilibrium — each significant "action" tends to be followed by a partial "reaction" (Kaufman Ch.4). Historical lineage: Frank Tubbs' 1930s "Law of Proportion"; the 1975 Trident System combined pattern recognition with magnitude projection.
- **Survey of retracement levels used by different theorists**: 100% ("unity," full retracement — the single most important support level per most analysts); 50% (next most accepted, "strongly supported by Gann"); Schabacker (⅓ or ½, anything larger implies a full trend reversal); Angas (25% for intermediate trends); Dunnigan and Tubbs (½, ⅔, or ¾); Gann (inverse powers of 2: ½, ¼, ⅛...); Elliott (the Fibonacci ratio 0.618 and its complement 0.382) (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Kaufman's own statistical caveat**: with so many candidate retracement percentages in circulation, price is likely to "stop" near one of them by pure chance alone; absent other information, the largest levels are argued most reliable (100% > 50% > 33%/25%, with 12.5% "too small to consider seriously"); Fibonacci's 0.618/1.618 are treated as an exception with claimed genuine mass-behavioral significance. Combined ranking of most important retracements given: 100%, 50%, 61.8% (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Liquidity requirement**: markets with high volume/broad participation (e.g., S&P 500) are more likely to respect standard retracement levels than thin or individual-stock markets — "broad participation is a requirement" (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Practical caution on countertrend pullback entries**: entering countertrend at an anticipated retracement level without a confirming signal (slowing momentum, declining volume near the target) is "comparable to stepping in front of a moving train"; if price continues with rising volume instead of stalling, close the countertrend attempt quickly (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Fibonacci Retracement Ratios**: derived from the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21...; the ratio of consecutive terms approaches 1.618, its inverse 0.618, a secondary less-used ratio ≈0.382 (Kaufman Ch.4, Indicators.md). DeMark's refinement additionally uses 0.382, 0.50, 1.382, 2.236, and 2.618, applied to a move measured from the highest point reached since price last traded at the current low (rather than from the most recent swing high). **Practical caveat**: exact hits are unrealistic — use a tolerance band or multiple targets rather than a single price (Kaufman Ch.4).

### Prathap's 3-Bar Inside Day Pattern (a pullback-shaped continuation entry)
- **Bullish setup**: (1) an upward day, (2) followed by an inside day (a 1-bar consolidation/pullback), (3) followed by another upward day — a short-term continuation signal, especially noted for gold, silver, crude oil (Kaufman Ch.4, Johnan Prathap, 2011).
- **Bearish setup**: the mirror-image pattern. No explicit exit/stop rules given beyond "a short-term indicator" (Kaufman Ch.4).

### Concrete Mean-Reversion (SMA-Distance) Pullback Entry (Hilpisch)
Hilpisch's SMA-Distance Mean Reversion strategy is the book's closest coded analogue to a "wait for the pullback to fade" entry, though it targets reversion to the mean rather than confirmation of trend continuation (Hilpisch Ch.4, Strategies.md):
- **Entry logic (as coded)**: `distance = price − SMA`; `IF distance < −threshold THEN` go long (price too far below trend); `IF distance > threshold THEN` go short (price too far above trend) — long-short version only; the long-only event-based version omits the short branch.
- **Exit is distinctive versus Hilpisch's other strategies**: `IF distance crosses back through zero THEN` close to neutral — i.e., exit is triggered by price recrossing its own SMA, not by an opposite entry signal (Hilpisch Ch.4). This is the only one of Hilpisch's three core strategies with an independent exit rule rather than a bare position-flip.
- **Calibration weakness (flagged in this project's own extraction)**: the threshold is an absolute price-distance, not normalized (not in standard deviations or percent terms), so it must be re-tuned per instrument/price scale and its cross-instrument robustness is unaddressed by the author — parameters shown (SMA=25/threshold=3.5 for GDX; SMA=43/threshold=7.5 for GLD) appear hand-picked/illustrative rather than derived from a formal search (Hilpisch Ch.4).
- **Cost sensitivity**: this strategy's no-cost performance collapsed the hardest, proportionally, of Hilpisch's three core strategies once transaction costs were applied (AAPL.O 2010-2019: +439.08% no-cost → +53.75% with $10 fixed + 1% proportional costs) — despite a lower nominal trade count than the momentum strategy (Hilpisch Ch.6).

## 5. Pattern Entries (Inside Days, Reversal Days, Wide-Range Bars, Gaps)

Source: Kaufman Ch.3 (Charting) and Ch.15 (Short-Term Patterns), with day-trading-specific pattern variants covered separately in Ch.16 (see §6 below for the ones that are genuinely intraday-only).

### Reversal Day Patterns
- **Key Reversal Day (with volatility filter)** — bearish definition: a new high in an established uptrend, reversing to a lower-than-previous low, closing below the previous close, with elevated volatility (Kaufman Ch.3). EasyLanguage logic given: `close[1] > average(close[2],n) AND high >= highest(high[1],n) AND low < low[1] AND close < close[1]`.
  - **Kaufman's own tested improvement**: adding a volatility filter — trade only when the current day's true range exceeds 1.5× the 20-day average true range — "was much better" than the unfiltered rule, tested on heating oil 2005-2011 where the unfiltered rule gave only marginal gains (Kaufman Ch.3).
  - **Caveat**: cited academic studies (Eric Evans, *Futures*, 1985) found standalone key-reversal-day performance "strikingly unimpressive"; Kaufman frames the practitioner's task as finding the specific *context* (e.g., the added volatility filter) in which the pattern becomes reliable (Kaufman Ch.3) — an explicit, general statement of the "confirmation/filter" theme in §6.
- **Reversal Day / Outside Reversal Day (Ch.15's statistically-tested definitions)**: a downward reversal day posts a higher high than the previous day but closes lower; an upward reversal day has a lower open and a higher close; an *outside* reversal is the more extreme case — yesterday's high exceeded the prior high AND yesterday's close was below the prior low (or the mirror) (Kaufman Ch.15).
  - **Newer test (2009-2018) vs. older test (2000-2011), 8 markets**: bull-market persistence shows clearly in the newer sample — S&P Reversal Day follow-through improved to 55% (close-based, up from 52%); Amazon's Reversal Day rose to 50% (from 45%) and Outside Reversal to 63% (from 41%); Exxon improved similarly (read as reflecting overall market trend, not stock-specific edge). Euro degraded (mostly on the trend measure), consistent with settling into a mid-range regime (Kaufman Ch.15).
  - **10-year T-notes** showed a strong tendency (well into the 50s%) to follow through on *downward* reversals in both test windows — an unexpected finding given the persistent bond bull market, interpreted by Kaufman as evidence that even in a bull market not every day is up, creating a genuine contrarian opportunity (Kaufman Ch.15).
- **Colby's Pivot Point Reverse Trading System**: buy (and close shorts) when a pivot-point bottom occurs AND the close is higher than the previous close; sell (and close longs) when a pivot-point top occurs AND the close is lower than the previous close. A pivot point is defined as a bar higher (or lower) than the bars immediately before and after it. Backtested on the DJIA over 101 years (ending Dec 2000): ~7,000 trades (~70/year), "significant profits" per the cited study. No stop-loss, sizing, or risk-control rules given in the source (Kaufman Ch.3).
- **Cambridge Hook** (cited to Elias Crim, 1985): outside reversal day + RSI > 60% + rising volume AND open interest → a high-probability *downward* trend reversal (mirror for upward); stop placed above the hook day's high (Kaufman Indicators.md, Ch.11 material). A concrete multi-indicator-confirmed reversal-day entry.

### Inside Day / Outside Day Patterns
- **Outside Day with Outside Close** (Arnold, 1984): an outside day (high & low both outside the prior day's range) with an outside close (close beyond the prior day's high or low). Original rules (tested profitable through the mid-1980s only): (1) buy on the close if close > prior high, sell if close < prior low; (2) stop-loss just below the outside day's low (long) or above its high (short); (3) close the position 3 days after entry (tested range 1-5 days) (Kaufman Ch.4).
  - **Explicit regime-decay warning**: this pattern's original rule reportedly stopped working after the mid-1980s; *reversing* it — fading the breakout by selling when the close is above the prior high on a volatile day — reportedly performs much better in more recent markets. A conditional/profit-taking exit and removal of trades following very narrow-range precursor days are both suggested refinements (Kaufman Ch.4).
- **Prathap's 3-Bar Inside Day Pattern** — see §4 above (an inside-day-as-pullback continuation entry).
- **Dunnigan's inside-day repeat signals** — see §4 above (inside day as a component of the Thrust Method's strongest repeat-signal patterns).
- **Toby Crabel's inside-day filter (day-trading context)**: preceding-pattern filters (inside days, NR4-style low-volatility bars, bull/bear hooks) meaningfully improved the percentage of profitable opening-range breakout trades — e.g., bonds 60%→76%, soybeans 60%→70% for "Any Day" vs. "Inside Day"-filtered subsets (Kaufman Ch.16, cited to Crabel 1990/1989) — a direct example of a daily-bar *pattern* used as a confirmation filter for an intraday breakout entry, bridging §5 and §6.

### Wide-Range Bars / Spikes
- **Quantified Spike Detection Rule**: a single, highly volatile bar whose high (or low) is well beyond the recent range, confirmed only once the following bar's range is much lower (for an upward spike). EasyLanguage logic: `Spike = high[1]-highest(high,n)[2] > k*average(truerange,n)[2] AND high[1]-high > k*average(truerange,n)[2]`. Parameters: n = lookback for highest-high/ATR; k = multiplier, recommended k > 0.75 (k > 1 gives more desirable but less frequent signals). Framed by Kaufman as an identification/flagging tool for a local top formed by a spike bar, not a full standalone entry/exit system (Kaufman Ch.3).
- **Rudd's "Day Trades Following a Wide-Ranging Bar"** — see §6 (day-trading pattern entries built directly on wide-range bars).

### Candlestick Patterns
- **Morning Star / Evening Star (Kaufman's simplified programmed rule)**: because the strict body-size/gap requirements of the classic 3-day pattern produced too few signals, Kaufman's own tested simplification uses: Day 1 candle body > its own 20-day average body; Day 2 body < its own 20-day average body; Day 3 body > its own 20-day average body, with directional sign following the classic morning-star (down-up-up) / evening-star (up-down-down) definition. Result cited: "a modest number of trades," but "the S&P performs well on the day following both patterns." No explicit stop-loss/exit/sizing rules given beyond "the day following" the signal (Kaufman Ch.3).
- **Bulkowski's candlestick testing (an explicit warning against trusting classic pattern names)**: several classically-labeled "reversal" candlestick patterns (deliberation, bearish doji star, last engulfing bottom/top) were empirically found to behave as *continuation* signals far more often than their traditional interpretation implies (Kaufman Ch.4, Concepts.md).
- **Academic validation of chart patterns (Lo, Mamaysky, Wang, 2000)**: a rigorous kernel-regression-smoothed, local-extrema-defined algebraic treatment of classic chart patterns found several patterns (e.g., head-and-shoulders, 3× more often) occur far more often in real NYSE/NASDAQ data than in synthetic random price series, and most tested formations produced statistically significant positive 3-day-forward returns — cited by Kaufman as broad academic validation that chart patterns reflect genuine participant behavior, while also noting it remains "far from certain" whether the algebraic definitions or a human technician's more flexible recognition yields better returns (Kaufman Ch.4, Concepts.md).
- **Cup and Cap Pattern**: a cup formation (buy setup in a downtrend) is a 3-bar cluster where the middle bar has the lowest low/close. Buy signal requires ALL of: (1) the cup is the lowest point of the current downtrend; (2) the upward breakout occurs within 3 days of the cup; (3) price closes above the highest high (middle bar) of the cup. Invalidation: a close below the cup's low. Expected forecast horizon only ~2 days forward, per the source (Kaufman Ch.3). Cap formation is the mirrored sell setup.

### Gap Entries
- **Opening-gap statistical structure (TSM Gap Study tool, multiple markets)**: across every futures market tested (S&P, bonds, crude oil, euro), downward gaps showed a very strong (98-100%) tendency to cross back above the previous close at some point in the session — a systematic intraday mean-reversion signature stronger than the corresponding upward-gap reversal tendency (Kaufman Ch.15).
- **275-stock generalized study (2000-2017, 2% gap-size buckets)**: upward gaps of 1-3% occur on 31.26% of days, with the pullback (retracement from the open) averaging ~40% of the gap size (larger for bigger initial gaps), and the average close ending near the opening price. Downward gaps are less frequent (27.50% for the same bucket) and their pullbacks (recoveries) run slightly larger, with the average close consistently *higher* than the open — further evidence of upward equity bias (Kaufman Ch.15).
- **Kaufman's derived gap-entry rule of thumb**: for an existing long facing an upward gap, take profits into the gap, wait for the ~40% pullback, then re-enter; for a flat trader wanting long-only exposure, capture roughly 40% of the opening gap outright by entering into the pullback. For downward gaps, a day trader can buy shortly after the open once volume starts dropping (a sign the sell-off is exhausting) and sell into a rally of about half the gap-down size (Kaufman Ch.15).
- **Individual-stock variation warning**: averages can hide problems — a 9-11% downward Netflix gap rallied 15.32% before closing 16.68% *lower* than the open on the same day, illustrating that single extreme events can dominate a bucket's statistics; not every volatile name presents an exploitable gap edge (Tesla showed no useful percentages despite its dramatic price history) (Kaufman Ch.15).
- **"True Gap" entry (DeMark / Larry Williams)**: a "true" upward gap opens above yesterday's high and does not trade back down to it. Kaufman's own TSM Intraday Gaps tool tested multiple entry/exit combinations on 15-30 minute data; the best combination found was entry on a pullback to the previous close plus exit on the next open (Kaufman Ch.16).

## 6. Day-Trading Entries

Source: Kaufman Ch.16 in full, cross-referencing Ch.15's time-of-day statistical foundations.

### Structural Preconditions Before Any Intraday Entry Rule
- **Transaction-cost reality check**: for stocks, capturing even half the average daily high-low range on a modest position can leave costs eating 12%+ of the maximum theoretical range unless position size scales up considerably; futures (especially the emini S&P) are generally a better day-trading vehicle because fixed round-turn costs are under 1% of the average multi-year dollar range (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **"Not So Fast" finding**: for both stocks and futures, the open-to-close range is close to 50% of the full high-low range — meaning many intraday systems are structurally mean-reverting by design: wait for a move after the open, then trade against it if conditions are right, extending the effective capturable range (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **Slippage asymmetry**: trend/directional entries (buying new highs, selling new lows) face materially higher slippage than mean-reversion entries (trading against the move); waiting for a reversal rather than chasing a breakout entry reduces both risk and slippage, and day traders' high trade count allows greater selectivity (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **Trend as a filter, not a direct entry, at intraday scale**: a 20-minute-bar worked example (crude oil, 10/20/40-bar MAs) shows the fastest MA whipsawing on the gap open while slower MAs lag; Kaufman's conclusion is that intraday trends are best used as a *directional filter* based on daily data, not as a direct entry trigger at the bar level (Kaufman Ch.16). Traditional trend-following technical analysis is generally not used for holding periods under 3 days, and probably not under 20 days.
- **Key-level hierarchy for day-trading entries (decreasing importance, confidence rises when levels coincide)**: today's open/high/low → yesterday's close/high/low → last week's high/low → memorable older support/resistance → life-of-contract high/low (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **Bar-size choice**: no single correct intraday bar length; common choices 5/10/15/20/30-min and 1-hour; tick bars (fixed number of price changes rather than fixed time) can produce a cleaner trend signal than fixed-time bars during low-activity periods — a single worked USDGBP example showed 100-tick bars recognizing one clean downtrend then one clean uptrend where 5-minute bars showed false trend turns, though Kaufman flags this as a single-example finding, not a general claim (Kaufman Ch.16).

### The Opening Range Breakout Family
- **1st Hour Breakout (Opening Range Breakout Using Time)**: the foundational time-based version — breakout of the high/low range formed in a fixed period (e.g., the first hour) after the open (Kaufman Ch.16, Strategies.md).
- **Toby Crabel's Opening Range Breakout Using Volatility**: Crabel used fixed per-market threshold values (not a simple fixed-tick breakout) and found preceding-pattern filters (inside days, NR4-style low volatility, bull/bear hooks) meaningfully improved the percentage of profitable trades — see §5 above for the specific bonds/soybeans percentages (Kaufman Ch.16, cited to Crabel 1990/1989).
- **Raschke's NR4 Range-Contraction Breakout**: an opening-range/volatility-breakout variant gated on a prior narrow-range (NR4-style) contraction bar (Kaufman Ch.16, Strategies.md).
- **Mark Fisher's Opening Range Breakout (from *The Logical Trader*)**: uses the opening range high/low (ORH/ORL) plus A/C threshold levels, time-based confirmation, and a pivot-range bias, with a fade-the-key-levels variant also given. Kaufman's own extraction flags the exact A/B/C multiplier percentages (beyond the illustrative 100%/150% examples) and the precise OR-multiple time-confirmation values as partially garbled by PDF extraction — the qualitative rule structure is fully preserved, but exact default values beyond 100%/150% are not claimed (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **Larry Williams' Filtered Opening Range Breakout (S&P and bonds)**: adds a %R-style filter to the opening-range breakout; the full normalized %R formula is an embedded-graphic gap, though the numerator (Highest(High,10) − current close) is stated qualitatively (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **Volatility Breakout Based on the Open / Previous Close**: volatility measured as an n-day ATR (5-20 days); a breakout factor (a percentage, typically <1.0) of that ATR is added/subtracted from either the day's open or the previous close to form breakout bands. Centering on the previous close embeds trend information and can generate a signal on the very first bar; centering on the open is trend-neutral. No a priori answer as to which is superior — Kaufman states it requires testing, and notes trending markets are expected to favor the previous-close method, which also tends to generate more signals (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **TSM Flexible Intraday Breakout parameter-selection case study (S&P 30-min, 2010-Apr.2017, $8/contract/side cost)** — a fully worked one-parameter-at-a-time optimization walkthrough, explicitly recommended by Kaufman over simultaneous multi-parameter optimization so each parameter's individual contribution stays interpretable:
  1. Breakout factor from the open: profitable factors cluster 0.5-1.0, peaking at 0.6 (~$450,000 total).
  2. Breakout factor from the previous close: similarly clustered, peaking near $800,000 — better overall than the open-based version.
  3. Adding a trend filter: open-based version improved only slightly (win rate 55%→56%, avg trade $979→$1,100); previous-close version improved more clearly (no-filter $801,912/347 trades/61% win rate → with a 50-70 day trend filter, ~$816,000/319 trades/62% win rate).
  4. **General lesson stated by Kaufman**: neither breakout variant showed a decisive case for a trend filter; the recommended process is to test options individually, record results, then combine only the previously-selected best parameters in pairs, and to judge visually via equity-curve *smoothness*, not just net-profit tables.
  5. Mean-reversion variant (reverse the breakout logic, open-based): overall profit only 14% less than the directional version but with a much smoother equity curve, average profit per trade roughly double ($1,973) on fewer trades (219), 64% profitable (Kaufman Ch.16).

### Non-Breakout Day-Trading Pattern Entries
- **Rudd's Special Set-Up Patterns for Stocks (5-minute data)**: five named patterns — Dip and Rally, Consolidation, Delayed Consolidation, Early Breakout, Reversing After a False Breakout (Kaufman Ch.16, Strategies.md — full rule sets not further detailed in the cross-cutting files beyond the pattern names).
- **Day Trades Following a Wide-Ranging Bar (Rudd)**: two named setups — Breakout of a Higher Open; Selling a Gap Open (Kaufman Ch.16, Strategies.md).
- **Rudd's Reversal Bar Set-Up**: a 2-day precondition pattern with entry on the 3rd day (Kaufman Ch.16, Strategies.md).
- **Raschke and Conners' Momentum Pinball (LBR/RSI™ + 1st-Hour Breakout)**: LBR/RSI™ = a 3-day RSI applied to a 1-day rate of change (reduces lag vs. plain RSI); ties conceptually to Taylor's 3-day cycle (Kaufman Ch.15/16). Tested on emini S&P 2000-May 2011: $703/contract gain on 1,734 trades, no costs (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **TSM Midday Support and Resistance**: 15/30-minute bars, morning-range vs. afternoon-breakout/reversal logic, tested on S&P and crude oil (Kaufman Ch.16, Strategies.md). Bressert's chart (1970s) is the underlying manual template — a detailed table mapping time-of-day + developing-range + previous-day-range combinations to specific buy/sell trigger levels that tighten as the day progresses (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **TSM Intraday Shocks 2**: limit-order shock-fade entries (factor=2.2 for gold), exiting on volatility reversion or session end; backtested profitable on gold, 2010-April 2017, >50% winning trades. Kaufman suggests (untested) refinements: add a volume filter, and adapt a "floor-trader noise" heuristic (extend the hold while both volume and volatility remain elevated, exit when either reverts to average) (Kaufman Ch.16).
- **Volume-spike mean-reversion**: a volume spike ≥1.5× the highest average-volume bar can trigger a mean-reversion trade with a profit target or close-of-day exit — the intraday analogue of daily volume-spike trading, occurring more frequently at bar frequency (Kaufman Ch.16).

### Time-of-Day Statistical Foundations Underlying These Entries (Kaufman Ch.15)
- Volume and volatility both follow a U-shape (highest at open and close, lowest at midday) in stocks and futures alike (Kaufman Ch.15, Ch.16).
- **Intraday high/low distribution (30-year bonds)**: the first bar has the highest frequency of being the day's high (25%) or low (27%); the last bar is next-highest (16%/13%). Because of this, confidence that an early extreme is the actual session high/low grows as the day progresses without a breach — the explicit statistical basis Kaufman gives for the classic opening range breakout system (buy a new high / sell a new low after some elapsed time past the open) (Kaufman Ch.15).
- **S&P 500 (2009-2017 bull regime)**: by 12:30pm NY time there is a 50% chance the day's high has already occurred and a 61% chance the day's low has already occurred — framed as giving explicit probabilities to an opening-range-breakout decision rather than trading it blind (Kaufman Ch.15).
- **Regime sensitivity, explicitly flagged**: during the mid-1990s NASDAQ bull run, investors bought both the open and every intraday pullback, replacing the normal U-shaped high/low distribution with a jump at the open followed by a flat line — "a reminder that even robust intraday structural patterns can be regime-overridden" (Kaufman Ch.15). Cross-ref `07_market_regimes.md`.

## 7. Confirmation and Filters (ADX, Volume, Multi-Timeframe)

### Volume/Open-Interest Confirmation
- **Bollinger's own risk-reduction rule**: standard Bollinger Band mean-reversion use is risky in volatile conditions; Bollinger recommends confirming a downside band penetration with volume/breadth indicators — if price falls but volume isn't rising and negative breadth doesn't confirm, a buy is realistic (i.e., the breakdown lacks confirmation, so fade it rather than follow it) (Kaufman Ch.8).
- **Combined Bollinger/Keltner/Chaikin signal** (Williams' method, cited to Billy Williams, 2010): long entry requires the standard Bollinger Band to narrow fully inside a 20-day Keltner Channel WHILE a 21-day Chaikin Oscillator is < 0, THEN the Chaikin Oscillator crosses above 0. Short entry is the mirror image (Kaufman Ch.8) — a three-indicator confirmation stack for a single entry decision.
- **Cambridge Hook** (see §5): outside reversal day + RSI > 60% + rising volume AND open interest → a high-probability reversal entry, with volume and open interest both required as confirmation (Kaufman Indicators.md).
- **Volume-spike day-trading confirmation**: see §6.

### ADX / Directional Movement as an Entry Filter
- **Full derivation chain** (Kaufman Ch.23, cited to J. Welles Wilder): PDM = today's high − yesterday's high; MDM = yesterday's low − today's low; the daily DM is whichever of PDM/MDM is larger (both zero on an inside day); after Wilder's average-off smoothing, PDI14 = 100×(PDM14/TR14), MDI14 = 100×(MDM14/TR14) (both reconstructed to the standard Wilder form, consistent with the chapter's stated 0-100 normalization); DX = 100×|PDI14−MDI14|/(PDI14+MDI14) (reconstructed standard form); ADX = DX smoothed with the same ~0.133 equivalent constant.
- **Ruggiero's ADX trending/consolidating threshold rules** (Kaufman Ch.23): ADX crossing above 25 → trending; below 20 → consolidating; below 45 after having been higher → consolidating; rising above 10 on 3-of-4 days after being lower → a trend is starting (remains in effect until the 5-day ADX difference turns negative). Additional levels given: >40 strong trend, >50 extremely strong, >70 "power" trend.
- **Oscillator Method with ADX Filter** (Lars Kestner, 2003): a 10-day/50-day MA-difference oscillator gated by an ADX no-trend filter — i.e., only take the oscillator's entry signal when ADX confirms a trending (not consolidating) regime. Kaufman flags the exact inequality thresholds for this specific combination as an embedded-graphic gap, though the qualitative gating structure is explicit (Kaufman Ch.9, Indicators.md).
- **Kaufman's own critique of a related ADX-adjacent method**: Chande's Trend Strength Ranking (St = net return over n days ÷ f(volatility)) is critiqued by Kaufman as implicitly assuming volatility rises with price when applied with a log transform to stocks — Kaufman states this assumption "is not correct" (Kaufman Ch.23, Indicators.md). Included here as an explicit instance of one source-book author critiquing another named author's published trend/confirmation method — preserved as an opinion, not adopted as fact.

### Multi-Timeframe Confirmation (Elder's Triple Screen and Related Systems)
Source: Kaufman Ch.19 in full.

- **Core rationale**: trends are best identified over longer periods, but entries require a faster response; combining two or three frequencies (each with a specific role) lets a trader filter/select short-term entries with a better-than-average chance of becoming winners (Kaufman Ch.19). Named single-timeframe problems this design solves: very short-term data has noise that obscures direction; weekly-only charts show clear direction but present higher risk and little opportunity for a good entry point.
- **Standard 3-tier structure that has "solidified"**: very short bars for timing, medium bars for the primary trading signal, longer bars for the overall trend/big picture (Kaufman Ch.19).
- **Tuning two time frames together**: once a trend signal has fired, only the *first* subsequent oversold/overbought reading of the timing oscillator matters for entry — and "oversold" in an established uptrend may be a much higher-than-classic value (worked example: 50 on a 0-100 scale can function as the effective oversold threshold within an uptrend). Explicitly identified by Kaufman as the same technique already used in Raschke's First Cross system (Kaufman Ch.9, Ch.19).
- **Rule of thumb for sizing the timing oscillator's speed to the trend's holding period**: if a trend system's typical holding period is ~2 months (~45 business days), the timing oscillator should have a good chance of an improved entry within the first 3 days; for a ~1-week average holding period, use a 30-minute or 1-hour chart for the momentum timing (Kaufman Ch.19).

#### Elder's Triple Screen Trading System (Dr. Alexander Elder, *Trading for a Living*, 1993/2014)
- **Time-frame ratio rule**: each time frame relates to the adjacent one by a factor of ~5 (Elder's own observed ratio). If daily is the middle period, the short interval is ~1-2 hours and the long interval is ~1 week; if the middle interval is 10 minutes, short-term = 2 minutes, long-term = 1 hour (Kaufman Ch.19).
- **Screen 1 — Major Move (lowest-frequency data, e.g., weekly)**: identifies the market's overall "tide." Elder's specific tool is the slope of the weekly MACD histogram (roughly a 13-week/1-quarter exponential smoothing). Rule: trend is up when this week's value is higher than last week's; down when lower.
- **Screen 2 — Intermediate Move (middle-frequency, e.g., daily — the actual trading timeframe)**: the specific oscillator is secondary to correctly matching the time frame and identifying market waves within Screen 1's major move. Elder's two named oscillators (a stochastic can substitute):
  - **Force Index** (2-day exponential smoothing, sc=0.333): long-entry logic — (1) Screen 1's major move must be up; (2) the 2-day-exponential Force Index falls below its center line but does NOT fall below its own recent multi-week low. (A stochastic substitute buys when the stochastic falls below 30.)
  - **Elder-Ray** (separates bull power from bear power): buy logic — (1) Screen 1's major move must be up; (2) bear power is negative but rising, and must not turn positive. Two optional filters: (3) the most recent bull-power peak is not significantly lower than the previous peak; (4) bear power is rising from a bullish divergence.
- **Screen 3 — Timing (highest-frequency data, e.g., 60-minute bars)**: fastest-response screen, primarily for intraday breakout entries (cross-ref §6) — mechanically a simple Buy Stop at the shortest bar's breakout level (worked example: buy when the current hourly bar's high exceeds the highest high of the prior day's hourly bars); no separate calculation needed.
- **3-step stop-loss process (long position)**: (1) initial stop below the entry day's low OR the previous day's low, whichever is lower; (2) move the stop to breakeven as soon as practical (must leave room, or it will be hit immediately); (3) thereafter trail the stop to protect 50% of the highest achieved open profit, optionally also considering profit-taking when the stochastic/Force Index rises above 70% (Kaufman Ch.19; cross-ref `09_exits.md`).
- **Note on a terminology quirk Kaufman preserves rather than resolves**: the source describes the screens in reverse numbering relative to display order in one place ("Screen 1 (or panel 3) holds the longest time frame while Screen 3 (panel 1) shows the shortest") — preserved verbatim in the Kaufman extraction to avoid introducing an ambiguity not clearly present (or clearly present) in the original (Kaufman Ch.19).

#### Robert Krausz's Multiple Time Frames (*The Fibonacci Trader*)
- Described by Kaufman as "the most robust and fully automated approach to multiple time frames," combining Gann-derived geometric/fractal theory with three coordinated time frames — shortest = the actual trading time frame, two longer frames add confirming context (Kaufman Ch.19).
- **Six "Laws of Multiple Time Frames" (Krausz, copyrighted)**: (1) every time frame has its own structure; (2) higher time frames overrule lower time frames; (3) prices in the lower time frame tend to respect the "energy points" of the higher time frame's structure; (4) energy points of support/resistance created by the higher time frame's "vibration" can be validated by lower-time-frame action; (5) the trend created by the *next* (higher) time period defines the tradable trend; (6) what appears as chaos in one time period can be order in another (Kaufman Ch.19).
- **Practical warning, explicit and non-substitutable**: a 10-period MA of 1-hour bars is NOT interchangeable with a 40-period MA of 15-minute bars, even though both span the same nominal elapsed time — nor is a 10-week MA interchangeable with a 50-day MA. Reason given: averaging cannot fully remove noise, and fewer, larger data points over the same span always produce a smoother result than more, smaller data points — genuinely different bar frequencies (hourly/daily/weekly) give a materially different market picture than three "equivalent-length" moving averages built from a single frequency (Kaufman Ch.19). This is a direct, important caution for any freqtrade multi-timeframe-informed entry design.

#### Martin Pring's KST (Know Sure Thing) System
- Multiple-time-frame confirmation built entirely from stacked rate-of-change (ROC) indicators at different periods, each with its own "unique cycle" — alignment across cycles flags major/intermediate tops and bottoms (Kaufman Ch.19, Martin Pring, 1993).
- **Basic trading logic from the raw ROC lines (before the composite KST)**: (1) a trendline based on roughly half the longest ROC period sets trade direction; (2) the longer (24-period) ROC identifies the major move; (3) strongest price moves occur when all three ROC values move in the same direction; (4) if the 24-period ROC peaks while the other two are still rising, any resulting sell-off is expected to be minor (likewise if the shorter two peak while the 24-period is still rising); (5) a significant sell-off is expected only when all three ROC values peak together and then decline together (Kaufman Ch.19).
- **Composite KST construction**: a weighted sum of four smoothed ROC periods, step-weighted in proportion to length — 24-period ROC weight 4, 18-period weight 3, 12-period weight 2, 9-period weight 1. **Author-flagged gap**: Pring's original description does not specify the smoothing period for the 18-period ROC component; Kaufman's own extraction uses 9 as a working assumption for that missing value, explicitly labeled as such (Kaufman Ch.19).
- **Trading signal generation**: trade in the direction of a 12-period MA trendline, timed by the KST crossing the ROC line following the trendline's first turn; can exit on the opposite-direction KST/ROC crossover. Kaufman's source explicitly requires KST signals always be used in combination with a trendline — never as a standalone system (Kaufman Ch.19).

## 8. Pardo's Entry Design Principles (Detail Beyond §1)

Pardo Ch.5's material is already integrated into §1 above as the file's organizing frame. This section adds the design-tradeoff material Pardo attaches specifically to entries, and cross-references the risk/sizing components he insists must accompany any entry rule.

- **Complexity is not neutral**: strategy complexity is "not inherently good or bad, but higher complexity is harder to verify and is 'the ultimate hiding place of overfitting'" (Pardo Ch.5). This is stated as a general strategy-design principle but is aimed specifically at entry-rule and entry-filter design — the section immediately follows with the filter-count overfitting warning quoted in full in §1.
- **Named entry-rule archetypes Pardo lists as strategy *families*** (not detailed as full systems in this source): moving average crossovers, volatility breakouts, price channel breakouts, single-bar patterns, multiple-bar patterns, chart patterns, candlestick patterns, swing-based systems, indicator-based systems (Pardo Ch.5). This taxonomy maps directly onto §2-6 of this file, confirming that Kaufman's much more granular treatment covers every category Pardo names at the top level.
- **An entry rule is inseparable from its accompanying risk stop, in Pardo's framing**: the chapter moves immediately from entry/exit definitions into three formal risk-stop sizing methods (dollar risk stop, volatility risk stop, percent-of-equity risk stop) with worked numeric examples, treating stop design as the entry rule's mandatory companion rather than a separate later decision (Pardo Ch.5; full detail belongs in the risk-management files, not duplicated here). The same inseparability is Kaufman's own repeated theme (Master_Summary.md Author Insight #4: "risk control is inseparable from system design — not an overlay added later").
- **Pardo's worked entry-rule illustrations** (buy side; sell side is the mirror unless stated otherwise): 5/20-day MA cross; RSI closes below 20; daily and weekly close both up ≥1% (an explicit two-timeframe confirmation example, conceptually adjacent to §7's multi-timeframe material though Pardo does not develop it further); close > yesterday's close + 50% of the daily range (a volatility-scaled breakout-style entry); close > each of the previous 3 closes (a short-run momentum/pattern entry) (Pardo Ch.5).
- **The Turtle Trading Strategy as Pardo's own illustrative example (not a Pardo design)**: "Goes long when an x-day high has been penetrated and goes short when an x-day low has been broken," exiting on "opposite signals from a shorter y-day high or low" — used by Pardo in Ch.2 purely to contrast systematic vs. discretionary trading knowledge, explicitly described as "information-poor" (Pardo Strategies.md, Ch.2). This is the same N-day-breakout family covered exhaustively in Kaufman §2 above, presented by Pardo at a much higher level of abstraction and with no risk/sizing detail given.
- **Two-Moving-Average Crossover System (MA2/EOTS-MA) — Pardo's own running teaching example** across Chapters 2, 5, 7, 9, 10, 13: go long when MA1 crosses from below to above MA2 (yesterday MA1<MA2, today MA1>MA2), go short on the mirror cross; a reversing, always-in-the-market strategy; required condition given in Ch.7's formal spec is that the slow MA period must be at least double the fast MA period ("Y is never less than 2 times X") (Pardo Strategies.md, Ch.7). Used specifically as a vehicle for Pardo's overfitting parable in Ch.13, where escalating parameter additions (a 2nd MA plus 2 volatility bands) inflate in-sample profit from $10,000 to $65,000 but the final over-parameterized version LOSES $15,000 out-of-sample (Pardo Strategies.md, Ch.13) — directly germane to this project's own overfitting-gate discipline (cross-ref MEMORY.md's DSR audit).
- **Confirmation-signal example specific to Pardo's Ch.8 methodology narrative**: adding "a two-day time filter" requiring an MA crossover to persist 2 days before triggering entry on day 3 — shown by Pardo as an illustration of rule-verification technique, explicitly NOT claimed to be part of the "official" MA2 system (Pardo Strategies.md, Ch.8).
- **Position sizing as an entry-adjacent decision Pardo treats as potentially more important than the entry rule itself**: "many professional trading strategists believe that the sizing principle is more important than the trading strategy itself" (Pardo Ch.5) — four named sizing methods given (volatility-adjusted, Martingale, Anti-Martingale, Kelly/Optimal f), detailed in the risk-management knowledge-base files rather than duplicated here.

## 9. Concrete Implementations (Chan, Hilpisch) — Cross-Cutting Notes

This section synthesizes what Chan's and Hilpisch's code-level strategies collectively reveal about entry-rule *implementation* that the more narrative Kaufman/Pardo material does not surface as directly: the practical gap between an entry idea and a coded, tested, cost-aware trading rule.

### Chan's Entry Implementations Are Almost Entirely Cross-Sectional / Statistical, Not Price-Pattern-Based
Unlike Kaufman, Chan's book contains essentially no breakout, MA-cross, or chart-pattern entry logic as core content — his entries are statistical/econometric triggers:
- **Cointegration z-score entry** (GLD/GDX pair trade): short the spread when z-score ≥ +2 (or a tighter ±1 in sensitivity testing); long the spread when z-score ≤ −2 (or −1); exit when the z-score returns to within 1 (or 0.5) standard deviation of the mean, or when the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck half-life (~10 days in the worked example) elapses (Chan Ch.2-3, Strategies.md). **Explicit warning preserved from the source**: correlation is not a substitute for a cointegration test — KO/PEP are correlated (0.4849) but fail the CADF cointegration test, and are therefore not a suitable pair-entry candidate despite being same-industry (Chan Ch.3, Strategies.md).
- **Bollinger Band mean-reversion entry** (illustrative failure case): short when price exceeds +2 moving standard deviations above the MA, buy when it drops below −2; exit at ±1 standard deviation. This example exists specifically to demonstrate that an apparently excellent pre-cost entry signal (Sharpe ≈3) can become catastrophically unprofitable (Sharpe ≈ −3) once a mere 1 basis point of transaction cost is applied at 5-minute rebalancing frequency (Chan Ch.2). **This is the single most load-bearing transaction-cost warning in the Chan extraction and directly qualifies every mean-reversion entry rule in this file** — any high-frequency mean-reversion entry (Kaufman's day-trading and congestion-phase rules included) should be read alongside this warning.
- **Cross-sectional mean-reversal entry** (Khandani & Lo style): weight each stock inversely to its deviation from the market's average prior-day return — buy the worst previous-day performers, short the best. Chan's own finding: switching the rebalancing trigger from the market CLOSE to the market OPEN dramatically improved viability (pre-cost Sharpe 0.25→4.43; post-cost Sharpe −3.19→+0.78) — "a striking demonstration of how execution-timing choice, not just signal logic, materially affects viability" (Chan Ch.3, Strategies.md).
- **Regime-switching/turning-point entry** (machine-learning rule-combination example): entry fires when a 1-day percent-change threshold (±1% or ±3%) is breached in combination with the stock being at/near a 10-day high or low, with the exact holding period (1-60 days) dynamically chosen by a perceptron reoptimized on a rolling 50-day window — an explicit example of an ML-selected *holding period* attached to a simple technical entry trigger (Chan Ch.7, Strategies.md).
- **PCA factor-model entry (an explicit negative/failure example)**: long the top 50 / short the bottom 50 stocks by PCA-factor-implied expected return, assuming factor returns persist unchanged into the next period. This assumption failed in Chan's own worked test (average annualized return ≈ **−1.81%** per the source's own code comment — the source itself does not clearly state whether this is a percent or decimal-fraction convention, an ambiguity flagged as unresolved rather than silently resolved either way; stated consistently with `16_research_hypotheses.md` and `13_indicator_reference.md`), explicitly presented by Chan as a genuine negative result included for pedagogical/cautionary purposes (Chan Ch.7, Strategies.md).
- **Seasonal/calendar entries with no technical trigger at all** (January Effect, year-on-year monthly seasonality, gasoline/natural-gas calendar trades): entries fire purely on a calendar date or a prior-period return ranking, with no price-pattern confirmation whatsoever. Chan's own backtests show these are inconsistent (January Effect: losses in 2 of 3 tested years; year-on-year seasonality: negative Sharpe, explicitly "disappeared" post-publication) except where a genuine, real-economic seasonal driver exists (gasoline/natural-gas driving/cooling-season demand, profitable every tested year 1995-2008) (Chan Ch.7, Strategies.md). **Cross-references file `07_market_regimes.md` and `10-18` overfitting files** for the data-snooping-bias framing Chan attaches to all of these.

### Hilpisch's Entry Implementations — the Coded Baseline for §2-4
Already detailed inline in §3 (SMA Crossover) and §4 (SMA-Distance Mean Reversion) above. Two additional cross-cutting findings from Hilpisch not yet stated:
- **Time Series Momentum entry** (`sign(rolling_mean_return)` over an N-bar window; go/increase long on +1, go/increase short on −1): this is Hilpisch's highest-turnover, most cost-sensitive entry family. On gold (XAU=, 2010-2019, momentum=3, $10,000 start): no transaction costs → +$7,395.53 outperformance; with just 0.1% proportional transaction costs → **−$2,652.93, a net LOSS** relative to benchmark (Hilpisch Ch.4, Strategies.md). This is Hilpisch's direct analogue to Chan's Bollinger-Band cost-collapse warning above, and reinforces the same point from an entirely independent source book: **short-lookback, high-turnover entries that look excellent gross of costs can be completely destroyed by realistic transaction costs, and this must be tested explicitly, not assumed.**
- **Lagged linear-regression and logistic-regression direction entries** (`sign(predicted_return)` from N lagged log returns as regressors/features): both show the "hit-ratio vs. P&L disconnect" pattern explicitly warned about by Hilpisch — a >50% hit ratio does not guarantee net profitability (it can still lose if it misses rare large moves), and conversely a lower hit ratio can coexist with higher cumulative performance (5-lag GLD logistic regression: 53.1% hit ratio but higher cumulative return than a 3-lag version's 53.3% hit ratio) (Hilpisch Ch.4). **In-sample/out-of-sample splitting is explicitly built into these backtester classes** (`LRVectorBacktester`, `ScikitVectorBacktester`) as the book's main defense against entry-rule overfitting. Full lag-construction technique, hit-ratio metric definition (49.9%→51.9% improvement from regressing on `sign(return)` instead of raw return), and the scikit-learn three-step API pattern are detailed in `14_backtesting_and_validation.md` §12.6 (not reproduced here to avoid duplication).
- **DNN and AdaBoost entries**: both show large train/test accuracy gaps (DNN base version: 51.5% train / 50.5% test; AdaBoost: 80.5% train / 56.7% test) despite ensemble methods explicitly intended to reduce overfitting — and the DNN section explicitly omits transaction-cost modeling altogether, a gap the book itself flags relative to its own linear/logistic examples (Hilpisch Ch.5, Strategies.md). The AdaBoost example is the single most complete "idea to production" pipeline in the Hilpisch extraction (backtest → risk analysis → Kelly-based leverage sizing → live deployment), but Hilpisch's own extraction notes an unreconciled mismatch between the 10-minute backtest bar length and the 5-second/2-second live-deployment bar length — a caution about naively porting a backtested entry rule's statistics to a materially different live execution frequency (Hilpisch Ch.5, Ch.10, Strategies.md). **Full architectures** (DNN: `Dense(64,relu)`→`Dense(64,relu)`→`Dense(1,sigmoid)`, reproducibility-seeding technique, enriched 8-feature result; AdaBoost: `DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, min_samples_leaf=15)` base estimator × `AdaBoostClassifier(n_estimators=15)`, 36-column 6-feature×6-lag set) **and the full end-to-end AdaBoost pipeline** (vectorized backtest with costs → Kelly-leverage sizing → VaR/drawdown risk analysis at 30x leverage → model persistence → online-algorithm live deployment) are documented in `14_backtesting_and_validation.md` §12.6, with the online-algorithm live-deployment mechanics (the `iloc[-2]`/drop-incomplete-bar anti-look-ahead technique referenced above) in §12.5 of the same file.

### Cross-Book Synthesis: What the Concrete-Code Sources Add That Kaufman/Pardo Do Not
1. **Explicit, quantified transaction-cost destruction of high-turnover entries** — both Chan (Bollinger 5-min mean reversion) and Hilpisch (3-day momentum) independently demonstrate the same phenomenon on different instruments with different methodologies: a gross-of-cost edge of Sharpe 3+ (or +$7,395 outperformance) becomes a net loss with cost assumptions as small as 0.1%-1bp. Kaufman's Ch.16 day-trading chapter makes the same point narratively (slippage/cost arithmetic on the emini S&P) but without an equivalent single-strategy before/after collapse this stark.
2. **In-sample/out-of-sample splitting as a structural, code-level defense against entry-rule overfitting** — Hilpisch's `LRVectorBacktester`/`ScikitVectorBacktester` classes bake this in as a first-class parameter; Kaufman and Pardo both discuss the principle narratively (walk-forward, 60/20/20 splits) but do not provide code.
3. **The hit-ratio-vs-P&L disconnect**, demonstrated repeatedly and explicitly across Hilpisch's regression/classification entries, is a concrete quantitative instance of Pardo's more general point that a strategy's risk-adjusted return and robustness are what matter, not any single accuracy statistic (Pardo Ch.5's closing summary).
4. **Statistical/cointegration-based entries (Chan) sit entirely outside Kaufman's chart/trend/pattern taxonomy** and are not otherwise represented in this file's §2-6 — they belong conceptually beside Kaufman Ch.13 (Spreads and Arbitrage), which this project's own prior research (MEMORY.md: basis-proxy/funding-rate work) has already found difficult to operationalize on crypto data.
