## Strategy-selection heuristic: prefer low-capacity/niche strategies (Chan Ch.8)

- **Capacity, defined**: "the amount of equity a strategy can generate good returns on" (Chan Ch.2, recapped Ch.8). Chan's central, book-titular claim: it is far easier to generate a high Sharpe ratio trading a $100,000 account than a $100 million account — many simple, profitable, LOW-capacity strategies exist that are "totally unsuitable to hedge funds," and this low-capacity space is explicitly identified as **"the niche for independent traders like us."** *Project relevance*: this is a direct, actionable framing for how this project should prioritize crypto strategy candidates — small notional, low institutional interest is a structural advantage, not merely an access limitation to route around.
- **The mechanism, as Chan explains it**: most low-capacity profitable strategies act as **market makers**, supplying short-term liquidity and taking quick profits when the liquidity need disappears. A trader managing billions instead becomes the party **demanding** liquidity and must pay for it, which forces long holding periods — and long holding periods expose the portfolio to macro regime shifts that can cause severe drawdowns even when the underlying model is sound. This ties directly to §2 below (Chan's HFT law-of-large-numbers argument), since high-frequency/low-capacity strategies are the clearest example of the liquidity-supplying side of this mechanism.
- **Cascade of institutional structural disadvantages Chan lists explicitly** (large-capacity strategies specifically): intense inter-fund competition erodes shared-strategy profitability; lowered returns pressure managers to overleverage to hit targets; competitive pressure pushes toward complexity that "invites data-snooping bias"; crowded/correlated positioning sets up contagion-driven stampedes when the market environment changes (mechanically the same financial-contagion dynamic documented in Chan's Ch.6 material elsewhere in this knowledge base).
- **Institutional non-quantitative-constraint costs**: "every institutional constraint imposed on a trading strategy tends to decrease its returns, if not its Sharpe ratio as well" — Chan's explicit framing that any added optimization constraint can only decrease (never increase) the objective, invoking the general mathematical principle that constraining an optimization problem cannot improve its optimum. Independent traders are free of such externally-imposed constraints, provided they can maintain the same quantitative discipline themselves.
- **"Next steps for growth" once a strategy's realized capacity is exhausted** (Chan's own framework, not merely a formula-absence — see the note below): (1) **higher-frequency strategies** — requires better infrastructure and often-expensive high-frequency data; (2) **longer-holding-period, lower-Sharpe strategies** that "enormously improve capacity" — often requires expensive fundamental data; (3) **branch into new asset classes** (typically higher capacity than equities) — via collaboration/consultants if the trader lacks expertise there; (4) **further automation**, so daily manual intervention is needed only for genuine exceptions.
- **Note on what was already captured vs. what was missing**: this knowledge base's §6 below (Chan's own open questions) already correctly notes "no general formula or worked numeric example is given for calculating a strategy's capacity" — that captures only the *absence of a formula*. The qualitative concept (capacity as the mechanism explaining independent-trader success, the liquidity-supplier-vs-demander framing, the institutional-disadvantage cascade, and the four-avenue growth framework) is fully explained by Chan without a formula, and is captured here as the missing piece (Chan Ch.8, `02_Chapter_08_Conclusion.md`).

## 1. Regime and noise diagnostics

- **Efficiency Ratio (ER) as a regime classifier** — Kaufman's net-move/sum-of-absolute-moves noise measure (Ch.1) is a simple, computable filter for "trend-favorable" (high ER) vs. "mean-reversion-favorable" (low ER) markets. *Project inference*: test as an orthogonal regime signal alongside existing vol-target sizing (Kaufman Research_Ideas #1).
- **MESA-style "cycle presence" as a binary trend/sideways filter, not a cycle-trading signal** — Ehlers' "lateral shift in thinking" (Kaufman Ch.11): a market exhibiting a clean short-term cycle is, per Ehlers, definitionally not trending. Use presence/absence of a clean cycle (via Hilbert Transform phase-angle sawtooth uniformity) as a regime gate rather than trying to trade the cycle's phase directly (Kaufman Research_Ideas #35).
- **Fisher Transform as a sharper-turning-point oscillator** — claimed by the author to produce less-laggy turning points than typical momentum indicators, with best signals just after an extreme reading rather than at a zero-crossing (Kaufman Ch.11, #36).
- **Relative-volatility (RV) ratio as a regime-detection feature** — short-period volatility ÷ lagged longer-period volatility (nonoverlapping windows), distinct from a lagged volatility-band approach, as a regime-classification input (Kaufman Ch.20, #78).
- **Lengthen-calculation-period-first, then switch to mean-reversion escalation rule** — Kaufman's explicit decision procedure: before pursuing mean-reversion, first exhaust longer-calculation-period trend variants of the existing trend approach (Kaufman Ch.20, #79). *Directly informed this project's own sequencing given its repeated mean-reversion failures.*
- **Regime-switching via data-mining turning-point detection, not classic Markov models** — Chan is explicitly skeptical of constant-transition-probability (hidden) Markov regime models as "generally useless for actual trading" (his opinion) because they can't say *when* a transition probability spikes. More favorable toward a data-mining approach: feed many candidate predictive variables into a backward-looking-only ML search (perceptron, GA, k-NN). **Explicit caveat Chan raises himself**: even with strict backward-looking optimization, "model-category shopping" reintroduces data-snooping bias — "unavoidable whenever we are in the business of backtesting" (Chan Ch.7, Research_Ideas #4).
- **Parameterless / continuously-reoptimized ("moving-window") models** — rather than fixing parameters once, continuously re-optimize within a moving historical window so no single researcher-chosen parameter set is ever "baked in." Chan states this "minimizes overfitting/data-snooping danger," but discloses he "rarely traded such models" himself due to computational demands (Chan Ch.3 sidebar, Ch.7 Example 7.1, Research_Ideas #3). A related idea: **parameter averaging** — divide capital across multiple parameter values/condition sets simultaneously rather than picking one "best" set, which Chan states further stabilizes actual vs. backtested performance.

## 2. Testing-methodology improvements

- **Kurtosis-based overfitting screen** — Kaufman states daily-return kurtosis above 7-8 signals likely overfitting (too many similarly-sized wins clustered), and kurtosis should normally sit below the normal-distribution value of 3 for a genuinely trending, profitable system (Kaufman Ch.2, #4). Extended in Ch.21: whenever a new filter/rule is proposed, compute the kurtosis of the performance-vs-parameter distribution before/after the change; kurtosis rising above ~6 indicates the change is fitting a specific case rather than improving generally, and should be rejected even if the single headline number improves (Kaufman Ch.21, #88).
- **"Average of all tests, not the peak test" as the default reporting convention** — Kaufman's single most-repeated practical conclusion: the average return across a representative region of parameter space, not the single best-scoring combination, is the realistic forward estimate; for drawdown specifically, report the neighborhood *maximum*, not the average, per Kaufman's stated asymmetric rule (Kaufman Ch.21, #87). Pardo independently reaches the identical conclusion via **neighbor-averaging/smoothing of objective functions** across nearby parameter sets, to systematically de-emphasize isolated "profit spikes" likely to be noise/overfitting (Pardo Ch.10, Research_Ideas #4).
- **Full parameter-sweep reporting as a standard validator output** — report the fraction of the swept parameter range that is profitable, alongside total/net profit, as two independent robustness signals; a strategy profitable at only one narrow parameter value should be treated with more suspicion than one profitable across a broad range (Kaufman Ch.5, #12).
- **Parameter-grid shape as a hidden bias in any optimization sweep** — equally-spaced smoothing-constant grids over-sample short-effective-length trends; equally-spaced day-count grids imply a roughly logarithmic smoothing-constant distribution. Audit existing parameter sweeps for this exact bias (Kaufman Ch.7, #18).
- **Walk-forward parameter-stability diagnostic** — if the "best" selected parameter set oscillates erratically between short/long or high/low across consecutive walk-forward windows, the in-sample window is too short. Report the variance/range of the selected parameter across windows as an explicit correctness gate (Kaufman Ch.21, #86).
- **Walk-forward "feedback" audit** — re-running the full step-forward process more than once means the "out-of-sample" portion has actually been seen during development, invalidating the scientific-testing claim. Any window re-used across multiple walk-forward reruns should be treated as compromised (Kaufman Ch.21, #84).
- **"Test across a wide range of markets, expect suboptimal-but-consistent results"** — Kaufman's "loose pants fit everyone" principle: a strategy profitable on one market/asset but unprofitable on a second liquid one under an *identical, unmodified* parameter set is fine-tuned, not robust (Kaufman Ch.21, #90).
- **Price-shock P&L decomposition** — isolate and report the fraction of a strategy's total profit attributable to days where True Range exceeds some multiple of lagged average True Range; if more than roughly half of a candidate's profitable trades are shock-driven, treat this as a red flag independent of any other statistic — diagnostic of both overstated return and understated risk (Kaufman Ch.21, #89; reinforced Ch.22 reserve-capital sizing, #95).
- **Cumulative-trials multiple-comparisons arithmetic** — Kaufman's worked "1,000 tests over 10 years could yield ~50 profitable-by-chance results" is independent, plain-language corroboration for tracking a running trial count across all candidate strategies, not evaluating each Sharpe in isolation (Kaufman Ch.21, #92).
- **Data-integrity pre-flight checklist** — outlier (>4% single-bar), gap (>4 consecutive days), and open/close-outside-high-low-range checks as an automated pre-backtest gate (Kaufman Ch.22, #93). Paired with an explicit "develop on dirty (unfiltered) data" recommendation so a strategy is naturally desensitized to bad ticks (Kaufman Ch.22, #94).
- **Cost-as-%-of-range pre-screening for candidate assets/timeframes** — replicate the book's avg-price/avg-daily-range/%-range/$-range table style analysis across candidate crypto assets and timeframes to rank them by transaction-cost efficiency *before* committing further research effort — a cheap, immediately actionable first step for any short-horizon crypto work (Kaufman Ch.16, #57).
- **Combinatorial-sample-size audit for pattern/regime features** — report per-combination case counts, and prefer count-based features (e.g., "N of the last 20 days up") over exact-sequence-match features, as a direct audit checklist item for any existing or future short-term pattern feature in validator.py (Kaufman Ch.15, #54).
- **Explicit caution against over-generalizing single-worked-example findings** — several of the book's own most interesting intraday findings (tick-bar-vs-fixed-bar comparison, intraday-volatility U-shape reversion) are presented by the author as single illustrative examples, not broad statistical claims; any crypto adaptation should be tested across a genuine multi-asset, multi-period sample before being treated as validated, consistent with this project's existing DSR-gated discipline (Kaufman Ch.16, #61).
- **Live chi-square monitoring** — compare realized win rate against backtested expectation using a chi-square test (with Yates correction for small samples) as an ongoing live-monitoring check, rather than relying on subjective judgment about whether a losing streak is "normal" (Kaufman Ch.23, #105).
- **Sample-size rule of thumb for new-idea screening** — Chan's heuristic: data points needed ≈ 252 × (number of free parameters). A promising idea should be checked against this before serious development. **Explicit trap flagged**: using high-frequency data to satisfy the raw *point-count* of this rule while the model operates at daily frequency does not satisfy the *effective* sample-size requirement, since within-day observations of a daily signal are highly non-independent (Chan's own numeric example: 1 year of minute data ≈ only ~147 effective daily-model data points) (Chan Ch.3, Research_Ideas A).
- **The 5-parameter ceiling** — a rule of thumb, not a proven optimum, for the maximum number of parameters a strategy should employ at the idea-generation stage (Chan Ch.3, B). Pardo's degrees-of-freedom framework gives the same conclusion a formal footing: keep ≥90% of degrees of freedom remaining after all rules/indicators/startup overhead are deducted (Pardo Ch.13, "Insufficient degrees of freedom").
- **Qualitative (non-numeric) data-snooping is easy to overlook** — repeatedly trying different *qualitative* choices against the same historical data (enter at open vs. close, hold overnight or not, large-cap vs. mid-cap universe) is equally capable of overfitting as numeric parameter-fitting, even though none of these choices is a literal "parameter." Each such iteration should count as a "trial" for cumulative multiple-comparisons accounting (Chan Ch.3, C).
- **Treat the verification window between publication and your own test as genuine out-of-sample** — if backtesting a published strategy purely to replicate (not further optimize) it, the elapsed time since publication is a genuine OOS period, "equivalent to paper trading in rigor" (Chan Ch.3, D).
- **Refinements must improve BOTH training and test sets, never test alone** — a refinement improving only the test set is an explicit red flag that the test set has effectively become a second training set (Chan Ch.2/3/7, E).
- **Degrees-of-freedom-aware start-up-overhead bias** — a strategy's longest lookback disproportionately burdens longer parameter candidates in a scan, since it loses more of an already-small sample AND trades less often — potentially skewing optimization toward shorter parameters for reasons unrelated to genuine edge (Pardo Ch.13, worked example).
- **Overscanning's hidden harm beyond wasted compute** — an over-fine parameter step size pads the tested population with practically-identical (non-independent) results, artificially inflating the apparent profitable-percentage and depressing apparent standard deviation — a misleadingly robust-looking statistical profile (Pardo Ch.13, worked before/after comparison).
- **"Big Fish in a Small Pond Syndrome"** — an apparently robust strategy whose small trade sample is dominated by one or two very large, long-duration winning trades should be rejected as overfit/unsound *unless* it (and neighboring parameter sets) passes a comprehensive Walk-Forward Analysis across a diversified market basket (Pardo Ch.13).
- **Reconcile competing walk-forward-window-ratio heuristics before implementing either** — see `17_author_disagreements.md` for Pardo's own internal Ch.6-vs-Ch.11 inconsistency (1/8-1/3 vs. 25-35%).
- **Formal overfitting-quantification methods are cited but never implemented anywhere in the Hilpisch book** — the book cites Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2015) on "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting" as background but shows no code; flagged as an explicit gap this project's own DSR gate already fills more rigorously (Hilpisch Ch.4-5, Research_Ideas #5).
- **Walk-forward / cross-validation for time series is never used anywhere in Hilpisch** — only a single fixed in-sample/out-of-sample split appears throughout the book, despite its strong emphasis on avoiding overfitting (Hilpisch, Research_Ideas #6).

## 3. Sizing and risk ideas

- **Turtles' volatility-normalized, correlation-capped position sizing** — equalize dollar-volatility exposure (2×ATR measure) across all traded markets, with tiered position caps for correlated groups and a formulaic drawdown throttle (cut size 20% per 10% drawdown, restore 10% per 6⅔% recovery) (Kaufman Ch.5, #11).
- **Kase-style tiered (3-level) volatility-scaled stop-outs** — scale out 1/3 of a position at each of three ATR-derived stop levels, rather than exiting the full position at a single stop (Kaufman Ch.18, #68).
- **Volatility-target rebalancing with a threshold-based Volatility Factor (VF)** — Kaufman's worked macrotrend case study shows a stated 64% AROR improvement (8.7%→14.3%) purely from applying a 20%-threshold rolling VF rebalancing scheme at *equal* realized risk — a structurally different lever than simply picking a higher or lower static vol target (Kaufman Ch.24, #109, #112).
- **GASP's semivariance-of-drawdowns (SDD) objective function** as an alternative to Sharpe/Sortino for multi-strategy or multi-asset portfolio work, since standard mean-variance methods systematically misjudge systems that are flat part of the time (Kaufman Ch.24, #110).
- **Risk-of-ruin calculation as an independent capitalization sanity check** — using a strategy's own historical win rate/average win/average loss, compute risk-of-ruin via Vince's spreadsheet-form method at the actual capitalization level (Kaufman Ch.23, #102).
- **Counterintuitive "tighter max-risk threshold can INCREASE risk of ruin"** — before assuming a tightened stop-loss or max-drawdown cap is unambiguously safer, explicitly verify via the risk-of-ruin formula that it doesn't interact adversely with an already-thin edge (Kaufman Ch.23, Table 23.15 Case 6, #103).
- **Kelly/Optimal f as an upper-bound sanity check, not a sizing target** — compute Kelly-optimal f purely as a ceiling, confirming actual position sizing sits comfortably below it, consistent with Elder's "never trade full optimal f" and Markowitz's "use leverage-optimization only to reduce, not increase leverage" (Kaufman Ch.23, #104; see `17_author_disagreements.md` for the cross-book Kelly framing comparison).
- **Theory-of-Runs-informed entry-timing filter** — an RSI-filtered entry after an MA trend signal improved profit factor/win-rate/average-trade-size across every tested market in Kaufman's own study, including his own expected worst candidates (Apple, Amazon) — at the cost of fewer total trades (Kaufman Ch.23, #106).
- **The Commodity Selection Index (CSI)** as a market/asset-ranking overlay for a future multi-asset portfolio, combining ADXR, ATR, and cost/margin terms into a single rankable score by directional-movement quality per dollar invested (Kaufman Ch.23, #107).
- **Full Kelly formula (F* = C⁻¹M) and half-Kelly as an automatic, non-discretionary sizing discipline** — reduces size after losses, increases after gains, replacing emotionally-driven sizing with a formula-driven one (Chan Ch.6). See `17_author_disagreements.md` for how this compares across the three books that treat Kelly/optimal f (Vince, Chan, Kaufman).
- **Fat-tail-adjusted leverage cap** — MIN(half-Kelly, max-tolerable-drawdown ÷ historical-worst-one-period-loss), specifically to guard against the Gaussian approximation's known fat-tail failure (Chan Ch.6).
- **Low-beta-leveraged vs. high-beta-unleveraged portfolios** — Chan's stated conclusion that "the market is chronically underpricing high-beta stocks," so a leveraged low-beta portfolio should be preferred over an unleveraged high-beta portfolio of the same target beta/return, since long-term compounded growth scales with Sharpe² (via the Kelly-formula derivation), not average return. **Caveat Chan restates himself**: this depends on the Gaussian-return assumption; given real fat tails, one should be "quite wary of using too much leverage on normally low-beta stocks" (Chan Ch.7, #8).
- **Parabolic interpolation search for optimal f** — cited by Vince as "one of the fastest ways" but its mechanics are never given in this book; readers are referred to the predecessor *Portfolio Management Formulas*, not available to this extraction — a genuine, acknowledged gap (Vince Ch.1/3/4/5).
- **"What if" scenario modeling (Stretch/Shrink parameters, LOC/SCALE/SKEW/KURT perturbation)** as Vince's closest approach to Monte Carlo-style simulation — answering questions like "what happens to my optimal f if this system's edge shrinks to half its current level with 60% more dispersion?" **Explicit misuse warning**: it is easy to artificially flip a genuinely losing system's average trade to positive via a negative Shrink multiplier if the system's TRUE future expectation actually remains negative (Vince Ch.3-4).
- **Scenario Planning as a generalized decision framework** — enumerate a comprehensive set of future scenarios (recommended to cover ~99% of the plausible outcome spectrum, explicitly not just 3 "optimistic/pessimistic/unchanged" buckets, criticized as too crude), assign probabilities summing to 1, apply the same geometric-mean-maximization machinery. Vince generalizes this beyond trading (military strategy, underwriting, mortgage decisions) (Vince Ch.4).
- **Kelly criterion for capital/leverage allocation in a live automated system** — full Kelly produces high volatility; practitioners typically use half (Hilpisch Ch.10).
- **Advanced non-Gaussian-aware position-sizing mathematics** — Pardo flags that classical formulas (Kelly, Optimal f) are undermined by fat-tailed, non-Gaussian returns, and states more sophisticated approaches exist but are "beyond the scope of this book" — an explicit pointer to unexplored territory (Pardo Ch.5, Research_Ideas #9).
- **Robust statistics** as a distinct, newer statistical sub-field specifically relevant to small-sample trading performance measures — named but not elaborated by Pardo (Pardo Ch.12, #10).
- **Mandelbrot's fractal-distribution framework** as a serious, credible challenge to standard Gaussian-based risk statistics, including maximum drawdown estimation itself — Pardo states exploiting this "requires the use of some very sophisticated math" not covered in his book, and directs readers to Mandelbrot & Hudson's *The (Mis)Behavior of Markets* (Pardo Ch.12, #11).

## 4. Data-source ideas

- **CME BTC/ETH futures COT data** — a genuinely novel, non-blocked crypto data source (regulated CME crypto futures carry CFTC COT reporting even though spot/offshore-perp crypto does not) (Kaufman Ch.14, #49; see `15_crypto_specific.md` §(d)).
- **Crypto Fear & Greed Index** as a freely available sentiment-filter substitute, explicitly flagged against this project's prior blocked sentiment-data pivot, with the caveat that this specific source (simple, free, single-number daily) may sidestep the earlier access blocker (Kaufman Ch.14, #50).
- **Crypto perpetual-futures open interest** (Binance, Bybit, OKX) as a smoother substitute input for existing trend/momentum indicators, mirroring the book's own futures-OI-as-volume-substitute use case (Kaufman Ch.12, #41).
- **Deribit BTC/ETH options-implied volatility vs. realized volatility** as a crypto substitute for the VIX/HV framework — a genuinely novel angle distinct from this project's prior blocked funding-rate/basis pivots (Kaufman Ch.20, #106 cross-reference in Crypto_Specific.md).
- **Unstructured news/social-media data (NLP) for trading signals** — the Eikon Data API's news-headline retrieval capability (Hilpisch Ch.3) is demonstrated purely as data retrieval, explicitly never integrated into any trading strategy — an area the author flags as accessible via the shown tooling but leaves entirely unexplored as an alpha source (Hilpisch Research_Ideas #1).
- **Tick-bar / volume-bar construction as an alternative to this project's existing fixed-time OHLCV pipeline** — test whether accumulating crypto trade data into tick-count or volume-threshold bars (rather than fixed 1m/5m/1h candles) reduces false trend signals, per the book's own single (non-generalized) worked FX example — worth a low-effort comparative test given that crypto genuinely provides trade-level tick data (Kaufman Ch.16, #58).

## 5. Indicator combinations worth testing

- **Combining a slow trend filter with a fast oscillator for entry timing** — use a long trend purely for direction and a much faster, separately-tuned oscillator purely for entry timing on pullbacks within that trend, explicitly NOT as a standalone signal (Kaufman Ch.9, Raschke's First Cross pattern, #30; reinforced Ch.19 via Elder's Screen 2, #73).
- **Trend-conditional oscillator thresholds** — rather than fixed thresholds (e.g., RSI<30), use a trend-conditional threshold (e.g., RSI<50 during an established uptrend) for entry timing within that trend (Kaufman Ch.19, #73).
- **Momentum divergence as a defensive trend-exhaustion overlay, not a standalone countertrend signal** — used to tighten stops or reduce size on an existing trend position when price makes a new high/low unconfirmed by the underlying oscillator, rather than traded outright (Kaufman Ch.9, #28).
- **2-day (or n-day-overlapping) RSI** — replacing 1-day price differences with n-day-overlapping differences increases sensitivity while retaining the same data length; the book cites a finding that standard RSI's regime flipped from trend-confirming (pre-1998) to mean-reverting (post-1998) on the S&P (Kaufman Ch.9, #27).
- **KAMA / ER-adaptive smoothing as a replacement for fixed-period trend components** — with the chapter's own mandatory whipsaw-filter requirement (a fixed-move-away-from-extreme filter, not a volatility band, per the book's own finding) as a concrete implementation detail, not an optional refinement (Kaufman Ch.17, #62-63).
- **Multi-timeframe (Triple-Screen-style) decomposition** — a higher (e.g., weekly-resampled) frequency for major-trend direction, a lower frequency for entry timing, gated so timing signals only count when they agree with the higher-frequency trend (Kaufman Ch.19, #72).
- **KST's weighted-multi-lookback-composite construction** as a generic ensemble technique for building a smoother, better-phased trend/momentum feature from several different lookback lengths of the same underlying signal (Kaufman Ch.19, #75).
- **Kurtosis-Skew regime-based entry/exit logic** — buying/selling on an excess-kurtosis zero-cross combined with skew direction and a minimum-volatility filter; the source's own printed mean-reversion sell rule contains an internal inconsistency (see `17_author_disagreements.md`), so any implementation should use the corrected/mirrored rule, not the verbatim-as-printed one (Kaufman Ch.18, #70).
- **Price-shock-reversal as a directly testable, mechanically simple strategy candidate** — a True-Range-vs-lagged-ATR shock ratio >3.0 tends to be followed by next-day reversal across bonds/crude/S&P over a 28-year test (Kaufman Ch.14, #47).
- **Volume-spike mean-reversion and its volatility-proxy alternative** — the "Pseudo-Volume Strategy" substitutes price-volatility conditions for a volume spike (avoiding volume-data-quality problems entirely) — flagged as the more promising of the two for crypto specifically because it sidesteps this project's standing volume-reliability concern (Kaufman Ch.12, #39-40).
- **UTC-daily-candle-boundary and funding-settlement time-of-day study, as a crypto-native analog to intraday time-of-day findings** — bucket bars by time-since-open and measure win-rate/average net move per bucket, substituting UTC 00:00 (common daily-candle boundary) and 8-hour perpetual-funding settlement times for the book's exchange-session open/close. *Caveat*: crypto has no true "session," so the boundary choice is itself an assumption to test, not a given (Kaufman Ch.15, #52).
- **Gap-fade / mean-reversion-after-gap-down, reframed via a UTC-daily-open "gap" proxy** — the book's striking 98-100% cross-market tendency for downward opening gaps to recover above the prior close intraday is a strong, simple, well-specified pattern, worth testing on crypto using the UTC daily open vs. prior UTC close as the "gap" — though a weaker analog than a true exchange-session gap, since crypto's continuous trading produces no overnight order backlog to generate the discontinuity (Kaufman Ch.15, #53).
- **Opening-range-breakout (ORB) systems (1st Hour Breakout, N-Bar Breakout, Fisher's ORB, Crabel's volatility-calibrated ORB) as directly portable day-trading mechanics for BTC/ETH** — a fully worked-out breakout toolkit (threshold construction, confirmation timing, trend/compression filters) that the book's Ch.15 material only sketched conceptually; crypto has no true "session open," so any ORB test must substitute a UTC daily-candle boundary or exchange-specific reference time, as already flagged for the time-of-day/gap-fade ideas above (Kaufman Ch.16, #59).
- **Intraday price-shock fade as a higher-frequency companion to the daily-scale price-shock-reversal idea above** — the same True-Range-vs-lagged-average shock-ratio logic (see the price-shock-reversal bullet above, #47), applied at intraday bar resolution (e.g., 5-min or 15-min BTC/ETH bars) rather than daily, as a distinct, faster-turnover companion strategy — would require crypto tick/intraday data infrastructure this project may not yet have built out (Kaufman Ch.16, #60).
- **Day-of-month institutional-flow patterns flagged as a poor crypto fit** — the book's day-of-month findings are attributed specifically to retirement-account contribution schedules, month-end accounting closes, and foreign-currency repatriation, mechanisms without a clear crypto-native equivalent; recorded for completeness, not recommended as a near-term research direction absent a specific crypto-native institutional-flow mechanism to substitute (Kaufman Ch.15, #56).
- **Regime-switching via cross-sectional PCA factor models** — Chan's own worked S&P 600 small-cap PCA example FAILED (−1.81% average annualized return), presented explicitly as a negative result showing the idea is plausible in principle but did not work in his own test — a caution, not a recommendation (Chan Ch.7 Example 7.4, Research_Ideas #7).
- **Cross-sectional momentum and pair/basket/multi-instrument strategies** — both explicitly named by Hilpisch as out of scope for his book, flagging them as natural extensions beyond its single-instrument focus (Hilpisch Ch.1, Ch.4).
- **Feature importance/selection for the ML strategies** — despite growing to 36 features (6×6 lags) in Hilpisch's Ch.10 AdaBoost strategy, no feature-importance analysis or dimensionality reduction is demonstrated anywhere — an implicit overfitting-risk gap (Hilpisch Research_Ideas #4).
- **Descriptor/trade-profile (Aronson) labeling as a candidate ML-label-generation method for a future classifier-based crypto signal** — define a target profile (e.g., ≥X% move within Y days, max interim drawdown Z%) on BTC/ETH daily data, label all qualifying historical windows, then test whether existing project indicators (trend, momentum, volatility) concentrate meaningfully at the labeled points — a different research paradigm from this project's existing backtest-driven validator.py approach, worth flagging as a candidate future direction rather than an immediate priority given the project's already-established DSR-gated pipeline (Kaufman Ch.15, #55).
- **Recurrent/convolutional deep-learning architectures for return sequences** — Hilpisch's DNN work uses only feed-forward Dense layers; LSTM/GRU/convolutional architectures, naturally suited to time-ordered data, are never explored, an omission the author doesn't even flag himself (Hilpisch Research_Ideas #10).

## 6. Author's own open questions (from each book's Master_Summary.md)

**Kaufman** — (1) the drawdown-response dilemma (de-risk vs. hold size after a drawdown) is left explicitly unresolved, called "a difficult business decision" with only the meta-rule that a plan must exist in advance; (2) no universal best trend method or calculation period exists — the Ch.8 six-system study deliberately declines to crown a winner; (3) several named systems' exact numeric parameters are unrecoverable from the source (the Turtles' exact loss-threshold multiple and position caps, Raschke's First Cross best-fit parameters, Kestner's ADX-filter thresholds, the Rule of Seven's constants); (4) whether short-interval portfolio allocation can beat long-interval allocation is explicitly flagged as unproven either way ("No one has yet shown..."); (5) the book's Bernoulli-utility risk-preference framing is presented without a prescriptive resolution — risk appetite is treated as a legitimate personal trait.

**Chan** — (1) no numeric threshold is given for "significant" test-set performance degradation in the strategy-simplification procedure; (2) no general formula or worked numeric example is given for calculating a strategy's "capacity" (Ch.2/8's central concept) despite how load-bearing the concept is; (3) no fixed minimum paper-trading duration is prescribed beyond "a month or longer" in one specific context; (4) no diagnostic is given for real-time self-recognition of despair or greed — both illustrated narratively, not via checklist; (5) the retail Reg-T leverage-rescaling formula's treatment of partially offsetting/hedged positions is explicitly acknowledged as an approximation with no refined formula supplied; (6) no fixed re-estimation window is prescribed for the fat-tail worst-case-loss cap; (7) no worked example is given applying any of the book's techniques to any non-equity/futures/forex asset, crypto included.

**Hilpisch** — (1) why the Ch.10 live deployment uses a materially shorter bar length than the backtested/trained model, never addressed; (2) mean-reversion strategy thresholds (3.5 for GDX, 7.5 for GLD) appear hand-picked with no stated calibration procedure; (3) the vectorized SMA strategy is always fully invested while the event-based long-only version can be neutral — materially different strategies despite sharing "the same" crossover logic, not reconciled; (4) lag-count choices (3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 15) across ML examples are never justified via any stated selection procedure; (5) cross-sectional momentum and pair/basket trading are stated out of scope with no explanation beyond keeping the book "concise."

**Pardo** — (1) whether a systematic strategy could ever truly match/exceed the best discretionary traders — argued to be "a matter of degree, not kind" via the chess-computer analogy (chess computers were once thought unable to beat grandmasters until Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997, Pardo Ch.2), but explicitly stated as "not been done yet" to Pardo's knowledge; (2) the unreconciled walk-forward-window-ratio inconsistency (see `17_author_disagreements.md`); (3) whether "RSIct" (Ch.8) and "RSI CT" (Ch.11) are the same underlying system, not cross-referenced in the source; (4) an unreconciled discrepancy between two reported WFA totals for the RSI CT case study ($168,113 vs. $154,350), likely explained by a −$19,625 open-position adjustment the source never walks through explicitly; (5) whether markets are best modeled as one continuously-evolving entity, a series of discrete regime shifts, or an "endless succession of ever different minimarkets" — multiple views presented without resolution.

**Vince** — (1) the parabolic interpolation search algorithm is referenced repeatedly but never given (see §3 above); (2) classical closed-form risk-of-ruin equations are discussed conceptually but never actually derived in the main text — only Vince's own "risk of ruin = 1 for unlimited liability + infinite horizon" argument is developed; (3) several internal numerical inconsistencies are preserved as found (Ch.5's probabilities summing to 1.30 rather than 1.00; a Ch.8 margin-constraint table discrepancy; Appendix B's Bernoulli variance formula printed as `Q=P-1`; Appendix C's ambiguous exponent term); (4) the exact grid-search increments/ranges for fitting the adjustable distribution are given only for the one worked 232-trade example, not generalized into a formal algorithm; (5) no rigorous decision procedure is given for choosing between the multiple available optimal-f methodologies beyond an informal closing ranking; (6) no formal re-estimation-frequency framework is given for how often to re-fit optimal f, correlations, or distribution parameters in live trading.

## 7. Methodology this project should treat as settled cross-book convergence

Several ideas above are not single-book suggestions but converge independently across multiple books, which strengthens rather than merely adds to the case for adopting them:

- **Percentage/ATR/volatility-relative thresholds over fixed-dollar/fixed-point thresholds** — converges across Kaufman Ch.4, Ch.5 (swing filters, box-size dilemma), and Ch.9 (bounded oscillators vs. fixed-band momentum) as three independent chapters reaching the same design rule.
- **Robustness-over-peak-optimization, average-of-neighborhood over single-best-parameter** — converges across Kaufman Ch.21 (#87), Pardo's entire Walk-Forward Analysis/robust-optimization-profile apparatus, and Chan's moving-window re-optimization preference (Ch.3/7). See `17_author_disagreements.md` for where these three converging methodologies nonetheless differ in *how* they operationalize the same underlying principle.
- **"Prove the feature helps" against a genuine holdout, not just an in-sample improvement** — Kaufman Ch.8 (#24), Chan's rule E above (refinements must improve BOTH training and test sets), and Pardo's whole WFA apparatus all independently arrive at requiring an out-of-sample bar before accepting any strategy modification.
