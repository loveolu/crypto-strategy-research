## 1. Pardo's Eight-Stage Trading Strategy Development Process

Pardo frames strategy development as an explicitly iterative, feedback-driven cycle (not a linear pipeline), modeled on the scientific method: formulate a hypothesis about market behavior → translate into testable/computable form → test empirically → accept (trade it) or reject/refine based on results → repeat (Pardo Ch.3). This is contrasted with the "Path of Empirical Development" (neural nets, data mining, genetic programming search a large indicator/rule space, sometimes without the researcher's own reasoning driving the design) — Pardo's stated concerns about the empirical path (his own opinions, not universal facts): it easily devolves into "sophisticated overfitting"; resulting logic is often an opaque "black box" most professional traders (including himself) are reluctant to trade; and validating an empirically-derived strategy via Walk-Forward Analysis can be far more labor/compute-intensive. His conclusion: any empirically-derived strategy must still pass through the full testing/monitoring pipeline below, and rigorous WFA is "absolutely essential" for such strategies' final validation (Pardo Ch.3).

### The Eight Stages (Pardo Ch.3, Figure 3.1 — flowchart with feedback/rejection loops at every stage)

1. **Conceptualize/formulate the trading strategy.** Warning: "incomplete specification of rules is one of the most common and dangerous mistakes," especially for traders new to systematic trading. "If the trading idea cannot be reduced to such a form [a set of precise rules and formulae], then it is not a systematic trading strategy."
2. **Specify the trading rules in a definitive, objective, computer-testable form.** Translation into a scripting/programming language; an inaccurate translation produces an inaccurate simulation that compromises the entire downstream pipeline (full treatment: Pardo Ch.7).
3. **Preliminary testing** — two goals: (a) confirm the scripted strategy behaves as conceived (debug against intended logic); (b) get a rough profit/risk sense before committing further. Not exhaustive/definitive. "Every test does not have to be a winner; if every test is a loser... you should probably abandon the strategy" (full treatment: Pardo Ch.8, §2 below).
4. **Optimize the trading strategy** — arrive at the most robust, highest risk-adjusted-return parameter formulation. Rule of thumb: "a very good test would show anywhere from a 50 to 100 percent overall improvement in performance" versus the preliminary-test round, particularly in profit increase and drawdown decrease (full treatment: Pardo Ch.9–10, §3–4 below).
5. **Evaluate robustness via Walk-Forward Analysis** — the "go/no-go" stage, called "the most critical stage." WFA judges performance "strictly and solely on the basis of postoptimization or out-of-sample trading" and answers three questions: will the model make money post-optimization? At what rate? How will trend/volatility/liquidity changes affect performance? (full treatment: Pardo Ch.11, §5 below).
6. **Trade the strategy in real time.** Described as "the easy part, relatively speaking" — yet ironically where most traders fail. Larry Williams (quoted repeatedly across the book): "Trading strategies work. System traders do not." Causes of failure: insufficient confidence/understanding of the strategy to endure drawdowns, and lack of self-discipline. "When a trading strategy has been satisfactorily tested and is performing in real-time trading according to expectation, trust it. Stick with it and take every signal."
7. **Monitor real-time trading performance** against the historical performance profile. Common error #1: abandoning a strategy too quickly during losses that are actually within the expected drawdown profile. Less obvious warning: **unusually good performance is also a "deviation" requiring explanation** — "profits beyond expectation can be a warning of a problem to come," since outsized profit often correlates with rising volatility, and rising volatility often precedes larger losses.
8. **Improve and refine the strategy** based on accumulated observation. **Critical rule**: any change/improvement must go through the **entire** development/testing cycle again before being used in real trading — no shortcuts for "small" tweaks.

This same 8-stage skeleton recurs, restated, as the backbone diagram for later chapters (Fig 8.1/8.3–8.5 for Preliminary Testing; Fig 10.1–10.2 for Optimization; Fig 11.1 for WFA) (Pardo Ch.3).

## 2. The Accuracy of the Historical Simulation

Pardo frames simulation accuracy as the foundation everything else rests on: "the entire structure of strategy evaluation is proportionately weakened" if the simulation is inaccurate, "much like a building erected on an unsound foundation" (Pardo Ch.6). Two prerequisites for the "most perfect simulation possible": (1) thorough understanding of software limitations and data issues; (2) conservative assumptions about costs/slippage.

### 2.1 The Essential Reports (Pardo Ch.6)
- **Performance Summary**: net profit, max drawdown, # trades, % profitable, etc., split All/Long/Short. Worked example: strategy "PTIx" on S&P futures, 1989–2006, net profit $338,625, MDD $149,100, 218 trades, 66.9% win rate, profit factor 1.62.
- **Trade List**: entry/exit date, price, signal name, per-trade P/L, running cumulative P/L — useful early as a diagnostic tool, and later (an underappreciated use, per Pardo) as an intuitive "trading simulator" the strategist reviews trade-by-trade to build a felt sense of what real-time trading will actually be like.
- **Equity Curve**: cumulative P/L plotted, typically under a price chart — assesses smoothness/consistency and behavior during specific regimes (esp. drawdown).
- **Performance by Period**: breakdown by interval (commonly annual) — checks consistency/robustness over time; concentration of profit in one or two periods is "cause for concern" (elaborated §6.3 below).

### 2.2 Software Limitations
- **Rounding issues / "tick math"**: markets trade in fixed minimum ticks (soybeans $.25, T-bonds 1/32nds, S&P $.10). Simulation software that fails to round data/order prices to the proper tick can wrongly include/exclude trades near the fill boundary and systematically distort P&L via floating-point imprecision. Worked example: a trade that should show $675 profit (tick-rounded) shows $699.69 unrounded — a ~$25/trade distortion that can flip a marginal strategy (avg trade ~$100) from usable to unusable, compounding to roughly a 10% profit overstatement in some cases. Matters most for fast, small-average-trade strategies (Pardo Ch.6).
- **Phantom trades / tracking errors**: when two contingent orders (an entry stop and its covering risk stop) could both trigger within the same price bar, daily-bar-only data cannot determine which happened first. Simulators typically "guess" based on which extreme is closer to the open — a guess that can be wrong, silently including/excluding trades. Pardo calls these "tracking errors"; the only real fix is a smaller-time-frame parallel dataset (e.g., 5-minute bars) to resolve intraday sequencing (Pardo Ch.6).
- **Price (limit) orders**: not guaranteed to fill even if the limit price is touched. Perry Kaufman is cited (via Pardo's Notes) as estimating **up to 30% of limit orders go unfilled** in real trading. Most simulators naively assume a fill whenever the limit price is touched; Pardo recommends requiring the price be exceeded by a minimum buffer (e.g., 8 ticks) before counting a fill (Pardo Ch.6).

### 2.3 Realistic Assumptions — Slippage and Costs (Pardo Ch.6)
- **Commission**: "the transaction expense charged by a brokerage firm to execute a trade" — fixed, non-controversial.
- **Price slippage**: "the transaction expense charged by poor quality executions... the difference between the order price and the actual execution or fill price." Real cost concentrates in rare, very-large-slippage events during fast/volatile markets — easy to underweight if judged only by "typical" days.
- Order types: market order; stop order (becomes market once touched — subject to price slippage, worsened by "bunched" stops from many participants at similar levels); price-limit order (guaranteed price-or-better, not guaranteed fill — subject to **trade slippage**, i.e., missed-trade cost); Market If Touched (MIT — becomes market once touched, subject to price slippage).
- **Trade slippage**: "the cost of missed trades that are never entered or trades that are exited at a worse price because of price limit orders that are not filled."
- **Opening gap slippage**: overnight close-to-open gaps silently slip GTC stop orders (negative) and limit orders (positive). Not itself recorded as "slippage" by the simulator, but a real monitored cost/benefit: unusual concentration of adverse-gap stop fills flags a design weakness; concentration of favorable-gap limit fills flags a hidden strength.
- **Opening/closing range slippage**: recorded open/close prices are usually the first tick of the day or a settlement/last-30–60-second average; real Market-On-Open/Close/Stop-Close-Only fills typically land near the *worst* price within that range — a systematic, easy-to-overlook cost.
- **Slippage due to size**: larger orders increase slippage, especially for price-limit orders; strategists trading at scale should design for staggered/scaled execution.
- **Significance of slippage — two worked contrasts** (Pardo Ch.6): (a) infrequent, large-average-trade system: 10 trades/4 years, $20,000 gross profit, $125/trade cost → net $18,750 — costs immaterial. (b) high-frequency system: 200 trades/1 year, $10,000 gross profit, same $125/trade cost → **net result flips to a $15,000 LOSS**. Demonstrates transaction costs can be the deciding factor for active strategies.
- **Limit moves**: exchange-set daily price bands (e.g., soybeans ±50¢). A locked-limit day (equal O=H=L=C at the limit) has essentially zero real tradable volume; simulations should NOT allow entries/exits on locked-limit or near-limit days.
- **Major events/black swans**: scheduled reports, rollovers, expirations cause outsized fast moves; Pardo recommends evaluating results **with and without** trades caused by rare, large, non-repeatable moves (example: the October 27, 1997 mini-crash) — enjoy windfalls but don't build expectations on their repeatability; ensure the design survives such events on the loss side.

### 2.4 Historical Data Issues (Pardo Ch.6)
- **Stock prices**: don't expire, but must be dividend- and split-adjusted; some vendors lack accurate opening prices, some even backfill "today's open" with yesterday's close, making the data unusable for open-dependent strategies.
- **Futures markets**: finite-lived contracts create two problems: (1) any single contract's history is too short for long-run testing; (2) volume/volatility/liquidity evolve predictably through a contract's life, necessitating concatenated/adjusted series and, for real trading, rollover of open positions (which creates a rollover gap — usually immaterial but can matter for high-rollover-frequency markets like monthly energy contracts).
- **Continuous / perpetual / adjusted continuous contracts**: a **continuous contract** (concatenated front-month contracts) solves length/fidelity problems but leaves a rollover price gap at each splice that can appear as spurious simulated windfall profit/loss. A **perpetual contract** (mathematically interpolated, not real prices) solves length/rollover problems but (1) contains no real prices, (2) artificially dampens volatility, (3) requires transforming any derived real-time signal price before live use. A **back-adjusted continuous contract** (recent data unchanged, prior data shifted by cumulative roll-gap amounts) preserves point differences but can distort percentage calculations and can even produce **negative prices** far enough back. A **front-adjusted continuous contract** reverses the adjustment direction. Overall conclusion: back-adjusted series solve "all three of the major problems for most systems" but are "not without problems testing some types of trading strategies."

**Cross-book corroboration — Kaufman gives an independently overlapping treatment** (Kaufman Ch.21): back-adjusted futures data is the usual choice for the same length/fidelity/rollover reasons; old back-adjusted data commonly goes negative, so **price differences, not percentage returns, should be used** for most calculations on such data. Kaufman adds a specific worked backward-adjustment procedure (shift each older contract segment by the price difference between new/old contract on the roll date, cumulative across segments) and recommends **ATR as a volatility substitute** on back-adjusted data since annualized volatility built on daily % returns is unreliable there (percentages near a zero-crossing in adjusted data can spuriously blow up). Kaufman also separately flags **duplication risk** from overlapping individual-contract date ranges when *not* using a concatenated series — careless date selection across overlapping contracts can double-count a windfall profit/loss (Kaufman Ch.21).

**Chan's parallel treatment on the equity side** (Chan Ch.3): split adjustment = multiply pre-split prices by 1/N for an N:1 split; dividend adjustment = multiply pre-ex-date prices by `(Close(T-1) − d)/Close(T-1)` — adjustment via **multiplier**, not subtraction, so historical daily *returns* (not just price changes) remain unchanged, matching the Yahoo! Finance convention. Unadjusted data shows a spurious "drop" at the ex-date open that can trigger erroneous signals. Worked Example 3.2 (IGE ETF) verifies a cumulative-multiplier calculation against Yahoo!'s published adjusted closes to two decimal places.

### 2.5 The Multimarket/Multiperiod Test, Worked (Pardo Ch.8)
Pardo's own preliminary-testing chapter (Ch.8, distinct from the optimization-stage multimarket/multiperiod worked example in §5.6 below) gives the clearest concrete anchor in the book for the abstract "excellent/deplorable/mixed" decision framework used throughout this file. Worked example: **5 markets** (coffee, crude oil, S&P, T-bonds, yen) × **5 two-year segments** (1997–2006) × one fixed parameter set = **25 simulations**, run for three example strategies:
- **MA 5×20 crossover**: total across all 25 cells = **+$66,301**, but with large losses in individual cells (e.g., −$57,175 on S&P 2001–2002) — judged **"questionable results."**
- **RSIct**: total = **+$333,489**, positive in nearly every cell — judged **"typical to good results."**
- **XTct**: total = **+$865,688**, strongly positive across virtually every market/period — judged **"excellent results."**

**Decision framework these three tiers illustrate**: exceptional results across the board → proceed without reservation; deplorable results → likely abandon; average/mixed results (the MA 5×20 case, and the most common real-world outcome) → further development still warranted. This is the Ch.8 preliminary-TEST stage; the same multimarket/multiperiod philosophy recurs one stage deeper as multimarket/multiperiod OPTIMIZATION (§5.6 below, with its own separate worked T-bond numbers) and finally as full multimarket Walk-Forward Analysis (§7).

### 2.6 Preliminary Testing in Full — Verification, Theoretical Expectations, and Preliminary Profitability (Pardo Ch.8)
Preliminary testing (Development Stage 3) serves **five stated functions**: (1) verify all formulae/rules calculate correctly; (2) determine whether formulae/rules/combinations behave as designed; (3) confirm performance is consistent with theoretical expectations; (4) provide a preliminary estimate of profitability; (5) give preliminary insight into robustness.

- **Verification of calculations and trades**: run one historical simulation on a sample large enough that every rule/formula fires at least a handful of times, using "reasonable" (not yet optimized) parameter values, then hand-check a significant sample of signals bar-by-bar against a spreadsheet or manual calculation — indicator math for built-in platform functions is generally low-risk, but the strategist's own custom formulae should always be verified this way; entries/exits are separately checked to confirm they fire on the correct day, at the correct price, under the correct condition.
- **Theoretical expectations check**: does overall trading behavior (trade frequency, direction relative to trend, whipsaw rate, average trade size) match what the strategy's underlying theory predicts? E.g., a long-term trend follower should NOT trade 3x/week; a countertrend intermediate-swing strategy should show a moderate trade count (2–4/month) with limited whipsaws and moderately large average profits. A sharp deviation from theoretical expectation requires distinguishing whether (a) the DATA was unusually hostile/favorable to the strategy's premise (doesn't necessarily invalidate the strategy, but should be documented) or (b) the THEORY itself is wrong — OR an unexpected-but-attractive result may point to a genuinely different, previously unanticipated strategy worth pursuing on its own merits (Pardo draws an explicit analogy to accidental discoveries in the history of science, encouraging strategists to pursue such "happy accidents" provided performance is attractive, theory is sound, and scripting is verified error-free).
- **Preliminary profitability rule of thumb**: expect annual profit roughly equal to the market's margin requirement; risk should not exceed annual profit. Worked contrast (both a moving-average strategy, same rules): S&P — net profit $19,750, 33 trades, 30.3% win rate, profit factor 1.28, judged **"acceptable"**; pork bellies — net loss −$5,804, profit factor 0.67, judged **"questionable."** **Decision rule**: an extreme loss under conditions theoretically favorable to the strategy (e.g., a trend-follower losing money in a strongly trending market) is a red flag suggesting the underlying theory itself may be flawed, warranting abandonment/redesign at this early stage — UNLESS the tested sample is judged an anomaly, in which case proceed cautiously to the next round.
- **Selecting the multimarket/multiperiod test basket**: two screening criteria for maximizing diversification — (1) statistical correlation of closing prices (correlation coefficient of +1 → reject one of the pair as redundant; −1 or between −0.5/+0.5 → both may be included); (2) fundamental/sector diversity (e.g., coffee vs. cattle vs. stock index — governed by unrelated fundamentals). With a small basket (5–10 markets), a full correlation matrix is called generally unnecessary — fundamental judgment suffices. Example baskets given: futures = coffee, cotton, crude oil, gold, pork bellies, soybeans, S&P 500, sugar, yen, T-bonds (or a smaller subset: coffee, cotton, crude oil, S&P 500, T-bonds); equities = Microsoft, Home Depot, United Airlines, Ford, Citigroup.
- **Test period length**: more history is generally better; **10 years is called a "solid test," 5 years (daily data) is stated as the practical minimum**, ideally spanning at least one each of bull/bear/sideways markets. Testing on ONE long contiguous block risks masking inconsistency (e.g., a $100,000 profit that is actually all earned in 1–2 good years, with losses/mediocrity in the rest), so Pardo recommends splitting the total window into several equal smaller segments (e.g., a 10-year test as five 2-year segments — the same segmentation used in the §2.5 worked example above) to expose period-to-period consistency, adjusting segment length upward for slower strategies if 2-year segments would yield too few trades to be statistically meaningful.

## 3. The Size of the Test Window, Degrees of Freedom, and Sample Requirements

### 3.1 Statistical Requirements (Pardo Ch.6, extended Ch.13)
- **Standard error**: `Standard Error = Standard Deviation / SqRt(Sample Size)`. Worked 3-way comparison (avg win $200, StDev $100) across sample sizes 10/30/100 winning trades → standard error $32/$18/$10, i.e., expected-win ranges of $168–232/$182–218/$190–210. **Conservative rule of thumb: assume the pessimistic (lower) end of the range.**
- **How many trades?** Traditional statistical minimum ~30 (a common statistician's rule of thumb), but "more are always better." Fewer than 30 trades requires extra caution: the strategy should be theoretically sound, robust across a wide contiguous parameter range, and profitable across a broad range of markets, to partially compensate for the small trade sample.
- **Degrees of freedom**: `Rdf% = 100 × [1 − (Udf/Odf)]` (Udf = used/consumed degrees of freedom by rules/indicators/start-up overhead; Odf = original/total data points). Worked examples: 30-day MA on 100 days → Rdf 70% (rejected — below the **≥90% remaining** rule of thumb); 10-day MA on 1,000 days → 99% (fine); a 100-rule strategy on 100 days → 0% remaining (absurd/invalid). This exact DoF formula and threshold is reused verbatim as Overfitting Cause #1 in Ch.13 (§7.1 below).
- **Start-up overhead**: distinct from DoF-consumption proper — a strategy cannot generate its first valid signal until its longest lookback has elapsed, an additional loss of usable trading days. Disproportionately burdens longer-lookback candidates in a scan, potentially biasing optimization toward shorter parameters for reasons unrelated to genuine edge (worked contrast: 3×8-day MA on 300 points consumes only 8-day start-up; 25×100-day MA on the same 300 points consumes 100-day start-up AND trades far less often — a "decided discrepancy in trade sample size" between candidates in the same scan). Posed as a specific, named problem for Walk-Forward Analysis tooling — must be handled correctly or it compounds across every walk-forward window.

### 3.2 Frequency of Trading and Market Types (Pardo Ch.6)
Smaller test windows bias optimization toward faster-trading parameter sets (easier to hit an adequate trade count); larger windows are needed for slower/longer-term strategies. Fast strategies on volatile markets (e.g., S&P) can use short windows (1–3 years); slow trend-followers on trend-persistent markets (e.g., yen) need long windows (3–6+ years).

**Four market types formally enumerated** (Pardo Ch.6, regression-slope heuristics):
1. **Bull market** — sustained rising prices; "typical" ≈15–50° regression slope; "roaring" ≈50–70°+ (rarer, less sustainable). "Among the most effective markets for most strategies."
2. **Bear market** — sustained falling prices; "typical" ≈15–40°; "panic" ≈50–70°+ (often mass-hysteria driven — 1929, 1987 cited). "Among the better types of markets to trade," but generally less profitable to trade than bull markets for many systems "because of differing market dynamics" (stated, not elaborated).
3. **Cyclic (trading-range) market** — oscillates between a defined top/bottom; can be flat or mildly tilted. "The cycling bull market is the best market for most strategies to trade" (stated opinion).
4. **Congested market** — absence of trend/volatility/tradable movement; short, narrow fluctuations. "The worst type of market to trade for most strategies"; good only for very short-term scalpers, and even then difficult.

### 3.3 Window Size and Model Life (Pardo Ch.6)
States as an explicit "operating assumption" (not proven in the book) that parameter sets — possibly strategies themselves — have a finite useful life. Two schools noted: (1) the "one true universal strategy that never needs updating" school ("if you have one, you do not need this book"); (2) periodic updating/reoptimization is normal and beneficial (Pardo's stated position, reinforced by Richard Dennis's reported comment that the Turtle System driver "no longer works," and by his own firm's practice of using different parameters per market).

**Rule of thumb**: walk-forward/real-time trading window ≈ **1/8 to 1/3 of the optimization (test) window size**. Worked example: 24-month optimization window → usable real-time trading life of roughly 3 (24/8) to 8 (24/3) months. *(Flagged internal inconsistency, preserved per traceability rule: the text's own arithmetic — 24/3 = 8 — sits alongside a "three to six months" prose framing that does not perfectly agree; not resolved further in the source.)*

**Rationale**: a smaller test window has, by definition, "seen" fewer market types/volatility regimes — a well-adapted-but-narrow parameter set will need more frequent reoptimization. A larger window has adapted across a broader domain and needs less frequent reoptimization, at the cost of being less peak-responsive to *current* specific conditions. This exact broad-but-less-responsive vs. narrow-but-more-responsive trade-off is the motivating tension resolved by Walk-Forward Analysis's Theory of Relevant Data (§5.4 below).

### 3.4 Kaufman's Independent Convergence on "More Data Is Better" — With a Sharp Caveat
Kaufman's Ch.21 opens with the same core recommendation stated even more emphatically: **"More is better."** More data captures more price patterns, price shocks, bull/bear markets, and rare-but-vital situations; a system successful on more data is more robust, "although not as profitable as when using less data," and its results are more realistic (Kaufman Ch.21). Kaufman adds a **trade-count fallacy** explicitly not present in Pardo's framing: a fast trader generating e.g. 2,500 trades in 5 years avoids small-sample error by trade *count*, but "5 years could all be one bull market" — a large number of trades is not, by itself, enough to guarantee regime coverage (Kaufman Ch.21). See §4.3 below for Kaufman's own in-sample/out-of-sample ratio convention, which differs numerically from this project's own 70/15/15 split while sharing its three-tier structure.

## 4. In-Sample / Out-of-Sample Discipline Across the Books

### 4.1 Pardo's Definitions (Pardo Ch.11, Concepts.md)
In-sample = data used to select/optimize parameters. Out-of-sample (also "postoptimization data") = data NOT used in that optimization, used to test the resulting parameters' real predictive/tradable value; testing on it is also called "double-blind testing." Central WFA claim: "the performance of a trading strategy on data which were never part of the optimization process is a far more reliable measure than performance based solely on in-sample simulation." A single walk-forward result "could still be a product of chance" — statistical confidence requires MANY walk-forwards, not just one.

### 4.2 Chan's Sample-Size and Out-of-Sample Mechanics (Chan Ch.3)
- **Rule of thumb (explicit numeric guidance)**: do not employ more than **5 parameters** (including entry/exit thresholds, holding period, MA lookback, etc.).
- **Sample-size rule of thumb**: data points needed to optimize parameters ≈ **252 × (number of free parameters)** (the author's experience-derived figure, not literature-derived). A 3-parameter daily model needs ≥3 years of daily data; a 3-parameter model updating every minute needs only ≥252/390 year (~7 months) of minute data — BUT using that same minute data to fit a *daily-frequency* model yields only ~7×21=147 effective data points, "far from sufficient."
- **Out-of-sample testing procedure**: split data into training and test sets (roughly equal, or test-set ≥1/3 of training-set size, subject to the minimum-training-size rule above); optimize on training only; verify "at least reasonable" performance on test. Poor test performance → likely data-snooping bias; remedy by simplifying (fewer parameters).
- **Sensitivity analysis**: after optimizing/verifying test-set performance, perturb parameters/qualitative features and observe both sets. A drastic performance drop for any parameter set other than the "optimal" one signals data-snooping. **Simplification test**: eliminate conditions/parameters one at a time; watch training-set degradation; if test-set performance does NOT also degrade in step, keep simplifying (eliminate as many conditions as possible provided test-set performance holds). **Explicit prohibition**: never add conditions/parameters or retune values specifically to improve test-set performance — this converts the test set into a second training set and reintroduces data-snooping bias.
- **Worked example (GLD/GDX pair trade, Example 3.6)**: Sharpe ≈2.3 (training) / ≈1.5 (test) at default thresholds — both strong, judged free of data-snooping bias. Tightening thresholds raised Sharpe to 2.9 (training) / 2.1 (test) — improved on BOTH sets, accepted as genuine refinement per the "must improve both" rule.
- **Ultimate out-of-sample test**: paper trading, or — if backtesting a *published* strategy purely to verify it (no further parameter optimization) — the entire period between publication and the test date is a genuine out-of-sample period, "equivalent to paper trading in rigor."
- **Parameterless trading models** (a named sidebar concept): parameters dynamically re-optimized within a moving lookback window rather than fixed once by the researcher — minimizes overfitting/data-snooping risk at the cost of being computationally demanding to run at every historical bar.

### 4.3 Kaufman's 60/20/20 Convention and the "Feedback" Trap (Kaufman Ch.21)
Kaufman gives an explicit three-tier split convention:
- **In-sample**: normally ~60% of available data — the scientific-method development/testing phase.
- **Validation**: another 20% — used after settling on rules/parameters, "to fix anything that might have been overlooked."
- **Out-of-sample (final)**: the remaining 20% — the final step, no further changes permitted after use.

**This project's own 70/15/15 split can be directly cross-referenced here**: the three-tier in-sample→validation→final-holdout *structure* is conceptually identical, though Kaufman's own stated default ratio (60/20/20) differs numerically from a 70/15/15 split (Kaufman Ch.21).

**Recency-order caution** (raised, not resolved by Kaufman): most analysts take the oldest portion of a long window for in-sample and validate on the most recent years, but recent data may be more volatile / reflect different economic conditions. The reverse choice (test on most recent years, validate on older data) raises the explicit unresolved question: "are you overfitting the current data? Does it matter [if] the older data doesn't perform as well?" **General principle stated**: any data not previously used in testing will underperform the tested profile — this underperformance is "expected and perfectly acceptable, as long as it is still profitable with reasonable risk."

**Alternating test periods** (an alternative to a single contiguous split): alternate fixed in-sample/out-of-sample blocks across the whole history; performance is calculated across all data but only in-sample periods are displayed/used for decisions. **Critical anti-cheating rule**: results must be shown as *daily* returns concatenated across in-sample periods, never as cumulative totals — comparing endpoint totals would leak information about whether the hidden interval between two in-sample blocks was net profit or loss ("would be cheating"). **Alternating random periods** — called "the most robust testing method" — requires segment boundaries fixed *before* development begins and never changed afterward, with a fresh random boundary draw for each new strategy (a documented spreadsheet procedure is given: choose segment count, set min/max segment length, draw uniform randoms scaled to the range).

**Two out-of-sample tiers, strict order**: the *first* out-of-sample period is a "second chance" permitting fixes to genuine, generalizable problems; the *last* is final — "there is no second chance." Concrete benchmark: an information-ratio drop from 2.0 (in-sample) to 1.0 (out-of-sample) is acceptable; an outright negative ratio (a loss) is a failure. **Integrity/feedback distinction (Kaufman's central ethical test)**: IF the first out-of-sample result disappoints AND you find a *logical, generally-applicable* fix (example: a volatility filter helping "many situations," raising the ratio from low to 1.0) → acceptable feedback-driven refinement, BECAUSE it corrects a general flaw. Conversely, changing a stop-loss specifically to avoid one devastating historical loss (e.g., 2008) is **explicitly labeled bad** — narrow, case-specific rule-fitting. **Strict rule, then qualified**: once final out-of-sample data has been used, no further rule changes are permitted — any change at that point "is always overfitting." Kaufman's own realism: "that's unrealistic. No one wants to discard their system so quickly" — a change is still tolerated afterward *provided the average of all tests (not just the final one) is improved*.

**Step-Forward (Walk-Forward) Testing — Kaufman's own version, overlapping heavily with Pardo's WFA** (Kaufman Ch.21): select total test period → select in-sample interval → test first in-sample block → select best parameters → apply unchanged to the next block of unseen data, accumulate out-of-sample performance → step the window forward and repeat. **Short-Term Bias** (a distinct named risk from Pardo's framing): a short in-sample window may contain too few trades for a genuinely long-term model to be properly evaluated; diagnostic — if the walk-forward-selected "best" parameter oscillates erratically between very short and very long across successive windows, that instability signals the in-sample interval is too short; fix: lengthen the in-sample interval. **Feedback — the chapter's most serious named concern**: each pass through step-forward testing uses *all* the data (in-sample AND out-of-sample combined), even though only the out-of-sample portion is reported. IF the entire process is re-run more than once (e.g., after tweaking a rule) THEN there is, strictly, no true out-of-sample data left — "which violates our concept of scientific testing." **Fix**: reserve a separate block of data never touched during any walk-forward iteration, for one single final out-of-sample test at the very end of the whole development process.

**Direct methodological corroboration for this project's own pipeline**: Kaufman's "Feedback" warning independently corroborates treating walk-forward and a final out-of-sample/DSR gate as *separate, one-shot* checks rather than something re-run after every tweak — re-running walk-forward repeatedly during development is exactly the "no longer out-of-sample" problem Kaufman flags. His short-term-bias/erratic-parameter-oscillation diagnostic is a concrete, actionable audit item: if a walk-forward step ever selects wildly different "best" hyperparameters across consecutive windows, that is Kaufman's stated signal to lengthen the in-sample window, not to trust the result (Kaufman Ch.21).

### 4.4 Hilpisch's Practice: A Single Fixed Split, No Walk-Forward
Every ML example across Hilpisch Ch.5 (`LRVectorBacktester`, `ScikitVectorBacktester`, the Keras DNN classifier) uses a **single fixed in-sample/out-of-sample date-cutoff split** (e.g., train 2010–2017 / test 2018–2019), never walk-forward re-fitting or k-fold time-series cross-validation — this is confirmed both in the chapter narrative and explicitly flagged as a documented gap in the book's own extraction: "**Walk-forward / cross-validation for time series** — only a single fixed in-sample/out-of-sample split is used throughout... despite the strong emphasis on avoiding overfitting" (Hilpisch, Research_Ideas.md). Hilpisch's own hygiene practices that ARE consistently applied: **feature normalization using only training-set mean/std** (both train and test data normalized using the same μ/σ computed from training data only, avoiding look-ahead/leakage from test-set statistics — Hilpisch Ch.5); **date-based, not random-shuffle, train/test splitting**, appropriate for time series; **`shuffle=False`** on the Keras validation split, preserving temporal order; **all engineered features (`momentum`, `volatility`, `distance`) explicitly `.shift(1)`'d** so a feature is knowable before the day it predicts (Hilpisch Ch.5).

### 4.5 Chan's Look-Ahead-Bias Detection Procedure (Chan Ch.3)
- **Definition**: using information that was not actually available at the time a hypothetical trade decision was made. Example: an entry rule "buy when price is within 1% of the day's low" is look-ahead-biased because the day's low is unknowable until market close. Another example: using regression coefficients fit on the *entire* dataset to generate signals throughout that same dataset.
- **Avoidance**: use lagged historical data for signal calculation at every opportunity, unless the strategy only enters at the close of the current period.
- **Recommended MATLAB-style final-check procedure**: (1) run the backtest on the full dataset, save positions as file A; (2) truncate the most recent N days (10–100) from the data, rerun, save as file B; (3) truncate file A's most recent N rows so both end on the same date; (4) compare A and B — if NOT identical, the program has look-ahead bias (some "future," truncated data leaked into earlier position generation). Demonstrated concretely in Example 3.6 (GLD/GDX pair trade), confirming no look-ahead bias was present there.

## 5. The Optimization Framework: Search Methods and Objective Functions

### 5.1 Search Methods (Pardo Ch.9)
The optimization space is "the set of all possible strategy parameters, or candidates, for which a historical simulation can be created." A search method is "only as good as its objective function" — "be careful of what you wish for because you just might get it."

- **Grid search (brute force)**: exhaustively evaluates every combination. Advantage: thoroughness (cannot miss the best candidate given a sound objective function/space). Disadvantage: combinatorial explosion. Worked: a 2-variable, 70-candidate grid ≈70 seconds at 1 sec/test; a 4-variable example (8×20×21×21 steps) = 70,560 candidates ≈19.6 hours; a finer-grained version = 1,911,735 candidates ≈531 hours (≈22.13 days).
- **Prioritized step search**: scan one parameter at a time (holding others at a constant midpoint), find its local best, fix it, move on, repeat the whole cycle a few iterations until convergence. A worked 4-variable example needs as few as 166 tests/iteration. Two extra benefits beyond speed: (1) reveals each parameter's relative importance (large P&L swings across the scan = important; near-flat scan = unimportant, a candidate for removal, reducing overfitting risk); (2) drawback — can miss the true global optimum if parameters interact strongly, and is more demanding of the strategist's own decision-making.
- **Hill climbing**: "enlightened/informed selectivity" — moves through the space along a path of improving performance, typically examining only ~5–10% of the full candidate space. Weakness: can get trapped in a local maximum, especially in "spiky" (discontinuous) optimization spaces.
- **Multipoint hill climbing**: several independent hill-climbs from different starting points, keep the best result of each run — a middle ground between speed and thoroughness, less prone to local-maximum traps than single-point hill climbing.
- **Advanced/stochastic methods** (Pardo Ch.9, "broad strokes" treatment, credited in part to colleague Bruce DeVault): **Simulated Annealing** (metal-cooling analogy; `Energy State = −Objective Function`; starts hot/exploratory, cools toward a narrowing exploitation of the best-known region, always remembers/can revert to the best point found); **Genetic Algorithms** (selection proportional to fitness → crossover → mutation → repeat until generation-count/convergence/"good enough"; can typically identify the top parameter set evaluating as little as 5–10% of the candidate population, not guaranteed global-optimal but "tend to find very good solutions"); **Particle Swarm Optimization** (particles pulled toward personal-best and global-best locations; some variants add sub-group communication to avoid premature convergence; "proven very competitive with GAs"). **Common caveat for all directed/advanced methods**: because they don't evaluate every candidate, result quality depends heavily on the objective function guiding the search.

**General search-method risks**: (1) lack of thoroughness (some candidates never examined); (2) risk of returning a **local maximum** instead of the true **global maximum**, especially in "spiky" spaces (Pardo Ch.9).

### 5.2 Objective Functions (Pardo Ch.9)
Objective function = fitness function = optimization function = search criteria — "that algorithm that the optimization process employs to rank and to ultimately select the best combination of parameters." Called "the eyes of the strategist into the optimization space."

**Nine listed characteristics of a robust trading strategy a *good* objective function should implicitly reward** (Pardo Ch.9): (1) relatively even distribution of trades; (2) relatively even distribution of trading profit; (3) relative balance between long and short profit; (4) a large group of contiguous, profitable parameters; (5) acceptable performance across a wide range of markets; (6) acceptable risk; (7) relatively stable winning/losing runs; (8) a large, statistically valid trade count; (9) a positive performance trajectory.

**Explicit warning**: "an unsound evaluation type can actually, and unwittingly, promote overfitting during the optimization process."

**A review of candidate objective functions**:
- **Net/gross profit**: inadequate ALONE — 4 named reasons: (1) can be dominated by a single large/non-repeatable trade (e.g., a single short during the October 1987 crash); (2) can hide that most profit was made early, masking a devastating recent-period loss; (3) ignores risk entirely; (4) ignores whether the sample size is even statistically adequate. Still useful as a **minimum-threshold criterion** in a composite evaluation.
- **Annualized rate of return**: same weaknesses alone, useful for cross-market/period comparison and as a threshold.
- **Maximum drawdown**: a defensive, risk-side-only measure — "looking for the smallest dollar loss, not the largest dollar gain." Unacceptable alone (no positive-performance signal), but useful as a **rejection threshold**.
- **Correlation between Equity Curve and Perfect Profit (CECPP)**: correlation coefficient (−1 to +1) between a strategy's equity curve and the market's Perfect Profit curve (the theoretical, unattainable profit from buying every valley and selling every peak — see §6.4 below) over the same period. Formula: `Correlation Coefficient = Sum[(x(i)−M'x)(y(i)−M'y)] / [(n−1) × SD'x × SD'y]`, x = Perfect Profit, y = Equity Curve. +1 = strategy captures growing opportunity and holds up (or gently flattens) when opportunity stalls; −1 = the opposite (a poor strategy). **Explicitly endorsed as "an excellent candidate as a sole evaluation measure"** — one of very few measures given this endorsement.
- **Pessimistic Return on Margin (PROM)**: Pardo's own signature metric (credited in Notes to "my old friend and mentor Bo Thunman for the basic idea"). Definition: "an annualized yield on margin that is adjusted in a way that pessimistically assumes that a trading strategy will win less and lose more in real-time trading than it did in its historical simulation."
  ```
  PROM = {[AW × (#WT − Sq(#WT))] − [AL × (#LT + Sq(#LT))]} / Margin
  ```
  (AW=avg win, #WT=# wins, AL=avg loss, #LT=# losses, Sq=square root; the win count is reduced by its own square root/standard error while the loss count is increased by its own square root, before recomputing an adjusted annualized return.) Worked: $25,000 gross profit/49 wins, $10,000 gross loss/36 losses, $10,000 margin → naive ROM 150%, **PROM 98%**. Second worked pair: at LARGER trade counts (144w/121l, same $ totals) PROM rises to 120%; at SMALLER counts (16w/9l, same $ totals) PROM falls to 54% — because `sqrt(n)/n` shrinks as n grows, so the "pessimistic penalty" automatically shrinks for large, statistically reliable samples and grows for small, unreliable ones. **This is PROM's core elegance: it automatically penalizes statistically thin trade samples, not just low profit or high drawdown.**
  - **PROM minus biggest win**: PROM recomputed after stripping the single largest winning trade — tests robustness independent of a possible outlier/windfall.
  - **PROM minus biggest winning run**: PROM recomputed after stripping the single largest consecutive winning streak's total profit — tests robustness independent of a possibly unusually favorable stretch.
  - **Pardo's stated preference**: "The best single evaluation method or objective function would be one of the PROMs, and preferably the most stringent [PROM minus biggest winning run]."
- **Multiple evaluation types (composite recommendation)**: combine a primary ranking metric (e.g., PROM) with secondary floor/ceiling thresholds (e.g., floor net return >$5,000; ceiling max drawdown <$5,000; floor ≥10 trades/year) — "certainly identify a more robust class of trading strategies than will net profit [alone]."

### 5.3 Kaufman's Parallel Objective-Function Survey (Kaufman Ch.21)
Kaufman independently reviews four candidate single-number objective functions with similar critiques, arriving at the same underlying conclusion without citing Pardo's PROM/CECPP:
- **Net profits alone**: fine if pure accumulation is the goal, "but not knowing the risk associated with profits could lead to ruin."
- **Reward-to-risk ratio** (information ratio or Sharpe ratio) — the industry-favored success statistic Kaufman names explicitly at the chapter's start — puts performance in useful cross-strategy perspective but "ignores the absolute level of profits."
- **Returns adjusted for sample error**: more trades → more trustworthy return measures; for long-horizon trend systems with inherently few trades, adjusting for sample error produces a fairer comparison — structurally the same logic as PROM's sqrt(n) penalty, though Kaufman does not give a named formula.
- **Returns adjusted for time**: a short track record has an inherent statistical advantage over a longer one unless it too is adjusted for its own greater potential error.
- **Open, explicitly unresolved question posed by Kaufman**: an objective function favoring higher profit + lower risk + more trades + shorter recovery time + higher per-trade minimum sounds ideal, but "would it continue into the future? Can a performance measure be overfit in the same way as a system with too many parameters?" — no single definitive formula is offered; "the criterion that lets you predict future performance is the actual key to success."

**Cross-book note**: Pardo's PROM and Kaufman's "returns adjusted for sample error" independently converge on the identical insight — a sample-size-aware penalty on raw profit — despite arriving via different named formulas and different books; neither source cites the other.

### 5.3a Probabilistic vs. Binary System Output — Fishman/Barr/Loick's Technical Expert System (Kaufman Ch.20)
All objective functions surveyed in §5.2–5.3 implicitly assume a system that outputs a binary decision (buy/sell/flat) to be scored after the fact. Kaufman's Ch.20 worked example (Mark Fishman, Dean Barr, Walter Loick, "Artificial Intelligence and Market Analysis," adapted to the DJIA) shows an alternative: a rule-based "technical expert system" that outputs a **confidence-scored probability** rather than a crisp signal, treating indicator values as expert opinions requiring no fundamental data. Worked rule set (illustrative, not a recommendation to replicate as-is):
1. IF ADX > 18 AND ADX ≥ ADX[2] AND nontrending is false/undefined THEN 95% chance market is trending.
2. IF ADX < 30 AND ADX ≤ ADX[2] AND trending is false/undefined THEN 90% chance market is nontrending.
3. IF market is trending THEN MACD probability = 0.80 AND stochastic-%D probability = 0.85.
4. IF market is nontrending THEN MACD probability = 0.55 AND stochastic-%D probability = 0.75.
5. IF stochastic active AND %K/%D confirm an oversold cross THEN sell with 80% confidence (mirror-image buy rule for an overbought cross).
6. IF MACD active AND MACD > signal line THEN buy with 75% confidence (mirror-image sell rule for MACD < signal line).

The regime rules (1–2) set a base trending/nontrending confidence from ADX, which then reweights how much the MACD and stochastic sub-signals are trusted (rules 3–4) before those sub-signals fire their own confidence-scored buy/sell rules (5–6). **Relevance to validation methodology**: a system built this way cannot be graded with a simple win/loss objective function — its output is a confidence level, not a trade — so evaluating it requires checking probability **calibration** (does an 80%-confidence signal actually resolve favorably ~80% of the time across the backtest, not just on average direction) rather than only a net-profit or drawdown metric. **Explicit limitation flagged in the source**: absent further information, only a 50% baseline confidence should be assumed for any one sub-indicator's correctness as a trend signal — the stated confidence percentages are illustrative, not empirically derived; testing each rule's historical hit rate is offered as the way to replace assumed confidences with real ones. This worked example is the direct precursor, within the same source chapter, to Merloti's fuzzy expert system (§10.5) and to the GA/AI overfitting-risk material cross-referenced at `18_common_failure_modes.md` §1.

### 5.4 The Optimization Framework — Four Setup Decisions (Pardo Ch.10)
1. **Parameters to optimize + their scan ranges.** Principle: use the fewest optimizable parameters that still provide needed flexibility. Test unclear-importance parameters empirically (scan alone, holding others constant): large P&L swings = keep in optimization; minimal swings = fix as a constant or drop the rule.
2. **The historical data sample.** More history is **not axiomatically better** — Pardo explicitly disagrees with the traditional statistician's default; optimization-window size and out-of-sample-window size are themselves just two more strategy "parameters" to be determined empirically via WFA, not maximized blindly (previews §5.5's Theory of Relevant Data). Two selection principles: (1) large enough for a statistically valid trade sample with ≥90% remaining DoF where possible; (2) broad enough for a representative range of conditions (ideally at least one instance each of the four market types and a range of volatility levels). Worked DoF stress case: a 2-MA strategy (longest MA 50 days) on only 200 days of data consumes 50 DoF (25% of sample) purely on start-up — flagged as too costly.
3. **The objective function(s).** Cross-references §5.2; once settled, unlikely to change dramatically project to project (though it "may evolve with knowledge, experience, and expertise").
4. **Guidelines for evaluating the optimization output** — feeds both (a) judging the top parameter set's quality/robustness **in the context of the full population of results**, and (b) informing the subsequent WFA and reoptimization-scheduling decisions.

### 5.5 The Scan Range — Overscanning Risk (Pardo Ch.10, elaborated Ch.13 §7.3 below)
Two governing considerations: **theoretical** (the range must make intuitive sense for what the indicator represents — scanning a "short-term" MA from 1–1,000 days is nonsensical) and **practical** (compute cost scales as the product of all parameter step-counts; a 5-parameter × 10-candidates scan = 100,000 simulations vs. 100 for a 2-parameter scan). **Overly fine step sizes are explicitly called an overfitting risk, not just a compute-time waste** — they pack the optimization set with essentially non-distinct, redundant simulations, artificially inflating the apparent percentage of profitable simulations and depressing the apparent standard deviation, producing a misleadingly rosy statistical-robustness picture. **Rule of thumb for step-size proportionality**: keep the *percentage* step size roughly consistent across parameters (a change from 3→4 days in a short MA is a 33% jump; 90→91 days in a long MA is only 1.1%; a step size of 1 suits the short MA but is far too fine for a 30–100-day range, where a step of ~5 is more proportionally appropriate).

**Kaufman independently states the identical non-uniform-spacing principle** (Kaufman Ch.21): a 49-vs-50-day MA differs by only 2% while a 2-vs-3-day MA differs by 50% — equal-increment spacing over-weights longer, less-sensitive, redundant periods. **Recommended alternative**: percentage/geometric increments (multiply the starting test value by a constant ratio, e.g., ×1.5 repeatedly, round to integers) — this reduces total test count while improving result value. This is the same conclusion as Pardo's, arrived at independently via the same underlying arithmetic observation.

### 5.6 Multimarket/Multiperiod Optimization (Pardo Ch.10)
Worked T-bond example: MA scanned 1–31 step 2 (16 steps) × stop-loss 1–6 pts step 1 (6 steps) = 96 parameter sets, run across 5 two-year historical windows (1997–2006) = 480 tests for T-bonds; repeated identically for 9 more markets = **4,800 total simulations**. Robustness evaluation proceeds through progressively deeper stages: multimarket/multiperiod TEST (Pardo Ch.8) → multimarket/multiperiod OPTIMIZATION profile shape/significance (this section) → multimarket Walk-Forward Analysis (§5, "the last and most important stage").

### 5.6a Does the Strategy "Deserve" Further Development? (Pardo Ch.10)
A strategy is judged to "benefit from optimization" if there is a statistically significant overall improvement across the majority of markets/periods tested — illustrated with an explicit (non-universal) benchmark: an improvement from ~5% to ~7% average annualized return is called **"neither significant nor attractive,"** while an improvement to ~15% is called **"both significant and more attractive."** If post-optimization average risk-adjusted return is negative or mediocre across the basket/periods, the strategy is deemed without merit at this point — either discard it, or reconsider whether the optimization *framework itself* (not the strategy) was poorly designed and worth redoing. If risk-adjusted performance is profitable and risk acceptable across most of the basket/periods, the strategy proceeds to the "final and decisive test": Walk-Forward Analysis (§7 below).

### 5.7 Aronson's Descriptor/Trade-Profile Pattern-Discovery Method (Kaufman Ch.15)
**Not to be confused** with Pardo's unrelated "trade profile vs. evaluation profile" live-monitoring concept (documented at `09_exits.md`, `11_risk_management.md`, and `18_common_failure_modes.md`) — Aronson's "trade profile" is a pre-backtest **pattern-discovery/labeling methodology** for generating candidate indicators, not a live-monitoring comparison of an in-progress trade against its expected statistical shape.

David Aronson's non-market analogy (as related by Kaufman): a "professional jockey" is *characterized* by a set of conditions (height under 5'5", weight under 120 lbs, age 16-35, over 10 years riding) — satisfying all four is **necessary but not sufficient** to guarantee someone is a professional jockey; further qualification would be needed to narrow the set precisely. Applying the same logic to strategy development, define a **trade profile**: a set of necessary-but-not-sufficient conditions describing a class of desirable price moves, e.g.:
1. Price moves ≥3% from the starting price.
2. The move completes within 20 days.
3. No interim loss exceeds 0.5%.

A computer then locates **all** historical price moves satisfying the trade profile; the days preceding a qualifying upward move are labeled "buy days," those preceding a qualifying downward move "sell days." Candidate indicators (direction of a moving average, an overbought/oversold oscillator, direction of volume change, a 10-day momentum indicator, etc.) are then tested for **concentration** at these labeled points — i.e., whether the indicator reliably fires at or near the qualifying days rather than being scattered randomly through the data. **Ideal condition** (never perfectly achieved, and explicitly flagged as unrealistic): at least one indicator exceeds a threshold at every true buy/sell signal, or at least two indicators jointly confirm — "in the real world there are no perfect solutions." **LIM (Logical Information Machines)** is cited as a commercial tool/query-language built for exactly this kind of historical-conditional pattern search (the mechanism behind media claims like "today's 3% NASDAQ drop has a 90% chance of reversing tomorrow").

**Direct link to this file's overfitting material**: the same source chapter attaches an explicit combinatorial sample-size warning to this method that mirrors §9's DoF/sample-size causes of overfitting almost exactly — with n=1,000 days, a 1-day pattern has ~1,000 candidate instances, a 2-day pattern only ~250 per combination, a 3-day pattern ~1/9 each, and by a 10-day sequence there are 2^10=1,000+ combinations each with a vanishingly small case count. Even a specific 10-day sequence recurring 10 times in 1,000 days with a 70% follow-through rate is explicitly flagged as untrustworthy — "there are just not enough cases to have confidence in the result." **Mitigation offered**: score a window's directional bias as a continuous count (e.g., 15 up/5 down within a 20-day window) rather than requiring an exact ordered-sequence match — this expands the qualifying sample dramatically and turns a binary exact-match test into a continuous "bullishness" score, improving statistical reliability. Neural networks and genetic algorithms are flagged in the source as the natural high-dimensional extension of this descriptor-matching approach.

## 6. Robustness: Three Components of the Optimization Profile

Robust (Pardo Ch.8/10) = "able to withstand or overcome adverse conditions," operationalized: withstand = "not lose a great deal of capital"; overcome = "make some profit"; adverse conditions = choppy/trendless or tight/congested markets. Larry Hite (quoted): "We are not looking for the optimum method; we are looking for the hardiest method." A maximally robust strategy is profitable across a broad range of parameter sets, all major market types, a majority of historical periods, and many markets; a maximally "anti-robust" strategy is profitable in only ONE parameter set, ONLY in bull markets, ONE period, ONE market.

### 6.1 Statistical Significance of the Optimization Profile (Pardo Ch.10)
Core logic: the greater the % of parameter sets in the full population that are profitable, the less likely the result is due to chance (the "enough monkeys pounding on enough keyboards" analogy). **Worked benchmark tables**: Failed profile = 3.7% of 1,000 tests profitable → reject. Marginal profile = 22.4% profitable → borderline (**stated guideline: at least ~20% of parameter sets should be profitable** as a floor). Robust profile = 67.1% profitable → strong. Also want the *average* profit per test across the whole population (not just winners) to be meaningfully positive.

**Kaufman independently gives a near-identical numeric floor**: "if 70% of all tests are profitable, the system is robust and the average return and risk is a good estimate of expectations" (Kaufman Ch.21) — a materially *higher* bar than Pardo's ~20% marginal floor, applied to Kaufman's own averaging-based methodology (§6.5 below); both numbers are preserved as-stated per the traceability rule rather than reconciled, since the two authors define "profitable percentage" against differently-constructed test populations (Pardo's grid/step search population vs. Kaufman's own well-distributed test set).

### 6.2 Distribution of the Optimization Profile (Pardo Ch.10)
Compute mean/max/min/StDev of the whole population's performance. **Four listed robustness checkpoints**: (1) top parameter set's performance is close to the population average, ideally within 1 StDev; (2) average simulation across the whole population is itself profitable; (3) average minus 1 StDev is still profitable; (4) average minus 3 StDev is still profitable (an extremely demanding bar — requires ~95% of all parameter sets to be profitable; failing it is explicitly "by no means reason for alarm," but passing it "suggests extreme robustness"). Parallel checks on the profitable-only subset: top set within 1 StDev of the subset average; profitable simulations a significant/majority share of the total; profitable-subset total profit strongly positive relative to whole-population total; smaller StDev within the profitable subset = more consistency.

### 6.3 Shape of the Optimization Profile — Plateaus Over Peaks (Pardo Ch.10)
Two poles ("Pivot Chart" visualizations): (a) a smooth, gently-declining "hilltop" profile around the top parameter set = robust — the top parameter is "surrounded by a large group of other profitable parameter sets," implying insensitivity to small parameter drift; (b) a "spiky" profile — a narrow, tall profit peak surrounded by steep drop-offs into losing territory = non-robust, a classic overfitting symptom — "a performance spike is typically a statistical anomaly and not the result of a viable trading strategy." Geometric generalization: 1-parameter → gradual decline on both sides of the peak; 2-parameter → smoothly declining hill; 3-parameter → sphere-like decline from a central peak.

**Detailed worked numeric example** (Table 10.4, single-parameter 1–15-day MA scan): the apparent "best" result is a lone 6-day-period spike at $12,500 (neighbors: −$500 and $3,000 — steep, isolated); a secondary, LOWER nominal peak at 13 days ($7,000) has strong, similarly-sized neighbors ($6,500/$5,000, and $6,000/$4,500 two steps out) — judged the more robust choice despite the lower headline number. **Neighbor-averaging objective function**: averaging the top parameter with its two nearest neighbors gives the 6-day spike an adjusted score of ($12,500−$500+$3,000)/3 = **$5,000**, vs. the 13-day set's ($7,000+$6,500+$5,000)/3 = **$6,167** — a neighbor-aware objective function correctly re-ranks the more robust candidate above the raw-profit "winner."

### 6.4 Perfect Profit and Model Efficiency (Pardo Ch.2, Ch.9, Ch.12)
**Perfect Profit (PP)**: "the sum total of all of the potential profit that could be realized by buying every bottom and selling every top... the sum of the absolute value of every price swing formed between a price peak and a subsequent price valley." An unattainable theoretical ceiling — "how much opportunity did this market actually offer" over a given window/granularity. Ch.2's illustrative table: 5 markets, 5 years (2002–2006), total PP $4,696,777, average $939,355/year. Ch.12's Table 12.1 (credited to Valerii Salov): PP by 5-min/30-min/daily/weekly/monthly time degree — e.g., S&P PP ranges from $432,400 (monthly) to $18,498,100 (5-minute) over the same window, illustrating PP scales enormously with finer granularity. Explicitly and repeatedly called "an idealized and unobtainable goal" — not itself a trading target, purely an analytical yardstick. **Real-world contrast cited alongside the PP table (Pardo Ch.2)**: per a Barclay Trading Group survey, only **16 of 375 CTAs** (as of June 2007) achieved 25%+ annualized returns over the preceding 5 years — used rhetorically to show how much headroom exists between the theoretical PP ceiling and what even skilled real-world managers actually capture.

**Model Efficiency (ME)**: `Model Efficiency = Net Profit / Perfect Profit`. Worked example: $25,000/$300,000 → ME = 8.33%, "actually quite good." **Stated rule of thumb: 5%+ ME is considered very good.** ME's advantage: enables meaningful cross-market comparison, and a STABLE ME across years — even amid varying raw dollar profit — is a positive robustness signal (the strategy consistently captures a similar *share* of whatever opportunity exists, rather than varying erratically).

### 6.5 Kaufman's Complementary Robustness Toolkit (Kaufman Ch.21)
Kaufman's chapter contributes a substantial, largely independent set of robustness-diagnosis and visualization techniques not present in Pardo's treatment:

- **Averaging over the single "best" result (the chapter's central robustness thesis)**: "since you cannot know in advance which specific parameter set will be 'best' going forward, the most robust performance is when the average of all tests of one strategy is better than the average of all tests of another strategy," and the *average* of all tests — not the single peak result — is the realistic future expectation. Practical implementation: hold multiple parameter sets simultaneously, equal capital allocation, chosen randomly or manually across the full test space, to approximate achieving that average return in practice. **This is the same underlying principle as Pardo's neighbor-averaging objective function (§6.3) and plateau-over-peak preference, arrived at via a distinct route and stated even more forcefully as an actual capital-allocation rule** (Kaufman Ch.21) — see §9 below for the explicit agreement/reinforcement note.
- **Kurtosis as an overfitting diagnostic**: when a rule change raises peak profit at its targeted parameter values but *degrades* performance elsewhere (leaving the average roughly unchanged while variance rises), this is measurable via **kurtosis** — "a kurtosis value above 6" is given as a **stated sign of overfitting**. A rule causing this pattern "is not an improvement" even though it raised a headline number.
- **Sensitivity Testing / Sensitivity in n-Space**: performance should be only modestly sensitive to small parameter shifts, with decay accelerating as the shift size grows. For n parameters, treat the chosen core set as the center of an n-dimensional sphere; sort tested combinations by (Euclidean-style) distance from that center into concentric "shells," average performance per shell — produces an n-dimensional decay curve as a function of distance from the core, in any direction.
- **Heat maps and 3-D surface plots**: arranging a 2-parameter test as a color-coded matrix makes visually clear which regions perform well; a genuinely good result set shows smooth contours, while erratic alternating high/low bars or a random mid-grid spike indicates the system "is unstable or badly defined and should not be traded" (spikes flagged as likely data errors or single extreme-event artifacts).
- **Averaging neighboring bars — asymmetric rule for profit vs. drawdown**: when a 1-parameter scan shows an odd local spike/dip against a smooth backdrop, don't trust the single spiking value. **For profit**: use the *average* of the neighboring bars — "explicitly likened to the k-nearest-neighbor AI technique." **For drawdown, a different (asymmetric) rule**: use the *maximum* (not average or minimum) drawdown among the neighbors, reasoning "a small change in the trend signal could have avoided a larger drawdown, but there is no assurance that will happen in the future."
- **"Four or fewer variables"** (empirical claim cited to Futures Truth organization, John Hill/George Pruitt): the best-performing validated systems commonly have four or fewer variables.
- **Four stated characteristics of a system with the best forecasting ability** (Kaufman Ch.21): (1) sound premise — every rule should exploit a real fundamental or price phenomenon, not a pattern discovered purely via optimization ("by testing enough patterns, it is statistically probable that one of them will seem to fit"); (2) adapts to changing market conditions (self-adjusting features, not fixed price levels); (3) uses the fewest rules possible; (4) properly tested (most data spanning all regimes, proper in-sample/out-of-sample discipline, or paper-trading until enough live trades accumulate). "At different times each of these four criteria may seem most important, but in the long run, they must all be satisfied to create a robust program."
- **"Loose pants fit everyone"** (Ken Tropin, founder of Graham Capital, quoted): a genuinely general/robust system will look *suboptimal* (not the best possible) on any single market, in exchange for working reasonably across many — reinforcing testing across a wide range of markets as a robustness check.
- **The Significance of "Significant" — a direct multiple-comparisons warning**: statistically-significant-looking results are often not actually significant, because of how many combinations were tried to find them. Restates the 70%-profitable-tests threshold as applying only to the *original*, honestly-wide parameter range — narrowing the range afterward to exclude known losers invalidates that percentage. Worked arithmetic: testing 1,000 different parameter values over a 10-year sample could produce roughly 50 "profitable by chance" results purely from multiple testing. **Broader warning**: the **cumulative number of tests across all strategies ever tried** (not just the final one reported) is the statistically relevant figure — "finding 1 profitable strategy in 100 should not be comforting."

  **Direct corroboration for this project's own methodology**: this is an independent statement of the same multiple-comparisons logic that motivates a Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) gate tracking a cumulative trial count across an entire research program rather than evaluating any single strategy's Sharpe in isolation — Kaufman's plainer-language "50 profitable-by-chance results out of 1,000 tests" arithmetic and "cumulative number of tests across all strategies is important" claim reaches the same conclusion as the formal Bailey/López de Prado DSR machinery, via a different (non-formal) route.

- **Trading more than one parameter set**: the core inference from the whole robustness discussion — choosing the single best-net-profit parameter set is not a good way to predict future returns, because the top-scoring combination most likely benefited from being on the right side of (or having avoided) a specific historical price shock. If a large area of the test grid is continuously profitable, select 3+ combinations at random from across that space, trade each as an independent sub-system with an equal fraction of capital, and expect the *average* return of the sampled sets as the realistic forward expectation.

## 7. Walk-Forward Analysis (Pardo Ch.11 — the Book's Centerpiece)

Called by Pardo "the only 99 percent foolproof method" of strategy optimization (Introduction) and "an idiot-proof way of optimizing a trading strategy" (Ch.11) — the single most emphasized claim in the book, though explicitly tempered by the acknowledgment that a genuinely sound strategy can still be inadvertently overfit through bad procedure even when WFA is used. Pardo states he "first introduced Walk-Forward Analysis to the trading public in the first edition... in 1991."

### 7.1 Four Questions WFA Answers (Pardo Ch.1/3/11)
1. Is the strategy robust? Will it make money in real-time trading?
2. What rate of return will it produce in real-time trading?
3. How will typical changes in trend/volatility/liquidity affect performance?
4. What are the best parameters to use in real-time trading for maximum profit with minimum risk?
Question 1 is "rather obvious[ly]" the most important; the rest "can almost seem like an afterthought" once #1 is answered affirmatively.

### 7.2 The Walk-Forward (single walk-forward — the atomic unit)
**Definition**: a two-step process — (1) standard optimization on an in-sample window; (2) evaluation of the resulting top parameter set on an immediately-adjacent, NEW (never-optimized-on) out-of-sample window. Also called out-of-sample testing or **double-blind testing**. "The closest simulation possible of the way in which an optimizable trading strategy is most often used by traders" in real practice.

**Worked single walk-forward example**: strategy "RSI CT" on S&P futures; 48-month optimization window (1/1/2000–12/31/2003), buy/sell variables each scanned 0–300 step 20 (256 candidates); top parameter set found via the objective function. 6-month trading window (1/1/2003–6/30/2003): Optimization P&L $47,390 (annualized $11,847); Trading P&L $20,265 (annualized $40,530). **Walk-Forward Efficiency: 341%** — this single walk-forward's out-of-sample rate of profit was over 3x the in-sample rate. Pardo notes even an impressive single result "could still be a product of chance," motivating the need for many walk-forwards (a full Analysis).

### 7.3 The Full Walk-Forward Analysis (the multi-window procedure)
**Definition**: "a set of individual, sequential walk-forwards performed on a comprehensive and representative sample of price history," where the optimization+trading window pair rolls/steps forward through the ENTIRE historical sample. Longer overall history → more individual walk-forwards → greater statistical reliability.

**Four purposes, explicitly enumerated** (Pardo Ch.11): (1) prove validity/robustness over a large enough sample to minimize the chance the result is random; (2) produce a more accurate profit/risk profile (including a more realistic drawdown estimate than a single in-sample optimization typically yields, since in-sample optimization tends to systematically screen OUT large-drawdown parameter sets, biasing the in-sample MDD statistic low relative to what real trading may show); (3) verify the validity of the optimization process itself (a strategy believed sound but showing poor WFE should trigger a review of the WFA setup for procedural errors before concluding the strategy itself is bad); (4) empirically determine HOW OFTEN to reoptimize in real trading — the walk-forward window length becomes the answer, and should be followed "with discipline and on schedule whether or not the strategist thinks it needs it or not" (an explicit prescriptive/behavioral rule, not just an analytical one).

**Setting up a Walk-Forward — required components**: (1) scan ranges for variables to optimize; (2) objective/search function; (3) optimization window size; (4) walk-forward (trading/out-of-sample) window size. Optimization window size depends on data availability, strategy style/pace (faster strategies start 1–2 years; slower strategies start 3–6 years — starting heuristics, adjustable), data relevancy, and parameter shelf-life.

> **Internal inconsistency in Pardo, explicitly preserved (per this file's rules and the caller's flagged known example)**: Ch.11's rule of thumb for the walk-forward-to-optimization-window ratio is **25–35% of the optimization window size**. Ch.6's rule of thumb for the same relationship (framed as real-time-trading-life vs. test-window size) is **1/8 to 1/3**. These two stated heuristics are not perfectly reconciled by the source and are both reported here as given, per Pardo's own Master_Summary, which flags this explicitly: "the walk-forward-window-to-optimization-window ratio is given as '25–35%' in Ch.11 but as '1/8 to 1/3' in Ch.6 — these do not perfectly align and the book does not reconcile them."

General shelf-life guidance repeated: a strategy optimized on 2 years of data will likely remain usable for roughly 3–6 months of real trading; one built on 5 years of data will likely remain usable for roughly 1–2 years.

### 7.4 Worked Full WFA Case Study — "RSI CT" on S&P, 1990–2007 (Pardo Ch.11)
Setup: price history S&P futures 1/1/1990–12/31/2005 (text also references through 12/31/2007 in the report); estimation (optimization) window 36 months; test (walk-forward) window 6 months; step window 6 months; RSI period scanned 2–50 step 2; OB/OS levels each scanned 0–80 step 20; **objective function: Pessimistic Return on Margin (PROM)**.

**Result**: 30 individual walk-forwards; **19 of 30 profitable (63%)**; total out-of-sample net profit **$154,350–$168,113** *(two related totals cited from different summary tables in the source — the Walk-Forward Report table sums to $168,113, the Performance Summary table shows $154,350 net profit with an additional −$19,625 open-position P/L noted, which reconciles the difference — preserved as an explicit open question in Pardo's own Master_Summary rather than silently resolved)*; total underlying trades 218; overall profit factor 1.26. **Conclusion**: the strategy "worked" out-of-sample across 30 consecutive 6-month periods spanning "unprecedented volatility and trend change" — judged robust and ready for real-time trading.

**Walk-Forward Efficiency calculation**: average annualized in-sample (optimization) profit across the 30 windows: $8,278. Average annualized out-of-sample (walk-forward) profit: $11,208. **WFE = $11,208/$8,278 = 135%.** Practical use: to forecast expected real-time annualized profit from a NEW optimization result, multiply the new in-sample annualized profit by the WFE ratio (worked example: fresh optimization shows $10,000/year in-sample, WFE 135% → expect roughly $13,500/year ± variance in real-time trading).

**Risk**: average max drawdown across the 30 optimization windows: $26,133 (high $59,950, low $4,200). Rule of thumb: if real-time drawdown greatly exceeds the optimization-window drawdown, this is a warning sign requiring investigation — the walk-forward-window drawdown is the primary risk benchmark going forward.

### 7.5 Walk-Forward Efficiency (WFE) — Definition and Benchmarks (Pardo Ch.11)
WFE = ratio of annualized **postoptimization (out-of-sample)** profit to annualized **in-sample (optimization)** profit — "how much of the in-sample edge survived into unseen data." **Stated benchmark**: WFE around **25%** signals "something wrong" (unsound or overfit strategy). **Robust strategies typically show WFE greater than 50–60%, with extremely robust strategies higher still.** No theoretical ceiling — WFE CAN exceed 100% (e.g., if post-optimization market conditions offered more profit potential than the optimization window did). A radically lower real-time WFE than the strategy's own established WFA-derived WFE — not explainable by different market conditions — is flagged as a likely symptom of overoptimization having crept in.

### 7.6 The Theory of Relevant Data (Pardo Ch.11)
WFA resolves a tension built since Ch.6: the trade-off between **Peak Performance** (best served by parameters fit tightly to CURRENT market conditions — a strategy adapted to current conditions, e.g., a strong bull market with clean swings, will outperform one fit to different conditions if applied to that bull market — "axiomatic," per Pardo) and **Statistical Rigor** (best served by using MORE data — sufficient data/trades/DoF/market-type coverage, restating Ch.6 principles).

**Key resolving insight**: a WFA's overall statistical rigor comes from the LENGTH OF THE FULL WFA (accumulating many walk-forward windows end-to-end, ideally **10–20+ years / 10–20+ walk-forwards**), NOT from the size of any single optimization (in-sample) window. This decoupling lets the strategist use SHORTER, more currently-relevant optimization windows (better peak performance/adaptiveness) while still achieving overall statistical rigor from the cumulative length of the whole WFA — "a long Walk-Forward Analysis is able to use shorter length in-sample windows to achieve both optimal trading performance... and statistical rigor at the same time." This is the single insight that directly explains WHY Walk-Forward Analysis outperforms a single static optimization for handling a market's continually-changing conditions.

### 7.7 Shifting Markets / Sources of Condition Variation (Pardo Ch.11)
Pardo surveys competing philosophical positions on whether/how markets change (some believe markets never fundamentally change; some believe markets shift seismically; some believe markets are an "endless succession of ever different minimarkets"). His own operating position (stated plainly, not proven): markets DO shift/fluctuate enough, in practice, that periodic strategy/parameter adaptation is advantageous for many strategies. (J.P. Morgan anecdote: asked what the market will do, "It will fluctuate.")

Named sources of variation: seasonal tendencies (esp. agricultural markets); bull/bear cycles tied to supply/demand and the business cycle; volatility regime shifts (possible cyclic component noted, citing unspecified "recent studies"); trending vs. mean-reverting market "personalities" (currencies cited as trend-persistent; S&P/NASDAQ cited as more mean-reverting with periods of strong trend); the "minimarkets" perspective (a 5-day rally can be "a bull market" to a very short-term trader and "a correction" to a long-term trader — a matter of the observer's time frame); volatility tends to rise as bull/bear trends reach extremes and near their end; contracting liquidity → smaller ranges + more gaps, expanding liquidity → smoother price action with fewer gaps.

### 7.8 The Role of a Minimal (Manual) Walk-Forward, and the Three-Way Outcome Interpretation (Pardo Ch.11)
If full automated WFA tooling isn't available, Pardo still strongly recommends running at least a handful of manual walk-forwards — "a strategist would be at a severe disadvantage deploying a trading strategy that was not tested to at least this level." **Worked minimal-example recipe**: with 15–20 years of data available, optimize on 10 years, walk forward on 5 years (and, if data allows, also walk BACKWARD on an earlier 5-year block, and test a middle segment too); run this on at least 10 diversified markets for added statistical validity given the test's otherwise limited scope.

**Three-way outcome interpretation**: (1) losing money in the majority of walk-forwards → not a tradable strategy; (2) modest/limited profit in a majority → possible sign of a poor-to-overfit strategy; (3) out-of-sample profit roughly proportional to in-sample profit (adjusting qualitatively for differing market conditions between windows) in a majority → likely a robust strategy.

### 7.9 WFA and the Portfolio (Pardo Ch.11)
The single-market WFA process must be repeated per market in the intended trading portfolio. Two purposes: (1) confirm tradability across the intended basket; (2) the more markets a strategy passes WFA robustly across, the more overall confidence in the strategy's general robustness (not just its fit to one market).

## 8. Performance Evaluation as an Investment Decision (Pardo Ch.12)

By this point a strategy is presumed to have passed the multimarket/multiperiod test (§2.5's worked coffee/crude/S&P/T-bonds/yen example), benefited from optimization, and passed WFA across a diversified basket — this chapter evaluates it as an **investment**, comparing it against the broader universe of alternatives. Pardo warns of sunk-cost bias: after so much developmental effort, it is tempting to keep a strategy even if it's not actually competitive with alternatives.

### 8.1 Comparing the Strategy to Alternatives
A trading strategy's return must be benchmarked against risk-free instruments (T-bills, CDs), other investment classes (stocks, bonds, real estate, art), other CTAs/hedge funds, and other commercially available trading strategies. Operating/maintenance costs (computers, data, software, staff, time) are a real cost of doing business that returns must overcome. "The profit and risk profile of a trading model must be superior, or at least sufficiently attractive in some other way, to other forms of competing investments to justify its existence."

### 8.2 Maximum Drawdown and Trading Risk
**MDD** (definition): "the dollar value of the largest decline from equity high to a subsequent equity low." Called "the single most important measure of risk for a trading strategy" — larger and more consequential in aggregate than either per-trade risk or overnight risk, given its potential to wipe out an undercapitalized account entirely.

**Why MDD estimates can be systematically wrong in either direction**: **volatility mismatch** — if historical-sim volatility was LOWER than subsequent real-time volatility, real drawdowns will likely exceed the historical MDD estimate (undercapitalization risk); if historical-sim volatility was HIGHER, real drawdowns will likely be smaller (safer but capital-inefficient overcapitalization). "Overcapitalization and underperformance... is a better situation than undercapitalization and the consequent risk of ruin. It is best to avoid both conditions, however." **Statistical/mathematical limitations** — small-sample statistics (as most trading-strategy simulations necessarily are) are inherently "fuzzier" than large-sample statistics; more significantly, Benoit Mandelbrot's fractal-geometry finding is cited: financial time series follow a **fractal, not Gaussian/normal, distribution** — meaning standard statistical assumptions embedded in most performance/risk calculations (including MDD itself) are "in error to some degree or another." **Conclusion stated plainly, an explicit epistemic limitation the author acknowledges without fully resolving**: "the most accurate measure of maximum drawdown that we can derive with current statistical measures is likely to remain to some extent inaccurate."

**MDD in context**: compare MDD to average/minimum drawdown — tighter clustering suggests a more robust model. **Diagnostic framing**: it is a POSITIVE/expected sign if MDD occurred during genuinely adverse conditions (extended congestion, or extended volatile/choppy activity — the typical settings for max drawdown across most strategies). It is a WARNING sign if MDD occurred under conditions indistinguishable from those that produced some of the strategy's BEST equity gains (e.g., mid-strong-trend) — potentially grounds for redesign or abandonment if unexplainable.

**Maximum run-up (MRU)** (the positive mirror of MDD): "the largest improvement in trading equity between an equity low and a subsequent equity high." Periods of MRU define the strategy's OPTIMAL operating conditions. Psychological warning: a big run-up can breed "damaging euphoria and a false sense of confidence" just as damaging to discipline as a big drawdown's fear.

### 8.3 Required Capital, RAR, and RRR — Full Formulas
- **Required Capital (RC)**: `RC = Margin + Risk`. Base formula example: margin $10,000, MDD $15,000 → RC = $25,000 (bare minimum — survives one more max-size drawdown, but the NEXT trade after that must be a winner). More conservative: `RC = Margin + (MDD × 2)` → RC = $40,000. Even more conservative: `RC = Margin + (MDD × 3)` → RC = $55,000. The degree of MDD multiplication is a function of personal risk tolerance and confidence in the accuracy of the original MDD estimate. **"Undercapitalization has been observed to be one of the primary causes of trading failure"** — erring conservative is explicitly recommended.
- **Annualized Risk-Adjusted Return (RAR)**: `Annualized RAR = Annualized Profit / (Margin + Risk)`. "Profit can not be correctly assessed without reference to its cost. The primary cost of profit in trading is its risk." RAR can be improved TWO ways: raising profit at constant risk, OR lowering risk at constant profit — both equally valid. Worked contrast: Strategy A $10,000 profit/$200,000 risk → RAR 5%; Strategy B $5,000 profit/$2,500 risk → RAR 200%. Despite B's smaller absolute profit, it is dramatically superior risk-adjusted. Worked full example: profit $25,000, margin $10,000, risk = MDD($20,000)×2 = $40,000 → RAR = $25,000/$50,000 = **50%**.
- **Reward-to-Risk Ratio (RRR)**: `RRR = Net Profit / Maximum Drawdown`. Worked (annualized): $25,000/$5,000 = **5.0**. **Rule of thumb: RRR should be 3 or better.** Explicitly caveated: RRR's validity is only as good as the accuracy of its two input components.

*(See `10_position_sizing.md` and `11_risk_management.md` for how these formulas are reused/extended in this project's own capital-sizing framework.)*

### 8.4 Consistency and the Four Profit-Distribution Cases (Pardo Ch.12)
"The more consistent the trading model is in every statistical measure, the better and more robust the trading strategy is likely to be." An inconsistent strategy shows "concentrated clusters of wins and losses" with highly variable win/loss sizes. Four categories where even distribution is sought: profit and loss; wins and losses; long and short trades; winning and losing runs. Three evaluation techniques: (1) compute avg/StDev/max/min per category — smaller max-min span and smaller StDev = more consistent; (2) remove the single maximum value from a category and recompute the average — a sharp drop (1–2 StDev) signals excessive reliance on a single outlier; (3) check even the timeline spread — concentration in one or two periods is a red flag, UNLESS explainable by a genuine, identifiable market-conditions shift.

**Four illustrative cases (Tables 12.2–12.5), all sharing identical totals ($50,000 net profit/$10,000 MDD, 2001–2005) to isolate the effect of distribution alone**:
1. **Suspect distribution**: profit declines every year ($50,000→−$25,000) while drawdown RISES every year ($5,000→$10,000). Judged concerning: performance may be genuinely deteriorating, or the early strong year may be masking a real recent problem.
2. **Failed distribution**: $110,000 profit concentrated in ONE year, every other year a consistent −$15,000 loss — the "big fish in a small pond" pattern (see §9.4 below). Judged: outright reject or seriously re-examine.
3. **Excellent distribution**: profit rises steadily/modestly each year ($8,000→$14,000) while drawdown falls ($10,000→$4,000) — judged the strongest, most confidence-inspiring case.
4. **Accelerating distribution**: same uneven shape as Case 1 but INVERTED direction — losses early, rising to strong profits later, with drawdown falling steadily. More favorable than Case 1's shape but still requires an explanation ("Why?").

**General rule distilled**: the more even the distribution, the better; any clear directional trend (in either direction, for either statistic) must be explained by a market-conditions narrative, not simply accepted at face value.

## 9. The Five Causes of Overfitting, in Full (Pardo Ch.13)

### 9.1 Terminology Clarification
Pardo argues four terms — overoptimization, overfitting, curve-fitting, data mining — are often wrongly used interchangeably. **Overoptimization = overfitting** (Pardo treats these as literally identical). **Curve-fitting** and **data mining** are related but DISTINCT concepts, "with little, in fact, relationship to one another" — the underlying problem these terms are meant to flag is really "the result of poor research procedures and an abuse of hindsight." Pardo criticizes blanket dismissal of ALL optimization as curve-fitting as a logical fallacy, comparing it to concluding "all women are blondes" from having only met blonde women.

**Precise definition**: "the overfitting or overoptimizing of a trading strategy is the identification of parameters that produce good trading performance on in-sample price history but produce poor trading performance on out-of-sample price history." Applies identically whether "out-of-sample" means an unseen historical window OR live real-time data. Occurs in degrees — mild (some real-time underperformance, still possibly profitable) to severe (catastrophic real-time losses). Important nuance: "an overfit strategy may actually be a sound strategy that is actually performing poorly because of overfitting" — the underlying premise could be valid even though procedural execution corrupted the result. A properly fit strategy "will perform essentially the same way in real-time trading as it did in the optimization or fitting process."

### 9.2 The Abuse of Hindsight (a distinct, arguably more common failure mode than pure statistical overfitting)
Defined via three illustrative (hypothetical) failure narratives, each following the same pattern (observe a pattern in-sample → build a rule around it without adequate out-of-sample testing → trade it → market conditions shift → catastrophic loss): (1) biasing toward longs after observing a 5-year bull run, then getting caught in the subsequent bear/choppy turn; (2) adding a $3,000 risk stop specifically to eliminate one observed large losing trade, seeing profit rise 50% (entirely attributable to removing that one trade), then getting whipsawed by the stop once the trend stops and the market turns choppy; (3) tripling position size on an observed T-bond/S&P "linkage" pattern, doubling backtested profit, then suffering catastrophic over-leveraged losses once the linkage "unlinks."

**Diagnosis**: (1) implementing a change without thoroughly re-testing it across the FULL range of markets/periods — the fix is validating any candidate improvement broadly; if it only helps on the narrow slice that inspired it, "it is not really such an improvement"; (2) "shoddy inductive reasoning" — over-generalizing a principle from too few observations. **Explicit cure**: "Don't let it creep into your design process. If a new rule suggests itself based upon a more detailed review of trading performance, be sure that it is exhaustively tested before assuming it is an improvement that will hold up in real time." Reviewing historical performance for insight is NOT itself forbidden — the abuse is acting on an insight without adequately re-testing it.

### 9.3 The Overfit Forecasting-Model and Overfit Trading-Model Parables
**Forecasting-model parable**: a statistician's stock-market linear regression is upgraded to follow rally/decline curvature more closely (modest improvement), then — reasoning "if a little is good, more is better" — fitted to a very-high-order equation tracing EVERY historical peak/valley (near-perfect apparent fit); live forecasts turn out far LESS accurate than even the original simple model, producing catastrophic trading losses. **Diagnosis**: abandoning sound statistical theory in favor of chasing closeness-of-fit for its own sake, consuming too many degrees of freedom for the available data, and never validating with a walk-forward/out-of-sample test before trading real money. **General principle**: "with enough variables, a curve can be fit perfectly to any time series," but a perfect fit has no guaranteed (and often zero) predictive value — a model's true forecasting ceiling is set by (1) the sophistication of the methodology, and (2) the actual proportion of NONRANDOM movement present in the underlying data; fitting to the random portion IS overfitting by definition.

**Trading-model parable (a direct, mirroring worked example)**: a trader optimizes a single MA (3–15 days) on 2 years of data, finds a set making $10,000/2yr with $5,000 MDD, forward-tests it 6 months → makes $2,000 (roughly on pace — "good results, so far so good"). Emboldened, adds a 2nd MA (10–100 days) → in-sample profit jumps to $25,000 (2.5x) at the SAME $5,000 MDD — **skips the out-of-sample test this time out of excitement**. Emboldened further, adds TWO MORE variables (buy/sell volatility bands, 0–5% in 0.25% steps) → in-sample profit soars to $65,000 (6.5x original) at only $7,500 MDD — "beside himself," about to start trading Monday. Reluctantly runs an out-of-sample test first (only because markets are closed on the weekend) → the 4-variable "best" system **loses $15,000 in the 6-month out-of-sample test**. **Diagnosis**: the trader "compromised degrees of freedom, overscanned the parameters, overparameterized, used too small a data sample, and neglected to walk-forward the test" at every step after the first (validated) result.

**Moral (Pardo's explicit statement of the limits of the entire method)**: "a trading strategy that has been developed according to sound research principles and evaluated and optimized by a walk-forward analysis is a trading strategy that can be reasonably expected to perform in real time in a way that is similar to the way it worked during development. If the trading strategy developer strictly and religiously follows these principles to the fullest degree, she has done all that she can. The rest is up to the markets."

### 9.4 Symptoms of an Overfit Trading Model
Simplest description: "devastating real-time trading losses," often from the very start of live trading, with real-time performance "completely different — often the opposite" of the testing profile. Milder overfitting produces underperformance rather than outright disaster (illustrative: test-profile avg losing streak 3-in-a-row/$4,000, avg winning streak 2-in-a-row/$6,500 vs. real-time 4-in-a-row/$7,000 and 2-in-a-row/$4,000; or a headline shift from 30% annualized RAR in-sample to 15% out-of-sample — "not a disaster" but "a warning that not all is perfectly well"). A severely overfit strategy can get lucky with a profit or two before "falling into the pit of unending losses," confusing/delaying a trader's correct diagnosis. **Key detection heuristic**: compare REAL-TIME walk-forward efficiency to the strategy's ESTABLISHED (from its WFA) walk-forward efficiency — a radically lower real-time WFE, not explainable by different market conditions, is likely evidence of overoptimization that slipped through; the remedy is reviewing the development process for a correctable error and redoing the evaluation on the corrected basis.

### 9.5 The Five Causes, Enumerated (Pardo Ch.13)

**1. Insufficient degrees of freedom.** Restated formula: `Rdf% = 100 × [1 − (Udf/Odf)]`. **Measuring DoF worked examples**: 2,080-data-point (2yr × OHLC) sample; a (10-day-high-avg + 50-day-low-avg) system consumes 62 DoF = 3% used, acceptable; a (50-day-close-avg + 150-day-close-avg) system consumes 152 DoF = 7.3% used, "still acceptable." **DoF + sample size + start-up overhead worked example**: a 10/50-day-close-MA + 5-rule system consumes 57 DoF (the shorter MA "free-rides" on the longer MA's already-counted data, adding only 1 DoF as a rule; the 5 other rules each add 1). On a 400-point (100-day OHLC) sample: 57/400=14.3% used, **85.7% remaining — flagged as too few** (the stated threshold: **<90% remaining degrees of freedom is generally considered too few**, warranting rejection or modification). On a 4,000-point (1,000-day) sample: 98.6% remaining — fine. **Start-up overhead worked example**: on the small 400-point sample, start-up overhead alone consumes 50% of available data (200/400 in that worked framing) — a separate cost from DoF-consumption proper. **Bias effect**: when data is scarce and trading pace is slow, start-up overhead disproportionately burdens LONGER parameter candidates in a scan, potentially skewing optimization toward shorter parameters for reasons unrelated to genuine edge (worked contrast: 3×8-day MA on 300 points consumes 13 DoF/8-day start-up; 25×100-day MA on the same 300 points consumes 102 DoF/100-day start-up AND trades far less often). **Five listed remedies**: use a larger test sample; allow for start-up overhead in planning; don't overparameterize; keep scan ranges strategy-and-sample appropriate; target ≥90% remaining DoF.

**2. Inadequate data and trade sample.** Reiterates 30–50 trades as an adequate stated minimum. Formula: `Standard Error % = 1/SqRt(Sample Size)`. Worked 3-way table: n=10 → 31.6% error; n=100 → 10.0%; n=1,000 → 3.0%. Trading pace interacts directly: a strategy trading 4x/year needs 12+ years of data for 50 trades (and typically also consumes more DoF/start-up overhead due to longer-period indicators); a strategy trading 2x/week needs only ~6 months for the same trade count (and typically lighter DoF/start-up burden).

**3. Incorrect optimization methods**:
- **3a. Overparameterization** — "the use of too many optimizable variables in a trading strategy," directly analogous to the too-many-variables problem in the overfit-forecasting-model parable. Remedy: "optimize only those parameters that are necessary" — more optimizable parameters require proportionally larger historical AND trade samples to remain statistically valid.
- **3b. Overscanning** — "a parameter scan uses a step size that is too small or steps over a range that is too large." **Two worked contrasting examples**: a short-term MA scanned 2–10 step 1 is fine (a 3→4 day step IS a 33% jump, near the practical minimum resolution); a long-term MA scanned 30–100 step 1 is "too fine" (a 90→91 day step is only 1.1% different, practically meaningless). **Explicit mechanism of harm, not just wasted compute — worked before/after comparison**: a 30–100-day-range-by-step-1 scan (639 total tests in a 2-variable worked example) needs ~40% (256) of its tests to show significant profit to be judged "robust" by the profitable-percentage heuristic — but because so many adjacent candidates are practically IDENTICAL (not independent, meaningfully different tests), the population is artificially padded with redundant "duplicate-ish" results, which can inflate the apparent profitable-percentage and depress the apparent standard deviation, producing a MISLEADINGLY robust-looking statistical profile. The properly-scanned version (30–100 step 5 = 135 tests, needing ~54 to hit the same 40% threshold) is far more trustworthy because its population consists of genuinely, practically distinct simulations.

**4. The Big Fish in a Small Pond Syndrome.** A distinct-but-related phenomenon, "not strictly" overfitting or pure procedural error, but "more there than anywhere else" — a specific risk for **longer-term, infrequently-trading strategies**. **Symptom**: an apparently robust-looking strategy whose trade sample is small (the "small pond") and whose total profit is dominated by one or two very large, long-duration winning trades (the "big fish"), spread over a long test history. **Remedy**: such a strategy should be REJECTED as overfit/statistically unsound UNLESS it (and similar neighboring parameter sets) passes a comprehensive Walk-Forward Analysis across a significantly diversified market basket — an unusually high robustness bar given this pattern's inherent fragility.

**5. Absence of a Walk-Forward Test.** "The easiest way to detect, and thereby avoid trading with, an overfit or otherwise unsound trading strategy is to perform a broad range of Walk-Forward Analyses upon it." Explicitly anticipates and rebuts the objection that this sounds like an oversimplified answer: "It is not oversimplification however. It is a statement that is the result of extensive research and use of this technique." WFA's dual diagnostic power: it directly reveals overfitting IN THE STRATEGY (bad strategy → fails to walk forward at all), AND it can reveal overfitting IN THE OPTIMIZATION PROCESS applied to an otherwise-good strategy (good strategy, poor procedure → also underperforms in WFA, via a low Walk-Forward Efficiency specifically) — the two failure modes are distinguished by whether the strategy makes ANY walk-forward profit at all (primary WFA signal) versus AT WHAT RATE/EFFICIENCY it does so relative to in-sample (WFE, the secondary/finer-grained signal).

## 10. Kaufman's Adding Reality: Costs, Data Integrity, Price Shocks

### 10.1 Costs (Kaufman Ch.21)
Faster trading systems have smaller per-trade losses but smaller per-trade profits and many more transactions — more exposed to slippage/commission drag. **Worked example**: at $25/side total cost, a 10-day-trend MA futures system loses about $50K of profit to costs, a 20-day system about $34K, a 40-day system about $23K — cost impact shrinks as calculation period lengthens (fewer trades). **Explicit warning against over-conservative cost assumptions**: using an unrealistically high assumed cost (example: $50/contract "just to be safe") turned every one of the 10–40-day results into a net loss — "using unrealistically large costs can cause you to eliminate a successful, fast-trading system."

**Cross-book agreement**: this is the same shape of finding Pardo gives in §2.3's worked slippage-significance contrast (10 trades/4yr vs. 200 trades/1yr) and that Hilpisch demonstrates repeatedly with real code (§12 below) — all three books independently converge on transaction costs as a first-order, not second-order, determinant of whether a backtest result survives contact with reality.

### 10.2 Data Integrity Checks (Kaufman Ch.21/22)
**Simple daily-data integrity checks** (explicit list, Ch.22): (1) outliers — data points >4% away from the previous data point (or a preset threshold); (2) missing dates — more than 4 consecutive days without data flagged as a potential problem; (3) open/close prices outside the high-low range are impossible and should be corrected/removed. **Additional intraday checks**: (4) frequency of data — compare average ticks per 5-minute interval against current frequency to spot silent gaps; (5) unusually large intraday price jumps that reverse within seconds indicate a data error or a large order filled away from market. **"Clean data" caveat**: a vendor's "clean data" has been corrected by someone using an unknown methodology — developing on clean data and then applying to live, dirty intraday data can turn theoretical profits into losses (a large number of additional trades from bad prices). **Recommended solution**: develop using the original, dirty data so the system is forced to be less sensitive to price noise.

### 10.3 Price Shocks — Definition, Decomposition, and Testing Implications (Kaufman Ch.22)
**Definition**: "usually unexpected, but always an unpredictable event." A price shock is operationally identified as **a 1-day trading range five times the size of the previous trading ranges, measured using Average True Range** (same operational definition given in Ch.21 and Ch.22).

**Core backtesting flaw, explicitly restated across both chapters**: historic backtesting does not identify shocks as a separate category but treats them as ordinary data, and parameter selection (by whatever performance criterion) systematically rewards designs that happened to profit from historical shocks — "you can be sure that selection has been the greatest beneficiary of these unpredictable price shocks," which "goes a long way toward explaining why out-of-sample and real trading are never as profitable as the simulated test results." Because an event were expected it would not be a shock by definition, "no one can profit from a price shock by clever planning, only by luck" — an open trade should never be assumed to be on the correct side of a shock more than 50% of the time, "and it may be far fewer." **Diagnostic**: scan trade history for unusually large single-day gains (shock days); IF more than 50% of a system's price-shock trades were profitable THEN the overall backtest returns are "unrealistic" and risk has been understated by an undeserved windfall. "If there are a number of large profits and no large losses, you can be sure that you have overfit the data." **Despite this weakness**, Kaufman states plainly "there are no other alternatives for validating a proposed trading strategy" besides back-testing — it remains necessary despite being "weakest" specifically in its handling of shocks.

**Six named historical price shocks analyzed** (Kaufman Ch.22): the Gorbachev abduction (Aug 1991); the British 1992 election (Major's surprise win); the Gulf War (Aug 1990–Feb 1991, incl. the Jan 17, 1991 invasion — futures opened near $30.80/bbl and closed at $20.50 the same day); 9/11 (2001); the Brexit vote (2016); the 2016 U.S. election. **Finding across these shocks**: mostly losses for both short-term (18-day) and long-term (80-day) trend traders; close-to-close losses generally larger than close-to-open losses (positions that did not continue in the shock direction mostly closed near the open, and even profitable cases were not improved by the close). **Conclusion**: "exit as soon as possible" after a shock. **Alternative (holding) analysis**: in 4 of 7 cases, prices recovered all or nearly all of the shock within 2–8 days — giving holding through a shock a **71% chance of recovery, "if you weren't wiped out by the price shock"** — the central practical problem being loss *magnitude* given futures' high leverage.

**System disconnect during a crisis**: worked 1987-crash example — 50-, 100-, and 200-day moving averages all turned down the day of the largest drop, generating a sell signal that (executed the next day) would have caught the lowest price of the move; but by the time each trendline eventually turned back UP (mid-December 1987, mid-February 1988, early June 1988 respectively for the 50/100/200-day MAs), prices had already recovered 22%/24%/30% — "these moving averages become 'out of phase' with price after a shock... they are catching up to an event long past." **Contrast case**: the 2008 subprime collapse is explicitly classified as **not** a price shock but an "evolving event" (news appeared slowly, steadily, clearly pointing to disaster) — trend-following futures traders as an industry posted their best year on record with no disconnect. **General recommendation**: crisis management needs special rules for unusual circumstances distinct from ordinary trend logic; the crisis-mode switch can be triggered on volume/volatility, then ended using the same criteria.

### 10.4 Synthetic and Monte Carlo Test Data (Kaufman Ch.21)
The "ultimate answer to getting more test data" is to create it. **Rearranging Data (Monte Carlo)**: scan real data for the period length giving the lowest autocorrelation between adjacent segments, then randomly re-order those segments into a new series. **Explicit caution**: mixing segments risks separating a bull run from its inevitable decline — "a system can survive a large loss if it follows a large gain, but may not survive if that loss occurs randomly"; suggests instead separating at low-volatility boundaries. **Unique/mathematically-generated data**: begins from random numbers; a plain random series resembles many (especially sideways) price series, but real prices are not normally distributed and have much longer directional runs — a sophisticated synthetic series can be engineered with a chosen trend/noise mix (example cited: 15% trend/85% noise), seasonal bias, and stock/futures-typical distribution. **Wolberg's method** (cited): skewed price distribution plus a noise component that is itself a (nonlinear) function of the trend; validation via **Fraction Same Sign** (the percentage of synthetic records where predicted and actual data move in the same direction).

**Monte Carlo Sampling for parameter search** (a distinct application from synthetic-data generation, Kaufman Ch.21): attributed to von Neumann and Ulam. Illustrated via the "lake area" analogy — overlay a fine grid, draw many random coordinate pairs, classify each as inside/outside the target region; the fraction landing inside, times total area, estimates the target's area. **Applied to parameter search**: with, say, 8 parameters × 10 possible values each, randomly draw one value per parameter to form a "chromosome," record performance, increment a per-value usage counter; continue until every individual parameter-value has been used a "reasonable" number of times (example: 20–100) — a worked minimum of ~8,000 total tests rather than the full 10^8 (100 million) combinatorial space. **Caveat, explicitly conceded**: academics will argue the statistical properties (e.g., variance) of this smaller random sample may differ meaningfully from the full population. **Confidence check for both GA and MCS**: if repeated independent runs converge on similar best results, that is evidence the smaller sample fairly represents the full space.

### 10.5 Merloti's Fuzzy Expert System — Explicit Warning Against Trusting Synthetic-Data Backtest Results (Kaufman Ch.20)
Paulo Merloti's "A Fuzzy Expert System as a Stock Trading Advisor" (Kaufman Ch.20) tested a fuzzy-logic expert system that mapped a Moving Average Divergence indicator plus a volatility classification into buy/sell-sizing decisions (buy more / no trade / buy few, mirrored on the sell side), across three rule-configuration tests, on **artificial/synthetic price data** (a random component combined with a `t mod 4` cyclical term). Result: pure mean reversion (Test 2, ignoring the indicator entirely) far outperformed the two indicator-driven tests — an artifact of how the synthetic data happened to be constructed, not evidence the underlying trading logic was sound.

**The warning itself (Kaufman's direct quote of the finding, generalizable to all synthetic-data testing, not specific to this one expert system)**: "No matter how cleverly it's constructed, the computer finds and exploits the components of synthetic data, in this case the random price movement, in other cases, the trend or cycle. **You cannot yet rely on the test results from synthetic data to show you if you have a successful strategy.**" This directly qualifies the "Rearranging Data (Monte Carlo)" and "Unique/mathematically-generated data" methods described above in §10.4: synthetic/Monte-Carlo-generated series are useful for *stress-testing* an already-validated strategy (checking robustness to reordering, generating more test volume) but the source explicitly cautions against treating strong performance *on synthetic data alone* as validation that a strategy has genuine edge — a computer-driven optimizer will find and exploit whatever artificial regularity the generation process happened to leave behind (a random seed's directional runs, a hidden cyclical term, etc.), the same way it exploits noise in real historical data (cross-ref §9's overfitting-to-noise material).

## 11. Chan's Backtesting-Bias Chapter: Look-Ahead, Survivorship, Data-Snooping, Costs, Metrics

### 11.1 Purpose of Backtesting, and Why Even a Published Strategy Must Be Re-Tested (Chan Ch.3)
Even when a strategy is fully described elsewhere with reported historical performance, Chan argues you must still backtest it yourself, because doing so: (1) confirms you have understood and can faithfully reproduce the strategy for implementation; (2) serves as an independent replication check, similar to replication in medical/scientific research, catching errors in the original research; (3) allows experimentation with variations to refine/improve the strategy.

### 11.2 Survivorship Bias — A Worked Demonstration of Its Magnitude (Chan Ch.3)
Databases free of survivorship bias are expensive and often unaffordable for startups. **Mitigations**: (1) start collecting your own point-in-time data going forward; (2) backtest on more recent data to reduce the number of missing (delisted) stocks in a biased database. **Worked Example 3.3** (toy "buy 10 lowest-priced stocks of the 1,000 largest by market cap, hold 1 year" strategy, year 2001→2002, during the dot-com bust): with survivorship-bias-free data, 8 of 10 picked stocks were delisted within the year, **actual portfolio return = −42%**; with survivorship-biased data (delisted stocks silently excluded from the selectable universe), a different (all-survived) set of 10 stocks was "selected," producing a **fictitious return of +388%**. This starkly illustrates how survivorship bias can invert a strategy's true (loss) result into an apparently spectacular (gain) backtest result.

### 11.3 High/Low Data Reliability (Chan Ch.3)
Daily high/low prices are **noisier** and less reliable than open/close — a limit order placed exactly at the recorded high/low might not actually have filled (small/thin execution, off-routed market, bad/uncleaned tick). A backtest relying on high/low data is less reliable than one relying on open/close, since high/low errors almost always inflate backtest returns. **Data quality check recommended**: compute daily returns from OHLC data, flag days with returns ~4 standard deviations from the mean, verify each extreme is corroborated by news or a broad market-wide move on the same day — otherwise the data point is likely erroneous.

### 11.4 Performance Measurement — Sharpe Ratio and Drawdown (Chan Ch.3)
Chan argues **Sharpe ratio and drawdown** are the two most useful standardized performance measures — more so than average annualized return, because raw return figures require many additional disclosures to be comparable (capital denominator conventions, leveraged vs. unleveraged, moving-average vs. point-in-time equity denominator). Quoting Sharpe ratio and drawdown avoids most of these ambiguities.

**Sharpe ratio subtleties**: for **dollar-neutral portfolios**, do NOT subtract the risk-free rate when computing excess returns (the portfolio is self-financing — short-sale proceeds fund the long side, financing-cost spread is small/negligible, and the margin balance earns credit interest ≈ risk-free rate, so the risk-free rate cancels out algebraically). For **long-only day-trading (no overnight holding)**, similarly no need to subtract the risk-free rate (no financing cost incurred). **General rule**: subtract the risk-free rate only if the strategy incurs financing cost (holds a net long/short position overnight without a matching hedge).

**Annualizing the Sharpe ratio**: `Annualized Sharpe Ratio = √NT × (Sharpe Ratio Based on Period T)`, where NT = number of trading periods T in a year (annualizing average return multiplies by periods/year; annualizing standard deviation multiplies by √(periods/year), assuming serially uncorrelated returns, per Sharpe 1994). **Worked common-mistake warning**: for an intraday hourly strategy held only during NYSE hours (9:30–16:00 ET), NT = 252 trading days × 6.5 trading hours = **1,638**, NOT 252 × 24 = 6,048.

**Worked Example 3.5**: max drawdown ≈10.53%, max drawdown duration ≈497 trading days, computed via a high-watermark/running-drawdown-counter procedure (full MATLAB `calculateMaxDD` function given in the source).

### 11.6 Data-Snooping Bias — Chan's Mitigation Toolkit (Chan Ch.3)
Recap: overfitting parameters to historical noise inflates backtest performance relative to real future performance — especially serious in finance due to limited independent data (high-frequency data is abundant but only useful for high-frequency models; only the last ~10 years of data is generally considered suitable for building predictive models even where longer history exists; regime shifts can further shorten the usable window). ### 11.7 Transaction Costs — A Worked Demonstration Reversing a Landmark Result (Chan Ch.3)
**Worked Example 3.7** (mean-reverting model with/without transaction costs, replicating Khandani & Lo, MIT): strategy buys stocks with the worst previous 1-day return, shorts stocks with the best previous 1-day return (cross-sectional reversal), on the S&P 500 universe. Without transaction costs, the original authors reported Sharpe = 4.47 (2006); Chan's own replication on the S&P 500 (large-cap) universe obtains only **Sharpe ≈ 0.25** — attributed to the fact that most of the original strategy's return came from **small/microcap stocks**, not large caps. Applying a 5-basis-point one-way transaction cost (a trade = a buy or a short, not a round trip) collapses the Sharpe ratio to **≈ −3.19** — "very unprofitable." **Worked Example 3.8**: simply changing position updates to occur at market **open** instead of close (all else unchanged) raised the pre-cost Sharpe to **4.43** and the post-cost Sharpe to a **profitable 0.78** — a striking illustration that execution-timing details, not just the core signal, can determine whether a strategy survives realistic costs.

### 11.8 Strategy Refinement Discipline (Chan Ch.3)
Any refinement to improve training-set performance must also improve (or at least not harm) test-set performance, mirroring the data-snooping mitigation principle. Many well-known simple strategies (e.g., basic pair trading) still work but with diminishing returns as competition erodes them; minor, less-well-known variations can restore edge, but **refinements should have a basis in fundamental economics or a well-studied market phenomenon rather than arbitrary trial-and-error rules** — otherwise "data-snooping bias looms." Explicit practical concession: "it is acceptable to skip some precautions initially, for a quick read on whether a strategy is worth deeper examination, given time/resource constraints — some problems only surface after months of paper or live trading, and issues can always be revisited after a model goes live."

### 11.9 Split/Dividend Price-Adjustment Mechanics (Chan Ch.3, Example 3.2)
Verifying split/dividend adjustment via **multiplier, not subtraction**, is already noted in the checklist at §15 below — this subsection gives the underlying formulas and worked example in full.
- **Split adjustment**: for an N:1 split with ex-date T (N usually 2, can be fractional for reverse splits), multiply all prices *before* T by 1/N.
- **Dividend adjustment**: for a $d/share dividend with ex-date T, multiply all prices before T by `(Close(T-1) − d) / Close(T-1)`.
- **Why multiplication, not subtraction (explicit reasoning)**: multiplying preserves historical *daily returns* across the adjustment point, whereas subtracting $d instead would preserve daily *price changes* but not daily *returns* — multiplication is how Yahoo! Finance (the most common convention) does it.
- **Explicit warning**: unadjusted data will show a spurious price "drop" at the ex-date's market open beyond normal fluctuation, which can trigger erroneous trading signals.
- **Worked Example 3.2 (IGE ETF)**: a 2:1 split (multiplier 0.5) combined with 9 subsequent dividends (aggregate dividend multiplier 0.976773) yields a combined multiplier of **0.488386** applied to all pre-split-date prices — verified to match Yahoo!'s published adjusted closes to two decimal places.
- **Practical recommendation**: obtain already-adjusted data where possible; otherwise a separate splits/dividends database must be used to adjust manually, which Chan describes as "tedious and error-prone."

### 11.10 High-Frequency-Trading-Specific Backtesting and Execution Challenges (Chan Ch.7)
Distinct from the general backtesting-bias material above, Chan's Ch.7 flags challenges specific to strategies that trade at high frequency (his own definition: any strategy not holding a position overnight — see `01_market_structure.md` for the full HFT section, including the law-of-large-numbers argument for why high frequency drives high Sharpe ratios). The backtesting/execution-specific half of that material, relevant to this file's scope:
- **Transaction costs dominate at this frequency** — without incorporating them, "the simplest strategies may seem to work," a sharper version of the general transaction-cost-reverses-results finding already documented in §11.7 and §14.6.
- **Last-price data is insufficient** — bid/ask/last quote data is needed to assess profitability of executing on bid vs. ask; sometimes full historical order-book data is needed, a materially heavier data requirement than the OHLC-sufficiency assumption underlying most of this file's other backtesting guidance.
- **"Quite often, the only true test for such strategies is to run it in real-time unless one has an extremely sophisticated simulator"** — an explicit acknowledgment that backtesting itself has a lower ceiling of reliability for genuinely high-frequency strategies than for the daily-bar strategies most of this knowledge base's validation methodology assumes.
- Execution speed (not just the strategy signal) can account for a large share of actual P&L; professional high-frequency firms co-locate servers near exchanges to minimize microsecond delays — a real-world gap Chan states "is not by any means easy for an independent trader to achieve in the beginning."

### 11.11 Diagnosing Live-Trading Underperformance — Chan's Recommended Investigation Order (Chan Ch.5)
When live results disappoint relative to backtest, Chan gives an explicit, ordered checklist rather than jumping straight to "the model is broken" or "the market changed" — the order matters because the first two causes are mundane and cheap to rule out, while the last two require harder, less certain judgment calls:
1. **Bugs in the automated trading system's software** — the most basic, most common, and cheapest-to-fix cause; rule this out first.
2. **Do the ATS-generated trades actually match the backtest-generated trades**, given the same input data? A divergence here (beyond expected timing/rounding) points back to (1) or to a look-ahead-bias/implementation mismatch between the backtest and live code paths.
3. **Are realized execution costs higher than the backtest assumed?** Revisit the transaction-cost minimization guidance in §9 of `10_position_sizing.md` (order-sizing, fourth-root market-cap capital scaling, avoiding sub-$5 names) before concluding the strategy itself has failed.
4. **Are illiquid names causing excess market impact** beyond what the backtest's simplified cost model captured?

**Only after ruling out all four of the above** should a trader consider the two harder-to-diagnose, more serious explanations: **data-snooping bias** (diagnostic: strip out as many rules/parameters as possible — if backtest performance "completely falls apart," data-snooping was likely present and a new strategy is needed; if performance holds up reasonably, the live shortfall may just be normal variance) or a genuine **regime shift** (see `07_market_regimes.md` §5 for the decimalization/uptick-rule examples). Chan is explicit that distinguishing bad luck from a genuine regime shift or data-snooping artifact "can only [be] believe[d]," not proven with certainty — no algorithm is given for a definitive determination.

## 12. Hilpisch: Vectorized vs. Event-Based Backtesting Architecture, ML Discipline, and Documented Gaps

### 12.1 Vectorized Backtesting (Hilpisch Ch.4)
**When appropriate** (explicit guidance): (1) simple trading strategies — has limits for complex strategy modeling but works well for many popular simple strategies; (2) interactive strategy exploration — few lines of code suffice for first results, different parameter combos easily tested; (3) visualization as a major goal — lends itself to quick, appealing plots; (4) comprehensive backtesting programs — fast execution allows testing many parameter combinations quickly.

**Mechanics**: NumPy's `ndarray`-based vectorization and pandas' column-wise `DataFrame` vectorization produce code "close to mathematical notation" for vector/matrix operations. Boolean/conditional vectorization (`df['a'] > 5`, comparisons between columns) is central to strategy signal generation throughout the chapter.

**Canonical vectorized-backtest recipe** (reused throughout Ch.4–6): (1) compute rolling indicators (e.g., `data['SMA1'] = data['price'].rolling(42).mean()`); (2) generate signal/position via `np.where(...)`; (3) drop NaN rows preceding full-window availability; (4) compute log returns `np.log(price/price.shift(1))`; (5) **critical detail — shift the position by 1 bar to avoid look-ahead**: `data['strategy'] = data['position'].shift(1) * data['returns']` — the position decided using information known at the end of bar t is applied to the return realized on bar t+1; (6) compare cumulative performance via `.cumsum().apply(np.exp)`; (7) annualize return/vol assuming 252 trading days/year; (8) compute max drawdown via a running cummax/drawdown-tracking procedure; (9) compute max drawdown *duration* by finding dates where drawdown returns to zero and taking the max gap between such dates.

**Worked SMA example** (EUR/USD, SMA1=42, SMA2=252, 2010–2019): strategy cumulative log return +0.253 (gross factor 1.288, +28.8%) vs. benchmark buy-and-hold −0.177 (0.838, −16.2%) — the strategy outperformed a *losing* buy-and-hold; max drawdown ≈17.8 percentage points, longest drawdown period 596 days. Brute-force `scipy.optimize.brute` grid optimization over SMA1∈[30,50] step 2 and SMA2∈[200,300] step 2 found an optimum at SMA1=48, SMA2=238, gross performance 1.5 (150%) — **the book's own text flags the implicit risk here**: optimizing SMA parameters over the full historical sample by brute force and reporting the best combination "is a textbook setup for data snooping/overfitting."

**Momentum strategy — sensitivity to lookback window, explicit warning**: the simplest form (`position = sign(returns)`, i.e., yesterday's return sign only) significantly underperforms; a 3-day rolling-mean version performs much better than either the 1-day or a 2-day version — the book explicitly states the strategy's performance "is quite sensitive to the time window parameter," an admitted first hint of overfitting risk baked into a simple-seeming rule.

**Transaction costs can completely reverse momentum results** (`MomVectorBacktester`, gold XAU=, 2010–2019, momentum=3, $10,000 initial): no transaction costs → ending value $20,797.87, **outperformance of $7,395.53**; with 0.1% proportional transaction costs → ending value $10,749.40, **underperformance of −$2,652.93** — a mere 10bps per trade completely erases the outperformance and turns it into a relative loss, because short-lookback time-series momentum trades frequently.

**Mean reversion is comparatively more cost-resilient in this book's examples**: `MRVectorBacktester` (GLD, SMA=43, threshold=7.5, 0.1% costs, $10,000 initial) → ending value $13,542.15, outperformance of $646.21 vs. benchmark **even after realistic transaction costs** — contrasted implicitly against the momentum example's total cost-driven reversal, attributed to mean reversion's lower turnover from its flat/neutral holding periods.

**Chapter's own explicit self-critique on data snooping** (a load-bearing section, Ch.4): "strategies, parameters, data sets, and algorithms used are sometimes arbitrarily chosen and sometimes purposefully chosen to make a certain point... it is more exciting... to see examples that show 'good results,' even if they might not generalize." Data snooping is defined, per White (2000, quoted): "occurs when a given set of data is used more than once for purposes of inference or model selection." Overfitting, per the CFA/Man Institute (quoted): "is when a model describes noise rather than signal. The model may have good performance on the data on which it was tested, but little or no predictive power on new data... There is a cost associated with overfitting—an overfitted strategy will underperform in the future." Cites Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2015) — modern computational power makes it "nearly trivial to test millions... of alternative investment strategies," and given weak signal-to-noise ratios in markets, calibration frequently "profit[s] from past noise rather than future signal." **Explicit, load-bearing disclaimer**: "if a trading strategy in this book is shown to perform well given a certain data set, combination of parameters, and maybe a specific machine learning algorithm, this neither constitutes any kind of recommendation for the particular configuration nor allows [one] to draw more general conclusions about the quality and performance potential of the strategy configuration at hand."

### 12.2 Machine-Learning Backtesting Discipline (Hilpisch Ch.5)
**Hit ratio vs. P&L disconnect — a repeated theme**: a strategy correctly timing the best/worst days can outperform even with <50% hit ratio, and a >50% hit-ratio strategy can still underperform if it misses the rare large moves — market timing quality matters as much as raw accuracy. Demonstrated concretely: a DNN classifier for EUR/USD direction achieves only ~51.5% in-sample and ~50.5% out-of-sample accuracy (barely above chance) — **yet** both in-sample and out-of-sample strategy P&L outperformed the passive benchmark, because the strategy's occasional correct calls on large moves compensated for near-random overall accuracy.

**ML-specific hygiene practices, consistently applied** (see §4.4 above for full detail): out-of-sample splitting via explicit `run_strategy(start_in, end_in, start_out, end_out, ...)` parameters, framed explicitly as a defense against data snooping/overfitting; training-set-only feature normalization; date-based (not random-shuffle) train/test splits; all engineered features `.shift(1)`'d to prevent look-ahead.

**Repeated warning across all three ML strategy families (linear regression, logistic regression/scikit-learn, Keras DNN)**: "Applying sophisticated machine learning techniques to stock market prediction often yields promising results early on... quite often such stellar performances are due to a mix of simplifying assumptions and also due to an overfitting of the prediction model." Testing out-of-sample and adding transaction costs "often shows that the performance... 'suddenly' trails the base instrument... or turns to a net loss." A GDX logistic-regression example with 0.25% proportional transaction costs shows out-of-sample performance "materially diminished" versus the no-cost case, though still shown as a net gain in the specific configuration presented.

### 12.3 Event-Based Backtesting — Why and How (Hilpisch Ch.6)
**Limitations of vectorized backtesting that motivate event-based backtesting** (explicit, three reasons): (1) **look-ahead bias** — vectorized backtesting operates on the complete dataset at once, not respecting that real data arrives incrementally; (2) **simplification** — vectorization (based on relative returns) cannot model fixed transaction costs, fixed per-trade amounts, or non-divisibility of instruments (e.g., whole shares only); (3) **non-recursiveness** — algorithms depending on evolving state (cumulative P&L to date, other path-dependent statistics) cannot be expressed via vectorization.

**Advantages of event-based backtesting** (explicit): incremental (mirrors real trading, tick/quote/bar-by-bar); realistic modeling (full freedom for event-triggered processes); path dependency (straightforward running max/min/P&L tracking); reusability (shared base functionality via OOP); close to trading (components sometimes reusable directly in live/automated trading code).

**`BacktestBase`/`BacktestLongOnly`/`BacktestLongShort` class hierarchy** — explicit, self-disclosed simplifications (boxed warnings in the source, not left implicit): **no liquidity check** is performed (the class allows unlimited "credit" — an "economic simplification"); certain methods assume at least one of `units`/`amount` is provided with no guard against neither being set (a "technical simplification").

**Costs flip the ranking of strategies — a repeated, load-bearing empirical finding, this time in event-based (not vectorized) form** (AAPL.O, 2010–2019, $10,000 initial):

| Strategy | No costs: Net Perf% | With $10 fixed + 1% proportional costs: Net Perf% |
|---|---|---|
| SMA (42,252) | +462.05% | +419.60% |
| Momentum (60 days) | **+1267.17%** | +280.74% |
| Mean Reversion (SMA=50, thr=5) | +439.08% | +53.75% |

**Key finding**: without transaction costs, momentum dramatically outperforms SMA and mean reversion. **With transaction costs, the ranking flips** — SMA (fewer trades) outperforms momentum (many trades), and mean reversion is hit hardest of all, dropping from +439% to just +53.75%. Boxed lesson: beyond hit ratio and market timing, there is "even a 'third side': the number of trades triggered by a strategy. A strategy that demands a higher frequency of trades has to bear higher transaction costs that easily eat up an alleged outperformance."

**Adding short-selling does not necessarily improve results — a counterintuitive, explicit finding**: the `BacktestLongShort` comparison table shows all three strategies performing *worse*, both without and after transaction costs, than their long-only counterparts, with the momentum and mean-reversion configurations even flipping to **net losses / a position of debt** (momentum: −31.17%; mean reversion: −151.11%) once costs are included. "Reality shows that this is not necessarily true. All strategies perform worse both without and after transaction costs... Some configurations even pile up net losses or even a position of debt." A boxed CFD-margin-mechanics explanation is given for how leveraged trading can produce this literal debt outcome (a $100,000 position at 20x leverage needs only $5,000 equity; a 10% adverse move wipes that equity out entirely, absent an automatic margin stop-out, requiring the trader to post additional funds to cover losses).

### 12.5 The Online Algorithm Pattern: Offline→Online Transformation (Hilpisch Ch.7, Ch.8, Ch.10)

Named in the source extraction's own Master_Summary.md as "the single reusable architecture" connecting real-time sockets (Ch.7), broker streaming (Ch.8), and ML live deployment (Ch.10) — this is Hilpisch's live-trading analogue of the vectorized-vs-event-based look-ahead distinction in §12.1/12.3 above, and is otherwise undocumented anywhere else in this five-book knowledge base.

**Offline vs. online algorithm framing (Hilpisch Ch.7)**: an **offline algorithm** (e.g., sorting, or any backtest run on a complete historical dataset) sees the whole dataset upfront. An **online algorithm** operates on incrementally-arriving data, knowing only current/past state, with no foresight — explicitly stated as the realistic model for any live trading algorithm. Deploying a backtested ("offline") strategy therefore always requires an explicit transformation into an "online" form.

**The four-step live signal-generation pattern (`OnlineAlgorithm.py`, Ch.7)**:
1. **Collect incoming ticks** into a growing DataFrame (one row per tick, indexed by arrival time).
2. **Resample** to a fixed bar length: `dr = df.resample('5s', label='right').last()`.
3. **Compute the signal** on the resampled bars (e.g., a rolling-momentum sign).
4. **Critical anti-look-ahead technique — drop or skip the still-incomplete current bar**: because `label='right'` resampling labels an in-progress bar with a not-yet-reached future timestamp, the code must read the **second-to-last** value (`dr['momentum'].iloc[-2]`), never the last (`iloc[-1]`), which represents an incomplete, still-forming bar. The `MomentumTrader`/`MLTrader` classes (Ch.8, Ch.10) apply the same discipline via an equivalent operation, resampling then explicitly dropping the final row (`.last().ffill().iloc[:-1]`) before computing any signal from it. **This is a concrete, reusable, code-level defense against look-ahead bias specific to live/streaming bar construction** — distinct from (though motivated by the same underlying concern as) the vectorized `.shift(1)` position-lag technique in §12.1.

**Order placement is explicitly deferred, not implemented, in the pure-signal Ch.7 version** — the chapter only prints "Long/Short market position" with a comment to take action later; actual order placement is added only once a real broker API is introduced (Ch.8/9, §10 in `11_risk_management.md`).

**Production hand-off pattern (Ch.10)**: a trained offline model is persisted together with the normalization parameters required to reproduce its exact feature pipeline live — `pickle.dump({'model': model, 'mu': mu, 'std': std}, open('algorithm.pkl','wb'))`. The live `MLTrader` class loads this bundle, recomputes the same rolling features tick-by-tick, applies the *stored* training-time `mu`/`std` (not freshly computed live statistics — critical correctness point, consistent with the training-set-only normalization discipline in §12.2), builds the resampled/dropped-last-bar feature row per the four-step pattern above, and calls `self.model.predict(features)[0]`.

**Position-flip order sizing (reused across Ch.8/Ch.10)**: `(1 - position) * units` — a single formula elegantly handles enter/hold/flip without branching (1 unit sized if flat, 2 units if flipping from the opposite side). Full mechanics and account-mechanics context in `11_risk_management.md` §10.

**Self-disclosed, unreconciled gap**: the live `MLTrader` example uses a materially shorter live bar length (5s/2s) than the model's original backtested/trained bar length (10-minute Oanda bars) — an inconsistency the book itself never addresses (see `17_author_disagreements.md` §8, `18_common_failure_modes.md` §8).

### 12.6 ML Strategy Architectures: Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, DNN, and AdaBoost (Hilpisch Ch.5, Ch.10)

Four of Hilpisch's seven documented strategy families are ML-based and share a common lag/classification framing not otherwise detailed in this knowledge base beyond a one-line mention in `08_entries.md` §9.

**Lag-construction technique (the shared building block for all four families)**: `for lag in range(1, lags+1): data[f'lag_{lag}'] = data['price'].shift(lag)` — past N values of a series used as regressors/features to predict the next value.

**(1) Linear regression on price levels**: regressing EUR/USD price *level* on 5 lagged price levels gives coefficients heavily weighted on `lag_1` (~0.986) and near-zero elsewhere — interpreted by the author as empirical support for the random-walk hypothesis ("today's price is the best predictor for tomorrow's price in a least-squares sense").

**(2) Linear regression on returns, and the hit-ratio metric**: regressing on 5 lagged log returns to predict the next return produces predicted magnitudes that track true returns poorly. **Hit ratio** = `sign(actual_return * predicted_return)` (positive product = correct directional call). Regressing on raw lagged returns: hit ratio ≈49.9% (coin-flip). Regressing lagged returns directly against `sign(return)` as the target instead of the raw return: hit ratio improves to ≈51.9% — evidence that directly targeting the sign, not the magnitude, is the better-specified prediction problem for trading purposes. **Boxed warning (repeated theme, see §12.2)**: hit ratio alone is not sufficient — a strategy correctly timing the best/worst days can outperform even with <50% hit ratio, and a >50% hit-ratio strategy can still underperform if it misses the rare large moves.

**scikit-learn's three-step API pattern (explicitly named the master pattern)**: (1) model selection — instantiate an estimator object; (2) fitting — `model.fit(X, y)`; (3) prediction — `model.predict(X)`. This unified API is what lets `LinearRegression` and `LogisticRegression` be swapped with minimal code change.

**(3) Logistic regression classification (GLD example)**: 3 lags, in-sample hit ratio ≈53.3% (up from linear regression's ~50%), but gross strategy performance was actually *lower* than the linear-regression version in the same run; 5 lags in-sample: hit ratio decreases slightly to ≈53.1% but gross performance *increases* — reinforcing that hit ratio and P&L are not tightly correlated. **`LRVectorBacktester`/`ScikitVectorBacktester` class architecture**: both bake in explicit in-sample/out-of-sample date-range splitting (`run_strategy(start_in, end_in, start_out, end_out, lags=...)`) as a first-class parameter, framed by the author as the primary defense against data snooping/overfitting (cross-ref §4.4 above); both apply proportional transaction costs only on position-change bars (`prediction.diff().fillna(0) != 0`).

**(4) DNN classification (Keras `Sequential`, EUR/USD direction)**: architecture `Dense(64, relu, input_shape=(lags,))` → `Dense(64, relu)` → `Dense(1, sigmoid)`; `optimizer=Adam(learning_rate=0.0001)`, `loss='binary_crossentropy'`. **Reproducibility technique** (necessary because Keras/TF training has multiple independent randomness sources): `os.environ['PYTHONHASHSEED']='0'` plus `random.seed()`, `np.random.seed()`, `tf.random.set_seed()`, bundled in a `set_seeds()` helper. **Training-set-only Gaussian normalization**: `mu, std` computed on the training data only, then applied to both train and test (avoids look-ahead/leakage from test-set statistics). Results: 51.5% train / 50.5% test accuracy — both barely above chance — **yet both outperformed the passive benchmark** in cumulative P&L, again illustrating the hit-ratio-vs-P&L disconnect. **Enriched-feature result**: adding momentum/volatility/distance features (8 features total: 5 lags + `momentum` + `volatility` + `distance`, each explicitly `.shift(1)`'d to avoid look-ahead) lifted in-sample accuracy to 56.5% and out-of-sample to 55.1%; in-sample cumulative return jumped from 1.32 to 2.70 while out-of-sample only moved from 1.11 to 1.14 — the far larger in-sample jump than out-of-sample jump is an implicit overfitting signal the book itself does not call out explicitly.

**(5) AdaBoost — the book's single most complete "idea to cloud" pipeline (Ch.10)**: `DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, min_samples_leaf=15)` base estimator wrapped in `AdaBoostClassifier(n_estimators=15)` — explicit motivation: boosting an ensemble of weak classifiers is "supposed to be less prone to overfitting" (direct callback to the Ch.4 data-snooping/overfitting discussion). Feature set: 6 features (return, rolling volatility, rolling momentum sign, SMA, rolling min, rolling max) × 6 lags = 36 columns, on Oanda EUR_USD 10-minute bars. 70/30 **sequential** (not random) train/test split; training-set-only normalization (same discipline as the DNN). Results: training accuracy 80.5%, test (out-of-sample) accuracy 56.7% — a large in-sample/out-of-sample gap, itself an implicit illustration of overfitting risk even with a boosting ensemble. **Framing**: because accuracy here equals the strategy's hit ratio, and it is "significantly greater than 50%," the author suggests this "might indicate — in the context of the Kelly criterion — a statistical edge compared to a random walk setting" (stated as a possibility, not a proven claim).

The full pipeline this strategy is walked through, end to end — the book's own most complete backtest→deployment narrative:
1. **Vectorized backtest with realistic costs**: `strategy = position * return`; 77 trades over the test period; proportional cost (`ptc ≈ 0.000106`, ~1.06bps, derived from the average bid-ask spread) subtracted only on trade bars. Results: benchmark 0.990 (a loss), strategy no-cost 1.016, strategy with cost 1.008 — the strategy still beats a losing buy-and-hold after costs, but costs visibly erode roughly half the raw edge.
2. **Optimal leverage via Kelly, applied to the strategy's own (not the instrument's) mean/variance**: full analysis and worked S&P 500 comparator number in `10_position_sizing.md` §2.6. For the AdaBoost strategy itself (with costs), the same formula gives optimal leverage **above 50** — noted as "feasible, even for retail traders" via CFD brokers.
3. **Risk analysis at leverage=30** (see `11_risk_management.md` §7 and `13_indicator_reference.md`'s VaR entry for the full risk-metric definitions cross-referenced here): initial equity 3,333 EUR → 100,000 EUR notional; max drawdown 511.38 EUR (~15% of equity); longest drawdown period ≈7.33 hours; VaR via `scipy.stats.scoreatpercentile` at 6 confidence levels (90%–99.99%), ranging ~63–163 EUR at the native 10-minute horizon, **increasing at every confidence level when resampled to a 1-hour horizon** (e.g., 99%: 132→245 EUR) — a concrete demonstration that VaR scales with the chosen time horizon.
4. **Model persistence**: `pickle.dump({'model': model, 'mu': mu, 'std': std}, open('algorithm.pkl','wb'))` — the deployable artifact bundles the trained model with its normalization parameters, since both are required together for correct live-feature preparation (see §12.5 above).
5. **Online-algorithm live deployment**: transformation into the `MLTrader` class per the four-step pattern in §12.5, with the self-disclosed, unreconciled 10-minute-backtest vs. 5s/2s-live bar-length mismatch noted there.

## 13. Vince's Caveats: Small Samples, Dependency-Testing Error, and Degrees of Freedom

Vince's *Mathematics of Money Management* is not primarily a backtesting-methodology book, but Chapter 1 contains testing-adjacent statistical caveats directly relevant to this file's scope — particularly around how much confidence a small trade sample can support, and the asymmetric cost of misjudging dependency in a trade sequence.

### 13.1 Trade-Dependency Testing and Its Statistical Demands (Vince Ch.1)
The **Runs Test** (formula `Z = (N*(R-.5)-X) / ((X*(X-N))/(N-1))^(1/2)`, where N=total trades, R=total runs, X=2*W*L, W/L=win/loss counts) determines whether a system's win/loss sequence shows more/fewer streaks than random chance would produce — but **requires a minimum of 30 closed trades** to be validly approximated by the Normal distribution. Statisticians generally want confidence ≥ high-90s to assume genuine dependency exists; some require >99%, some accept ≥95.45% (2 SD) as a floor. **Vince's own stated finding, an important caveat on real trading-system dependency claims**: "rarely, if ever, will a real trading system show confidence >95.45%; most are under 90%" — even 90–95.45% confidence is "not exactly a nugget of gold." You need to exceed 95.45% as a bare minimum to justify capitalizing on any apparent dependency.

A complementary **serial correlation** test (Pearson correlation of each trade's P&L against the immediately preceding trade's P&L) tests dependency of trade *sizes* (not just win/loss sign sequence) — Vince's worked 21-trade example yields r=.011322, described as "hardly indicative of anything, but this is typical for most real trading systems." Rule of thumb: high positive correlation (≥.25) generally means big wins are seldom followed by big losses (and vice versa); correlation below −.25 to −.30 implies big losses tend to be followed by big wins (exploitable in principle). Vince recommends **out-of-sample testing of any detected dependency**: split the data, check the dependency holds in both halves, to avoid false-positive dependency conclusions (Vince Ch.1).

### 13.2 The Asymmetric Cost of Dependency-Testing Error (Vince Ch.1)
Absent strong dependency evidence, traders should assume trade outcomes are **independent** of prior outcomes. Vince frames this via classical hypothesis-testing error types: **Type I error** = rejecting a hypothesis that should be accepted (here: assuming NO dependency when dependency actually exists → penalty = forgone profits). **Type II error** = accepting a hypothesis that should be rejected (here: assuming dependency exists when it doesn't → penalty = actual trading losses). Vince's reasoning: forgone profits (Type I) are a lesser penalty than actual losses (Type II), so "unless there is absolutely overwhelming evidence of dependency, you are much better off assuming trade profits/losses are independent of prior outcomes."

**Worked illustrative case (a small-sample overfitting parable structurally similar to Pardo's Ch.13 parables)**: a 60-trade system shows apparent dependency at 95% confidence via the Runs Test; rules are adjusted to exploit it, leaving 40 "cleaned" trades with no further apparent dependency — this smaller, cleaner set has a *higher* optimal f (Vince's position-sizing parameter — see `10_position_sizing.md`) than the full 60. IF the dependency wasn't actually real, live performance will resemble the original 60-trade set, not the superior 40-trade set — meaning the chosen f is **too far to the right** of the true optimal (over-betting, the costlier error). Conversely, if dependency IS real and it's ignored, f will be **too far to the left** (under-betting) — a less costly error than over-betting. **Rule**: look for dependency; if it holds at high confidence across parameter values and markets, adjust system rules to exploit it. Otherwise assume independence (accepting the lesser potential penalty if wrong).

### 13.3 Degrees of Freedom as an Explicit Guiding Principle (Vince Ch.1)
Vince independently names "**don't restrict degrees of freedom**" as one of his two guiding principles for building/evaluating a trading system (alongside a geometric-mean/arithmetic-mean/variance efficiency criterion — see `10_position_sizing.md` and `11_risk_management.md` for the full formula). His framing is capital-management-first rather than statistical-testing-first: "the money you will make trading will be made by how effective the money management you employ is," not by how profitable the system looks in backtest, and excessive optimization is called the "woulda, shoulda, coulda" game — "completely counterproductive." **This is the same underlying concern as Pardo's degrees-of-freedom formula and ≥90%-remaining-DoF threshold (§3.1, §9.5) and Chan's ≤5-parameter rule of thumb (§4.2)**, arrived at from a money-management angle rather than a statistical-testing angle, and stated without a specific numeric threshold of Vince's own (see §14 below for the explicit cross-book agreement note).

### 13.4 A Footnote Caveat on Positive Expectation and Portfolios (Vince Ch.1)
The core axiom "in a negative expectation game, there is no money-management scheme that will make you a winner" (and the parallel claim for even-money/zero-expectation games) is stated as holding for **one market system traded alone**. Vince explicitly flags, without full treatment in Ch.1, that when trading **multiple** market systems together, it is possible for a portfolio that includes one negative-expectation market system to have a **higher net mathematical expectation** than the portfolio without it — relevant context for anyone tempted to reject an individually-unprofitable backtest result out of hand without considering its portfolio-level correlation properties (full treatment in Vince's later chapters — see `12_portfolio_construction.md`).

### 13.5 Bounding-Parameter Sensitivity as a Quantified Estimation-Error Caveat (Vince Ch.3-4)

Beyond the sample-size/dependency caveats above, Vince's parametric optimal-f machinery (`10_position_sizing.md` §1.7-1.11) surfaces a distinct, sharply-quantified estimation-error finding: **the choice of sigma-bounding range (how many standard deviations either side of the mean to truncate an otherwise-infinite outcome distribution to) is the single most consequential, and most arbitrary, decision in the entire parametric framework** — more consequential, in Vince's own worked numbers, than the choice of fitted-distribution shape itself.

**Worked magnitude (Ch.4, custom-adjustable-distribution fit on the 232-trade dataset, fitted parameters held constant throughout)**: pushing the upper sigma bound outward while holding the lower bound fixed at -3σ moves optimal f from **.206 to .999** (f$ from $23,783.17 down to $4,904.46) purely as a function of the bounding choice. Widening BOTH bounds symmetrically instead moves optimal f from **.206 down to .053** (f$ from $23,783.17 UP to $322,625.17) — the opposite direction, since a wider two-sided range pulls in a larger unrealized loss tail without a correspondingly larger realized gain tail. Neither table changes the underlying fitted distribution at all — same LOC/SCALE/SKEW/KURT throughout; the entire swing is attributable to the bounding-parameter choice alone. Full tables in `10_position_sizing.md` §1.8.

**Why this belongs alongside this file's other estimation-error caveats**: it is a small-sample/model-uncertainty problem in the same family as this section's dependency-testing and DoF caveats (§13.1-13.3) and Pardo's/Chan's/Kaufman's overfitting and sample-size treatments elsewhere in this file (§3-4, §9) — but unlike those, it applies even when the underlying distribution fit itself is good (high K-S significance), because it concerns the un-observable, unbounded tail beyond whatever data happened to be sampled. Vince's own stated defense is not statistical but structural: bound the worst case with an instrument that caps liability (e.g. long options, `10_position_sizing.md` §1.11) rather than trying to estimate the true tail more precisely. Vince's explicit warning, worth repeating verbatim here: **"you should be very careful what value you choose for this range bounding parameter. You'll have a lot riding on it."**

### 13.5a Binomial Win-Rate Confidence Test (Vince Appendix B)

A distinct, formula-based tool from Vince's distribution-catalog appendix for asking "how confident can I be that this backtest's observed win rate reflects a genuinely-profitable underlying process, rather than a lucky small sample?" — treating each trade's win/loss outcome as a Bernoulli trial and using the Binomial's Normal approximation to bound the TRUE win rate from below at a chosen confidence level.

**Formula (B.12) — lower confidence boundary for an observed win rate P at Z standard deviations, over N trials:**
```
L = P - Z*((P*(1-P))/(N-1))^.5
```
**Worked example (Vince Appendix B)**: a system has won 51% of 100 plays (P=.51, N=100); solving for the 3-sigma (Z=3, ≈99.865% confidence via the Normal CDF, Eq 3.21) worst-case win rate: `L = .51 - 3*((.51*.49)/99)^.5 = .51-.1507255181 = .3592744819` — i.e., "it would take a 3-sigma event for the population of plays... to have less than 35.92744819 percent winners," so one can be 99.865% confident the true win rate exceeds ≈35.93%.

**Three required assumptions for validity, explicit (Vince Appendix B)**: (1) the N trades are independent and randomly selected — "easily verified for any trading system"; (2) trades can be classified into two mutually exclusive groups (win/loss, or above/below median trade, etc.) — "easily satisfied"; (3) **the probability of a trade falling into either group is CONSTANT from one trade to the next** — Vince's own explicit caveat: "This is not necessarily true in trading, and the technique becomes inaccurate to the degree that this assumption is false. Be that as it may, the technique still can have value for traders." This third assumption is the same stationarity concern underlying §13.1-13.2's dependency-testing caveats above — Eq (B.12) is only as trustworthy as the independence/stationarity of the underlying trade stream.

**Worked "trials needed for confidence" inversion**: solving Eq (B.12) for N instead of L — a gambling/trading system winning 51% at 1:1 payoff (breakeven at 50%) requires **22,492 trials** witnessing that 51% win rate before one can be 99.865% (Z=3) confident the TRUE win rate genuinely exceeds 50% (i.e., that the edge is real, not noise). This is a concrete, quotable illustration of how large a sample a thin edge requires before it can be trusted — directly relevant to this file's small-sample/overfitting caveats (§3-4, §9, §13.1) from a different (win-rate-confidence, rather than dependency-testing or parameter-count) angle. **Additional stated applications (Vince Appendix B)**: assessing confidence in a market INDICATOR's forecast-accuracy claim (correct vs. incorrect forecasts as the two Bernoulli groups); determining how many backtest trials are needed to establish a given confidence level before committing capital.

### 13.6 Additional Dependency Tests and Empirical Non-Normality Confirmation (Vince Appendices A and C)

Beyond the Runs Test and serial correlation (§13.1 above), Vince's appendices give two further dependency tests and a worked empirical confirmation that trade P&L is generally NOT Normally distributed — all on-topic for this file's validation-methodology scope.

**Turning Points Test (Appendix C)** — detects dependency AND its direction. A trade is a "turning point" if its P&L is greater than both neighbors or lesser than both (using individual trade values, not cumulative equity; excludes the first/last trade).
```
Expected number of turning points = (2/3)*(N-2)                        (C.01)
Variance = (16*N - 29)/90                                              (C.02)
```
Z-score = (actual − expected)/√variance, converted to a confidence limit via the 2-tailed Normal (Eq 3.22, `10_position_sizing.md` §1.7). **Directional interpretation, explicit**: FEWER turning points than expected → "like begets like" (momentum-style dependency, a run continues); MORE turning points than expected → "like begets unlike" (mean-reversion-style dependency, a run tends to reverse).

**Phase Length Test (Appendix C)** — detects dependency but NOT its direction (unlike Turning Points). A "phase" = trades elapsed between a turning-point high and the next turning-point low (or vice versa).
```
E(D) = 2*(N-D-2)*(D^2+3*D+1) / (D+3)!                                   (C.03)
```
(Source ambiguity preserved per traceability rule: printed as "D^2*3*D+1"; `D^2+3*D+1` is the more likely intended reading, per `10_position_sizing.md` §1.6.) Phases are tabulated into length-1/length-2/length-"3+" categories and compared via chi-square — using **2.5 degrees of freedom** specifically (not the naive categories-minus-1=2), attributed to Kendall, Stuart & Ord, because phase lengths are not independent of each other.

**Fisher's Z Transformation (Appendix C)** — converts a sample correlation coefficient r into a confidence level, extending the serial-correlation test (§13.1):
```
F = .5 * ln((1+r)/(1-r))                                                (C.04)
V = 1/(N-3)                                                             (C.05)
Z = (.5*ln((1+r)/(1-r))) / (1/(N-3))^.5                                 (C.06)
```
Worked (r=.25, N=100): Z≈2.5155 → confidence limit in excess of 98.8%. **Explicit small-sample caveat**: if N≤30, use the Student's t distribution with N-1 degrees of freedom instead of the Normal approximation.

**Appendix A — empirical confirmation that trade P&L is not Normally distributed**: the chi-square goodness-of-fit test (`X^2 = Σ(Oi-Ei)^2/Ei`, Yates'-corrected for non-integer expected counts) applied to the same 232-trade dataset used throughout `10_position_sizing.md` §1.7-1.8, binned 10 ways from -2σ to +2σ against Normal-distribution expectations, gives **X²=37.5336 at 7 degrees of freedom (10 bins − 1 − 2 estimated parameters), significance level .000002419** — i.e., essentially certain that this dataset is NOT Normally distributed. This is the direct empirical confirmation of the "slop" concept discussed in §13.5 above and `10_position_sizing.md` §1.7-1.8: the Normal-distribution parametric technique's imprecision on this dataset isn't hypothetical, it's measured.

## 14. Where the Authors Agree — Convergent Testing Philosophy Across Independent Sources

The five books were written across a 15+ year span (Vince 1992, Pardo 2008 2nd ed., Chan 2009, Kaufman 2019 (this edition), Hilpisch 2020), by authors working in different corners of the industry (a money-management theorist, a systematic-strategy-evaluation consultant, a quant fund founder/professor, a veteran systems-testing author, and a Python/fintech educator), with essentially no cross-citation between them in this knowledge base's extracted material. The following convergences are therefore genuinely independent corroborations, not restatements of a shared source — each is attributed to its specific origin below.

1. **Plateaus over peaks — the single most repeated point of agreement.** Pardo's entire "shape of the optimization profile" framework (§6.3) — smooth hilltop vs. spiky peak, with the explicit neighbor-averaging worked example showing a lower-headline-number but well-supported parameter set outranking an isolated spike — is independently and forcefully restated by Kaufman as "the most robust performance is when the average of all tests of one strategy is better than the average of all tests of another strategy" (§6.5), including Kaufman's own asymmetric averaging rule (average for profit, *maximum* for drawdown) and his kurtosis->6 overfitting diagnostic. Both authors conclude a strategist should trade **multiple parameter sets simultaneously with equal capital allocation** drawn from a broad profitable region, rather than the single best-backtested set (Pardo Ch.10; Kaufman Ch.21).
2. **Both explicitly warn against re-running Walk-Forward/step-forward analysis iteratively during development.** Pardo's WFA is framed as a final "go/no-go" gate whose credibility depends on genuine, un-recycled out-of-sample data (Pardo Ch.11, Ch.13 §5 "Absence of a Walk-Forward Test"). Kaufman states this as an explicit, named risk — "Feedback" — where re-running the entire step-forward process after any rule tweak means "there is, strictly, no true out-of-sample data left, which violates our concept of scientific testing," with the fix being a single, never-touched final holdout block (Kaufman Ch.21, §4.3 above).
3. **Both mandate a minimum ~30-trade sample and give near-identical standard-error arithmetic.** Pardo's `Standard Error = StDev/SqRt(n)` and `Standard Error % = 1/SqRt(n)` worked tables (n=10→31.6% error, n=100→10.0%, n=1,000→3.0%, Pardo Ch.6/13) are structurally identical to Chan's `252 × parameters` sample-size rule of thumb (Chan Ch.3) and to Vince's Runs Test's own explicit 30-trade minimum for valid Normal-approximation confidence limits (Vince Ch.1) — three independently-stated numeric floors converging on the same underlying statistical-power concern.
4. **Both flag degrees of freedom / parameter count as a primary, quantifiable overfitting cause, independent of any performance number.** Pardo's `Rdf% = 100×[1−(Udf/Odf)]` formula and explicit ≥90%-remaining threshold (§3.1, §9.5) is the same underlying concern as Chan's blunt "do not employ more than 5 parameters" rule of thumb (Chan Ch.3, §4.2) and Vince's naming "don't restrict degrees of freedom" as one of his own two guiding principles for system evaluation (Vince Ch.1, §13.3) — three books, three different numeric framings, one shared conclusion: parameter count is a testable, front-loaded risk check that should be done *before* looking at any backtest performance number at all.
5. **Both independently converge on a sample-size-aware penalty as the ideal objective function**, without citing each other or a shared formula. Pardo's PROM formula explicitly shrinks its "pessimism penalty" as trade count n grows (via `sqrt(n)/n`), rewarding large, statistically reliable samples over small ones at identical dollar totals (Pardo Ch.9, §5.2). Kaufman's own objective-function survey independently names "returns adjusted for sample error" as a desirable (if formula-unspecified) criterion for exactly the same reason — more trades should count for more confidence (Kaufman Ch.21, §5.3).
6. **Transaction costs are treated by every book that models them explicitly as capable of reversing a strategy's sign, not merely shrinking its magnitude.** Pardo's worked 200-trades/1-year contrast (net profit flips to a net LOSS purely from $125/trade transaction cost, §2.3); Kaufman's $25-vs-$50/side cost-assumption contrast that "turned every one of the 10-40 day results into a net loss" at the higher (arguably still realistic) cost figure (§10.1); Chan's replication of the Khandani & Lo mean-reversion strategy collapsing from Sharpe 4.47 (no cost, original authors' figure) to Sharpe ≈−3.19 at just 5bps one-way cost (§11.7); and Hilpisch's repeated code-level demonstrations (gold momentum $7,395 outperformance → −$2,653 underperformance at 10bps; AAPL momentum +1267% → +281% gross-to-net; long-short mean reversion +73% gross → −151% net, a literal position of debt) (§12.1, §12.3) — four books, entirely independent datasets and methodologies, the same structural finding.
7. **Both single out survivorship bias and look-ahead bias as the two "silent" backtesting distortions most likely to produce a fictitiously profitable result without any obvious red flag in the code.** Chan's worked Example 3.3 (a strategy's true −42% return inverted to a fictitious +388% purely by excluding delisted stocks, §11.2) is the sharpest single illustration of survivorship bias in this knowledge base. Chan's own look-ahead-bias detection procedure (§4.5) is functionally identical in spirit to the `.shift(1)` discipline Hilpisch applies mechanically throughout every vectorized and ML backtest (§12.1, §12.2), and to the built-in-lookahead-protection Kaufman credits to modern development software ("prevents you from looking ahead at tomorrow's price" — Kaufman Ch.22).
8. **Both frame price shocks / rare events as a backtest's most fundamental, unfixable weakness — necessary anyway.** Pardo recommends evaluating results with and without rare non-repeatable moves and designing for survivability on the loss side (§2.3, §8.2 MDD/Mandelbrot discussion). Kaufman gives this the fullest treatment in the knowledge base: the explicit "backtests always add profit... from price shocks" mechanism, the 5×ATR operational shock definition, the 6-event historical decomposition, and the >50%-profitable-on-shock-days red flag (§10.3) — while still concluding, in near-identical spirit to Pardo's "the rest is up to the markets," that backtesting remains necessary "despite being weakest" in exactly this respect.
9. **Neither Pardo nor Kaufman fully reconciles its own internally inconsistent numeric rule of thumb — both flags are preserved rather than silently resolved in this file.** Pardo's walk-forward-window ratio (25–35% in Ch.11 vs. 1/8–1/3 in Ch.6, §7.3) and Kaufman's dual Theory-of-Runs-derived and worked-example-derived percentages (§ — see Kaufman Ch.22's Theory of Runs section for the embedded-graphic-formula gaps flagged verbatim in that chapter's own extraction) are each internally under-specified in ways their own authors do not resolve. This is noted here as a shared property of even the most rigorous testing-methodology writing in this knowledge base: precise numeric rules of thumb should be treated as directional guidance, not exact thresholds, even when stated by the domain's own most authoritative voices.

## 15. Practical Synthesis: A Consolidated Pre-Trade Checklist

This section distills the above into an operational checklist, purely as a cross-reference aid — every line item traces to a specific claim already documented above and is not a new synthesis beyond simple aggregation.

1. **Before backtesting**: define the expected profile (holding interval, expected win rate by strategy type — Kaufman's stated ~30–40% for trend systems vs. ~75–80% for mean-reversion/arbitrage, Ch.21) and the objective/success statistic *in advance* — testing should validate, not discover (Kaufman Ch.21).
2. **Data**: use the longest available history spanning multiple market types/regimes (Kaufman Ch.21; Pardo Ch.6 four-market-type framework); verify split/dividend adjustment via multiplier not subtraction (Chan Ch.3, full formulas and the IGE worked example in §11.9; Kaufman Ch.21); run basic integrity checks (outliers >4%, missing dates, OHLC consistency — Kaufman Ch.22); confirm survivorship-bias status and its likely direction of distortion (Chan Ch.3 Example 3.3).
3. **Parameter count and degrees of freedom**: compute `Rdf%` and keep ≥90% remaining where possible (Pardo Ch.6/13); independently sanity-check against Chan's ≤5-parameter rule of thumb (Chan Ch.3) and Kaufman's "four or fewer variables" empirical benchmark (Kaufman Ch.21); prefer non-uniform (percentage/geometric) parameter step sizes over uniform ones (Pardo Ch.10; Kaufman Ch.21, independently convergent).
4. **Sample size**: target ≥30 trades minimum (Pardo Ch.6; Vince Ch.1's Runs Test floor), ideally far more; check Chan's `252×parameters` data-point rule (Chan Ch.3).
5. **In-sample/out-of-sample split**: use a genuine, never-recycled final holdout (Kaufman's 60/20/20 or this project's 70/15/15 — §4.3); never re-run walk-forward iteratively after tweaks without reserving a fresh final block (Kaufman's "Feedback" warning, §4.3, §14.2); verify look-ahead-bias absence via Chan's truncation-comparison procedure (§4.5) or Hilpisch's mechanical `.shift(1)` discipline (§12.1–12.2).
6. **Objective function**: never rank on raw net profit alone (Pardo Ch.9; Kaufman Ch.21, independently convergent); prefer a sample-size-aware metric (PROM or Kaufman's "returns adjusted for sample error," §5.2–5.3) combined with floor/ceiling thresholds (min trades/year, max drawdown).
7. **Optimization search**: prefer plateaus to peaks (§6.3, §14.1); use neighbor-averaging or Kaufman's kurtosis check to detect a spurious spike (§6.3, §6.5); consider trading multiple parameter sets simultaneously rather than the single best (Pardo Ch.10; Kaufman Ch.21).
8. **Walk-Forward Analysis**: run a full multi-window WFA, ideally 10–20+ years/windows for statistical rigor via the Theory of Relevant Data (§7.6); compute Walk-Forward Efficiency and compare against the ~25% (unsound) / ~50–60%+ (robust) benchmarks (§7.5); treat this as the primary go/no-go gate (Pardo Ch.11; the book's own most emphasized claim).
9. **Costs**: apply realistic transaction costs before drawing any conclusion — every source that models this finds costs can reverse a strategy's sign, not just shrink it (§14.6); avoid both under- and over-estimating cost (Kaufman's explicit warning against unrealistically high assumed costs eliminating a genuinely viable fast system, §10.1).
10. **Price shocks / tail risk**: check whether backtest profit is disproportionately shock-day-driven (Kaufman's >50%-of-shock-trades-profitable red flag, §10.3); evaluate results with and without rare non-repeatable moves (Pardo Ch.6).
11. **Final performance evaluation**: compute MDD-based Required Capital, RAR, and RRR (§8.2–8.3); check consistency across the four profit-distribution-pattern cases (§8.4); compare against a genuine investment alternative, not against zero (Pardo Ch.12).
12. **Before live deployment**: paper-trade even after a successful out-of-sample test, since in practice the "reserved" data is rarely used only once during development (Kaufman Ch.22's explicit realism on this point); monitor real-time performance against the *historical* profile, treating both underperformance AND unusually good performance as signals warranting investigation (Pardo Ch.3/14).
